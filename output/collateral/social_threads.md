## Threads (limit 500/post)

**1 (lead, + image):**
Last fall I ran a small experiment: I handed seven different AI models the same assignment — draft a climate assessment in the format of an IPCC report — then fact-checked every chapter. The headline surprised me. The model that wrote the MOST (~160k words) got flagged with about 2.8x more factual errors than a shorter model (~141k words) that scored slightly higher. More words bought more mistakes, not more reliability. 🧵

**2 (link):**
Everything is open — prompts, scoring code, the rubric, the write-up. Re-run it, disagree with it, swap in your own evaluator:
github.com/fredzannarbor/ar7-climate-assessment

**3:**
Important caveats, stated loudly: this is NOT affiliated with the IPCC and NOT a proposal to replace it. The real assessment process is slow and expert-governed for good reason. No climate scientist checked this output, and the scores were assigned by another AI. Treat it as "how models behave on a hard task," not as fact.

**4:**
The finding that worried me more than length: unless I explicitly required citations, none of the models cited sources well. They produced fluent, official-sounding, well-structured prose that wasn't actually tied to anything checkable. That's the dangerous mode — not nonsense, but polished plausibility with no roots.

**5 (ask):**
If you work in AI-for-science or climate informatics, I'd genuinely value your critique — especially a human-expert spot-check of the generated text. Reply or share, and thanks.
