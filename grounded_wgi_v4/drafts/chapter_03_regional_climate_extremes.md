# Chapter 3: Regional Climate and Extreme Events

## Executive Summary

**Regional warming has departed decisively from the global mean, with the Arctic and the world's high-mountain regions emerging as the fastest-warming large domains of the planet (*high confidence*).** Observational syntheses place recent Arctic warming at close to four times the global rate since the late 1970s (Rantanen et al., 2022), while elevation-dependent warming is now documented across mountain systems on every inhabited continent (Pepin et al., 2022). These regional signals are not merely scaled-down versions of the global average; they reflect distinct physical mechanisms — sea-ice and snow-albedo feedbacks, changes in the vertical structure of the atmosphere, and land-surface modification — that concentrate change in particular places and seasons.

**The intensity and frequency of hot extremes have increased across most inhabited regions, and the human and ecological consequences of extreme heat are now measurable at the scale of individual cities and populations (*high confidence*).** Urban populations experience heat that is systematically amplified relative to surrounding rural areas, and this amplification is distributed unequally across and within cities (Hsu et al., 2021). Extreme weather and climate events already impose a substantial and growing burden on population health and on health systems (Ebi et al., 2021), a burden documented in successive annual assessments of health and climate change (Romanello et al., 2022; Romanello et al., 2023; Romanello et al., 2024).

**Short-duration rainfall extremes are intensifying at rates consistent with, and in places exceeding, thermodynamic expectations, raising flood hazard in exposed and vulnerable communities (*high confidence*).** The anthropogenic intensification of sub-daily rainfall is now an identifiable feature of the observational and modelling record (Fowler et al., 2021). Exposure to flooding is concentrated among the poor: a large fraction of the global population exposed to serious flood hazard lives in poverty (Rentschler et al., 2022), so that identical physical hazards translate into sharply divergent human outcomes.

**Drought is evolving in character as well as in frequency, with a documented global shift toward rapidly developing "flash" droughts and toward multi-year megadroughts in several regions (*high confidence*).** A transition toward faster drought onset under warming has been identified globally (Yuan et al., 2023), while the early-twenty-first-century drought of southwestern North America intensified into one of the most severe multi-decadal dry episodes in the long paleoclimate record (Williams et al., 2022). Groundwater, the buffer on which much of humanity relies during drought, is under simultaneous pressure from depletion and from a changing climate (Scanlon et al., 2023).

**Compound and cascading events — where hazards coincide, cluster, or trigger one another — are an increasingly important frame for regional risk, and existing single-hazard assessment methods systematically understate them (*medium confidence*).** Compound climate-fire-smoke sequences illustrate the point: the extreme forest fires of southeastern Australia were connected to climate change and variability (Abram et al., 2021), and wildfire smoke has been shown to damage respiratory health more than equivalent particulate matter from other sources (Aguilera et al., 2021). A framework treating climate risk as complex, interacting, and cascading is required to capture these dynamics (Simpson et al., 2021).

**Event attribution science has matured to the point where the contribution of human influence to many individual extremes can be quantified, and this capability now underwrites both scientific assessment and the economic valuation of climate damages (*high confidence*).** The same physical understanding that supports attribution also informs the estimation of the social cost of carbon dioxide, which comprehensive assessments place substantially higher than many regulatory values in current use (Rennert et al., 2022), and the exploration of low-probability, high-impact catastrophic outcomes that attribution alone cannot bound (Kemp et al., 2022).

**Regional monsoon systems, which govern the water security of a majority of the world's population, are responding to both greenhouse-gas forcing and aerosol forcing in ways that vary by basin and remain a leading source of regional projection uncertainty (*medium confidence*).** Coordinated multi-model experiments provide the backbone for regional projection (Tebaldi et al., 2021), yet the translation of global forcing into monsoon rainfall depends on regional processes whose representation still differs across models. Because monsoon variability propagates directly into agriculture, the emergence of climate impacts on food production in the latest generation of crop and climate models (Jägermeyr et al., 2021) is of first-order regional importance.

**Adaptation to regional climate change is occurring but is uneven in coverage and, in some documented cases, has increased rather than reduced vulnerability (*high confidence*).** Interventions intended to reduce climate vulnerability have in places redistributed or deepened it (Eriksen et al., 2021), underscoring that regional risk is produced jointly by physical hazard, exposure, and the social processes that govern vulnerability and response.

## 3.1 Observed Regional Climate Change

### 3.1.1 Regional warming has diverged strongly from the global mean, with the Arctic warming nearly four times faster than the planet as a whole since 1979 (*high confidence*).

The global-mean surface temperature is a useful index of the planet's energy imbalance, but it conceals the geography of change. Warming is neither spatially uniform nor seasonally uniform, and the largest regional departures from the global mean are now large enough to constitute distinct climatic phenomena in their own right. The clearest example is the Arctic. Analyses of the observational record indicate that the Arctic has warmed at close to four times the global rate over the period since 1979, a ratio substantially larger than the factor of two or three that had been the conventional figure in earlier assessments (Rantanen et al., 2022). The exact multiplier depends on the definition of the Arctic domain, the reference period, and the observational dataset, but across reasonable choices the amplification is robust and pronounced.

The physical basis of Arctic amplification is well established in outline even where the partitioning among mechanisms remains debated. The retreat of highly reflective sea ice exposes a darker ocean surface that absorbs more solar radiation, warms, and further inhibits ice formation — a positive feedback that operates most strongly in autumn and early winter as newly opened water releases stored heat to the atmosphere. Changes in the vertical structure of atmospheric warming, in cloud cover, in water vapour, and in the poleward transport of heat and moisture all contribute. The seasonality of the signal is diagnostic: Arctic warming is largest in the cold season, when the sea-ice and lower-tropospheric feedbacks are most active, and comparatively muted in summer when surface temperatures are pinned near the melting point of ice. The consequence is a regional climate that is being reorganised more rapidly than any other large terrestrial or oceanic domain, with implications that propagate through permafrost, ecosystems, and — through altered temperature gradients — potentially into the mid-latitude circulation.

### 3.1.2 Mountain regions are warming in an elevation-dependent manner, so that high-altitude environments and the water towers they sustain are changing faster than adjacent lowlands (*high confidence*).

A second domain of amplified change is the world's mountains. Synthesis of observations across mountain systems shows that warming rates frequently vary with elevation, and that in many ranges the higher elevations are warming faster than lower ones (Pepin et al., 2022). This elevation-dependent warming arises from a combination of mechanisms whose relative importance differs by range and season: the upward migration of the snow line and the associated snow-albedo feedback, changes in cloud cover and in the altitude of the freezing level, differences in the radiative effects of water vapour with height, and the influence of aerosols and land-surface change. The pattern is not universal — some ranges and some seasons show the opposite gradient — but the prevalence of amplified high-elevation warming is a robust feature of the observational record.

The stakes are high because mountains function as the water towers of the continents. Snow and ice accumulated at altitude release meltwater through the warm season, buffering downstream rivers and sustaining irrigation, hydropower, and municipal supply for populations that far exceed those living in the mountains themselves. Accelerated high-elevation warming shortens the snow season, shifts precipitation from snow to rain, advances the timing of melt, and drives the long-term loss of glacier mass. These changes alter the seasonal distribution of river flow even where annual totals are little changed, moving water away from the dry-season months when demand is greatest. The role of groundwater as a complementary buffer becomes correspondingly more important, and that buffer is itself under stress (Scanlon et al., 2023). Elevation-dependent warming thus links a high-altitude physical signal to lowland water security across large regions of Asia, the Americas, and Europe.

### 3.1.3 Urbanisation superimposes a further, locally intense warming signal, and exposure to urban heat is distributed inequitably across and within cities (*high confidence*).

Superimposed on regional-scale warming is the urban heat island, a local modification of climate produced by the replacement of vegetated surfaces with heat-retaining materials, by the geometry of the built environment, by anthropogenic heat release, and by the reduction of evaporative cooling. Analysis of major cities shows that the intensity of the urban heat island — and therefore the excess heat to which urban populations are exposed — is not shared equally. Across major cities, exposure to elevated urban heat-island intensity is disproportionately concentrated among particular populations, so that the burden of urban heat falls unevenly along lines that track other dimensions of disadvantage (Hsu et al., 2021). Because more than half of humanity now lives in cities, the urban heat island transforms a regional warming signal into a public-health hazard experienced at the scale of neighbourhoods and individuals.

The health dimension of this exposure is documented in the annual assessments of health and climate change, which track the growing exposure of vulnerable populations to extreme heat alongside a range of other climate-sensitive health indicators (Romanello et al., 2022; Romanello et al., 2023; Romanello et al., 2024). Extreme heat is among the most direct pathways by which climate change harms human health, and its effects are mediated by age, by pre-existing illness, by occupation, and by the local intensity of the heat island. Extreme weather and climate events more broadly impose measurable burdens on population health and on the health systems that must absorb surges in demand during and after events (Ebi et al., 2021). The regional and local structure of warming therefore cannot be separated from the human geography of vulnerability: the same degree of background warming produces very different outcomes depending on where and to whom the heat is delivered.

### 3.1.4 Regional changes are consistent with, and best interpreted through, the framework of a biosphere approaching multiple planetary limits (*medium confidence*).

The regional temperature signals described above do not occur in isolation from other large-scale environmental changes. They are one expression of a broader reorganisation of the Earth system that has been characterised through the lens of planetary boundaries — the biophysical limits within which humanity can operate safely. Recent assessment finds that the Earth system has now transgressed the majority of these boundaries, with climate change among them (Richardson et al., 2023). This framing situates regional climate change within a set of interacting pressures — on freshwater, on biogeochemical cycles, on land systems, and on the integrity of the biosphere — that reinforce one another. The condition of the biosphere in what has been termed the Anthropocene is one of tightly coupled human and natural systems in which regional climate change is both a driver and a consequence of wider change (Folke et al., 2021).

The practical relevance of this systemic view is that regional impacts rarely arrive through a single channel. A warming, drying region may simultaneously experience declining crop yields, stressed water supplies, intensifying wildfire, and rising heat-related mortality, each interacting with the others. Comprehensive reviews of the impacts of climate change document this multiplicity across sectors and regions and emphasise that adaptation and mitigation must be pursued together (Abbass et al., 2022). The remainder of this chapter therefore treats regional change not as a catalogue of independent hazards but as an interacting system, beginning with the extremes through which regional climate change is most acutely experienced.

## 3.2 Regional Extreme Events

### 3.2.1 Hot extremes have become more frequent, more intense, and longer-lasting across most land regions, and their health consequences are amplified in cities (*high confidence*).

Among all classes of extreme, changes in heat are the most directly attributable to warming and among the most consequential for human welfare. As the distribution of daily temperatures shifts toward warmer values, the frequency of days exceeding any fixed high threshold rises disproportionately, and events that would once have been rare become common. The physical logic is simple, but its human expression is shaped strongly by regional and local factors. In cities the background warming is compounded by the urban heat island, whose intensity varies from block to block and whose burden falls unevenly across populations (Hsu et al., 2021). Nighttime minimum temperatures, which govern the body's ability to recover from daytime heat, rise particularly fast in urban cores, extending the physiological stress of heat episodes.

The consequences for health are documented and growing. Extreme weather and climate events, of which heatwaves are the clearest example, impose measurable burdens on population health and on the capacity of health systems to respond (Ebi et al., 2021). Successive annual assessments record rising exposure of vulnerable groups — the elderly, the very young, those with chronic illness, and outdoor workers — to dangerous heat, alongside heat-related mortality, lost labour capacity, and pressure on health services (Romanello et al., 2022; Romanello et al., 2023; Romanello et al., 2024). These burdens are regionally structured: they are largest where high temperatures coincide with high humidity, dense and heat-retaining urban form, ageing populations, and limited access to cooling. The same absolute warming therefore produces very different mortality outcomes in different regions and different neighbourhoods, and any assessment of heat risk that stops at the physical hazard omits the social determinants that govern who is actually harmed.

Heat extremes also propagate beyond direct health effects. High temperatures desiccate vegetation and soils, priming landscapes for fire; they raise evaporative demand, deepening drought; and they stress energy and water systems at precisely the moments of peak demand. The tendency of heat to co-occur with, and to intensify, other hazards makes it a central node in the compound-event problem discussed below.

### 3.2.2 Heavy precipitation is intensifying, with the strongest and most hazardous changes concentrated in short-duration, sub-daily extremes (*high confidence*).

A warmer atmosphere holds more water vapour, and this thermodynamic constraint underlies a general expectation that heavy precipitation will intensify as the climate warms. The observational and modelling record bears this out, but with an important refinement: the intensification is not uniform across timescales. Short-duration rainfall extremes — the convective downpours that fall over minutes to hours — are intensifying particularly strongly, in places at rates that exceed the simple thermodynamic scaling expected from the increase in atmospheric moisture alone (Fowler et al., 2021). This super-scaling behaviour reflects dynamical feedbacks within intense convective storms, in which the latent heat released by heavier rainfall invigorates the updraught and concentrates precipitation further. The anthropogenic intensification of these short-duration extremes is now an identifiable feature of the record (Fowler et al., 2021), and it matters disproportionately for hazard because it is sub-daily bursts of rainfall that drive flash flooding, urban drainage failure, and debris flows.

The translation of intensifying rainfall into flood risk is mediated by exposure and vulnerability, and here the regional picture is stark. A large share of the world's population exposed to serious flood hazard lives in poverty, so that the communities least able to prepare for, absorb, and recover from floods are precisely those most exposed to them (Rentschler et al., 2022). The same rainfall extreme that causes minor disruption in a well-drained, well-insured city can be catastrophic in an informal settlement on a floodplain. Flood risk is therefore not simply a function of changing precipitation; it is co-produced by where people live, by the built and natural systems that convey and store water, and by the resources available for protection and recovery. Because the sharpest changes are in the short-duration extremes that overwhelm drainage most readily (Fowler et al., 2021), and because exposure is concentrated among the poor (Rentschler et al., 2022), the flood consequences of a warming climate are expected to fall with particular force on vulnerable populations.

Heavy-precipitation change also interacts with the cryospheric and mountain signals discussed earlier. As warming shifts high-elevation precipitation from snow to rain and advances melt, rain-on-snow events and rapid runoff become more likely, and the buffering capacity of the seasonal snowpack — and of groundwater (Scanlon et al., 2023) — is eroded. The regional flood regime is thus reshaped from several directions at once.

### 3.2.3 Drought is changing in character, with a documented global shift toward rapid-onset "flash" droughts and toward severe multi-year megadroughts in vulnerable regions (*high confidence*).

Drought is a more complex hazard than heat or heavy rain because it integrates precipitation deficits, evaporative demand, and the state of soils, vegetation, and water stores over time. Two changes in drought behaviour are especially well documented and especially consequential at the regional scale. The first is a global shift toward more rapidly developing droughts. Analysis of the observational record identifies a transition under climate change toward "flash" droughts — episodes that intensify over days to weeks rather than months — driven by the combination of precipitation deficits with the high evaporative demand of a warmer atmosphere (Yuan et al., 2023). Flash droughts are dangerous precisely because their speed of onset compresses the time available for detection and response, catching agriculture and water management unprepared.

The second is the emergence of severe, persistent multi-year droughts in several regions. The most thoroughly documented case is southwestern North America, where the drought that began in the early twenty-first century intensified into one of the most severe multi-decadal dry episodes in a paleoclimate record extending back many centuries, with anthropogenic warming making a substantial contribution to its severity (Williams et al., 2022). Such megadroughts differ qualitatively from ordinary drought: their duration exhausts the multi-year buffers — reservoirs, soil moisture, and especially groundwater — on which regions rely to survive shorter dry spells. Groundwater is central to this story. It is the resource of last resort during prolonged drought, yet it is under simultaneous pressure from over-extraction and from a changing climate that reduces recharge in many regions (Scanlon et al., 2023). Where megadrought and groundwater depletion coincide, the resilience of a region's water system can be eroded to the point of structural crisis.

Drought also feeds directly into the fire and heat hazards with which it is physically linked. Desiccated soils and vegetation lower evaporative cooling, raising temperatures and deepening the drought in a self-reinforcing loop, while providing the dry fuel that transforms ignition into large fire. This coupling is the basis of the compound events considered next.

### 3.2.4 Tropical cyclone hazard is being reshaped by warming through intensity, rainfall, and coastal impact, even where basin-wide frequency changes remain uncertain (*medium confidence*).

Tropical cyclones concentrate several of the most destructive hazards — extreme wind, storm surge, and torrential rain — into single events, and their behaviour under warming is an area of active assessment. The evidentiary basis for tracking cyclone behaviour rests on carefully constructed regional best-track databases, such as the long-term record of western North Pacific cyclones maintained by the China Meteorological Administration (Lu et al., 2021), which underpin analyses of trends in frequency, intensity, and track. Interpreting these records requires care because observing practices and instrumentation have changed over the decades they span, and disentangling genuine climate signals from changes in observation is a central methodological challenge.

The physical expectations under warming are clearest for those aspects governed by thermodynamics. A warmer ocean provides more energy and a warmer atmosphere holds more moisture, favouring higher peak intensities and heavier cyclone rainfall even if the total number of storms does not increase. Because a warmer atmosphere intensifies short-duration rainfall generally (Fowler et al., 2021), the freshwater flooding produced by slow-moving or intense cyclones is a growing component of their hazard, distinct from wind and surge. Rising sea level raises the baseline on which storm surge acts, so that a cyclone of unchanged intensity produces higher coastal water levels and reaches further inland than it would have in the past. The regional distribution of cyclone impact is therefore shifting even where basin-wide frequency trends remain uncertain, and the interaction of surge, rainfall, and antecedent conditions makes cyclones a leading source of compound coastal flooding.

### 3.2.5 Compound and cascading events, in which hazards coincide or trigger one another, are an increasingly important and under-assessed dimension of regional risk (*high confidence*).

The hazards treated separately above rarely occur in isolation. Heat, drought, and fire are physically coupled; heavy rain, storm surge, and river flooding can combine at coasts; and the failure of one system can cascade into others. Compound events — the coincidence, clustering, or sequencing of hazards, or the combination of hazards with vulnerable conditions — frequently produce impacts far larger than any single hazard would, yet conventional single-hazard risk assessment tends to miss them. A framework that treats climate risk as complex, interacting, and cascading is required to represent these dynamics adequately (Simpson et al., 2021).

The compound heat-drought-fire-smoke sequence is the paradigmatic example at the regional scale. Prolonged drought and extreme heat desiccate landscapes; ignition then produces fire whose extent and intensity are governed by the accumulated dryness. The extreme forest fires of southeastern Australia illustrate how climate change and variability connect to such events, with warming and drying loading the dice toward larger and more intense fires (Abram et al., 2021). The cascade does not end at the fire perimeter. Wildfire smoke transports fine particulate matter across large regions, and this smoke has been shown to harm respiratory health more than an equivalent mass of fine particles from other sources (Aguilera et al., 2021), so that a fire in one location degrades air quality and health across a much wider area. A single climatic season can thus link heat, drought, fire, air pollution, and health impacts in a chain that no single-hazard framework captures.

Compound events also arise at coasts, where cyclone rainfall, storm surge, and high river flow can coincide; in cities, where heat and power failure can combine to remove cooling at the moment it is most needed; and across sectors, where a climate shock to water or energy propagates into agriculture, health, and economic activity. The systemic and planetary-boundary framing introduced earlier (Richardson et al., 2023), set within the coupled human-natural biosphere (Folke et al., 2021), is the natural home for such analysis, because it treats the coupled human-natural system as the unit of risk. Assessing regional climate risk therefore requires moving beyond the enumeration of individual extremes toward the interactions among them (Simpson et al., 2021), a shift that also raises the scientific bar for the attribution methods considered in the next section.

## 3.3 Event Attribution Science

### 3.3.1 Event attribution has matured into a quantitative discipline capable of estimating the human influence on many individual extremes (*high confidence*).

The scientific question that the public most often asks after a disaster — was this event caused by climate change? — is answerable only in a probabilistic sense, and the discipline that has grown up to answer it is extreme-event attribution. Its central logic is comparative: the likelihood or intensity of an observed event is estimated in the climate as it is, and compared with its likelihood or intensity in a counterfactual climate without human influence. The difference between the two is expressed as a change in probability or in magnitude attributable to anthropogenic forcing. This framing sidesteps the false dichotomy of single causation and replaces it with a rigorous statement about how human influence has shifted the odds.

The maturation of attribution rests on several developments that recur throughout this chapter. The first is the physical understanding of how warming changes each class of hazard: the thermodynamic intensification of heat and of heavy rainfall, the coupling of drought to evaporative demand, and the reshaping of cyclone intensity and rainfall. Where the physical mechanism is well understood and strongly expressed — as for heat extremes and for short-duration rainfall (Fowler et al., 2021) — attribution statements can be made with high confidence. The second is the availability of long, carefully constructed observational records against which to characterise the rarity of events and to detect trends; the regional cyclone databases exemplify the kind of curated record on which attribution depends (Lu et al., 2021). The third is the ensemble of climate-model simulations that allow the counterfactual climate to be constructed and sampled.

Attribution is now applied across the full range of extremes considered in this chapter. For heat, the shift of the temperature distribution makes attribution comparatively straightforward and the resulting statements robust. For heavy precipitation, the identification of anthropogenic intensification in the sub-daily record provides a physical foundation for event-level attribution (Fowler et al., 2021). For drought, attribution must account for both precipitation and evaporative demand, and the documented human contribution to the severity of the southwestern North American megadrought is a landmark result (Williams et al., 2022). The global shift toward flash droughts likewise represents an attributable change in the character of the hazard rather than merely in its frequency (Yuan et al., 2023). For fire, attribution connects the climatic drivers of fire weather to observed extreme events, as in the case of the southeastern Australian fires (Abram et al., 2021), while recognising that ignition, fuel management, and land use also govern outcomes.

### 3.3.2 The credibility of attribution depends on the fidelity of climate models and on the coordinated experimental frameworks that structure their use (*high confidence*).

Attribution is only as reliable as the models used to construct the counterfactual climate and to simulate the statistics of extremes. The coordinated multi-model experiments that underpin regional climate projection also underpin attribution, by providing large ensembles run under carefully specified forcings. The scenario-based experiments that span the range of plausible futures are the backbone of this enterprise (Tebaldi et al., 2021), and the same modelling infrastructure that projects future change enables the simulation of the factual and counterfactual present. Confidence in an attribution statement rests on the ability of models to reproduce the observed statistics of the relevant extreme, and this fidelity varies by hazard: models represent large-scale heat extremes well, but the sub-daily convective processes that drive the most intense short-duration rainfall are only partially resolved at typical model resolutions (Fowler et al., 2021), which is one reason those extremes are assessed with a mixture of observational and modelling evidence.

The field has increasingly drawn on advances in the modelling of weather itself. Machine-learning weather prediction has demonstrated skill in medium-range global forecasting that rivals or exceeds traditional physics-based systems (Bi et al., 2023), and data-driven approaches have shown skill in precipitation nowcasting, the very short-range prediction of rainfall that is most relevant to flash-flood hazard (Ravuri et al., 2021). While these tools were developed for forecasting rather than attribution, they expand the toolkit available for characterising extremes and for generating the large ensembles on which robust statistical statements depend. Their emergence illustrates a broader trend in which computational advances lower the cost of the large-ensemble experiments that attribution requires, potentially allowing rapid, operational attribution of events as they occur.

### 3.3.3 Attribution science provides the physical basis for valuing climate damages and for framing low-probability, high-impact risks (*high confidence*).

Beyond its scientific interest, attribution has become foundational to the economics and governance of climate change. The estimation of the social cost of carbon dioxide — the monetised damage caused by an additional tonne of emissions — depends on translating physical climate change into impacts, and comprehensive assessment now places this cost substantially higher than many values embedded in current regulation (Rennert et al., 2022). The chain from emissions to cost runs through exactly the regional extremes considered in this chapter: heat mortality, flood damage, drought-driven agricultural loss, and cyclone destruction. Improved attribution of these impacts to warming sharpens the damage functions on which the social cost rests, and the direction of recent revision has been toward higher costs as the evidence for severe and compounding regional impacts has accumulated.

Attribution also clarifies the limits of what can be quantified, and thereby motivates explicit attention to catastrophic tail risks. Much of the assessed distribution of climate outcomes is amenable to attribution and projection, but the low-probability, high-impact tail — the possibility of abrupt or extreme change with civilisation-scale consequences — is precisely the region that standard methods struggle to bound. The systematic exploration of catastrophic climate-change scenarios has been advanced as a necessary complement to the assessment of central outcomes (Kemp et al., 2022), on the grounds that the events of greatest concern for risk management are those whose probability is hardest to estimate. This concern connects to the planetary-boundaries framing introduced earlier: the transgression of multiple boundaries (Richardson et al., 2023) raises the possibility of interacting, nonlinear responses that would fall outside the range that attribution of past events can constrain. Attribution science, by delineating what is and is not currently quantifiable, thus performs a dual service — grounding the valuation of expected damages while flagging the tail risks that expected-value calculations omit.

### 3.3.4 The regional and equity dimensions of extremes are integral to attribution's practical meaning (*high confidence*).

An attribution statement about the changed odds of a physical event acquires its human significance only when combined with the geography of exposure and vulnerability. The same attributed intensification of flooding carries very different meaning in a wealthy, well-defended city and in a poor community on an exposed floodplain, and the concentration of flood exposure among the poor (Rentschler et al., 2022) means that the human costs of attributable changes in rainfall extremes (Fowler et al., 2021) fall disproportionately on those least responsible for and least able to withstand them. Attribution of heat extremes similarly gains meaning through the urban and social structure of exposure (Hsu et al., 2021) and through the documented health burdens of extreme weather (Ebi et al., 2021) and the annual health assessments (Romanello et al., 2023).

This equity dimension has consequences for how attribution is used. Attribution increasingly informs questions of loss, damage, and responsibility, because it makes possible statements about the human contribution to specific harmful events. The scientific caution appropriate to such statements — acknowledging the dependence on model fidelity, on record length, and on the class of hazard — must therefore be paired with clarity about the uneven distribution of the harms being attributed. Attribution that ignores this distribution risks describing a physically changed hazard while missing the socially produced disaster. The comprehensive-review literature on climate impacts and adaptation makes this coupling explicit (Abbass et al., 2022), and the framework of complex, cascading risk (Simpson et al., 2021) provides the structure within which attributed physical changes and their unequal human consequences can be assessed together. The next section applies this integrated view to the monsoon systems on which a large share of humanity depends.

## 3.4 Regional Monsoons, Projections, and Adaptation

### 3.4.1 The regional monsoon systems that govern water security for most of humanity are responding to competing greenhouse-gas and aerosol forcings, and their projection remains a leading source of regional uncertainty (*medium confidence*).

The monsoon systems of Asia, Africa, Australia, and the Americas organise the annual cycle of rainfall for a majority of the world's population. Their seasonal reversal of winds and the associated wet season deliver the water on which agriculture, hydropower, and daily life depend across vast regions, and even modest changes in monsoon timing, total, or variability propagate immediately into human welfare. Monsoons are shaped by the land-sea thermal contrast, by the distribution of atmospheric moisture, and by the large-scale circulation, and each of these is affected by anthropogenic forcing in ways that do not all point in the same direction. Greenhouse-gas forcing tends, through the thermodynamic increase in atmospheric moisture, to strengthen monsoon rainfall; aerosol forcing, by cooling the surface and altering the land-sea contrast and atmospheric stability, has historically tended to weaken several monsoon systems. The regional outcome depends on the balance between these forcings, which differs by basin and evolves over time as aerosol emissions change.

This competition of forcings is one reason monsoon projection remains among the most uncertain elements of regional climate assessment. The coordinated scenario experiments that structure projection (Tebaldi et al., 2021) span the range of plausible greenhouse-gas and, in some designs, aerosol futures, but the translation of a given global forcing into monsoon rainfall depends on regional processes — convection, land-atmosphere coupling, the representation of aerosol-cloud interactions — whose simulation still differs across models. The result is that models often agree on the direction of thermodynamic strengthening while disagreeing on the dynamical and aerosol-mediated components, leaving substantial spread in projected monsoon change at the regional scale. Sub-daily convective extremes within the monsoon, which drive much of its flood hazard, are subject to the same resolution limitations that affect short-duration rainfall generally (Fowler et al., 2021), so that changes in monsoon intensity at the timescales most relevant to flooding are assessed with particular caution.

The stakes of this uncertainty are amplified by the sensitivity of the systems that depend on the monsoon. Agriculture is the most immediate: the emergence of climate impacts on global crop production in the latest generation of coupled climate and crop models (Jägermeyr et al., 2021) shows that yield changes are appearing earlier and more clearly than previously assessed, and monsoon regions are central to this signal because their food systems are so tightly coupled to seasonal rainfall. Water resources are equally exposed. Where monsoon rainfall feeds the rivers and recharges the aquifers on which dry-season supply depends, changes in monsoon total or variability interact with the groundwater stresses documented globally (Scanlon et al., 2023) and with the mountain-derived flows discussed earlier (Pepin et al., 2022). A weakened or more erratic monsoon, superimposed on depleted groundwater and diminished snowpack, would compound water insecurity across some of the most populous regions on Earth.

### 3.4.2 Regional projections rest on coordinated multi-model experiments, whose spread must be interpreted alongside model fidelity and the representation of regional processes (*high confidence*).

The scientific basis for statements about future regional climate is the ensemble of projections produced under coordinated experimental protocols. The scenario-based experiments that span a range of socioeconomic and emissions futures provide the structured set of projections from which regional change is assessed (Tebaldi et al., 2021). Their value lies in comparability: because participating models run the same scenarios, the spread among their projections can be interpreted as a measure of structural uncertainty, and the response to different scenarios can be isolated from the response to model differences. For many regional variables — mean warming, the broad intensification of heat extremes — the models agree well and confidence is correspondingly high. For others — regional precipitation, monsoon change, the details of circulation — the spread is larger and reflects genuine uncertainty in how regional processes respond.

Interpreting projection spread requires attention to model fidelity, which varies by variable and by region. Large-scale thermodynamic responses are robust across models; smaller-scale dynamical and convective responses are less so. The advances in data-driven weather modelling noted earlier (Bi et al., 2023), and in precipitation nowcasting (Ravuri et al., 2021), point toward a future in which higher-resolution and hybrid approaches may narrow some of this spread, particularly for the convective extremes that current models resolve poorly (Fowler et al., 2021). In the meantime, the honest communication of regional projection uncertainty — distinguishing what is robust from what is not — is essential, because regional adaptation decisions must be made under precisely the uncertainty that the projections quantify. The framework of complex climate risk (Simpson et al., 2021) offers a structure for making decisions under this uncertainty by focusing on the interactions among hazards, exposures, and vulnerabilities rather than on any single projected variable.

### 3.4.3 Regional impacts propagate through tightly coupled human and natural systems, so that physical change is transmitted into food, water, health, and economic outcomes (*high confidence*).

The regional changes assessed in this chapter do not remain confined to the physical climate system. They propagate through the coupled human-natural systems of the biosphere (Folke et al., 2021) into the sectors on which human welfare depends. The pathway into food is direct and increasingly well quantified: climate impacts on crop yields are emerging earlier than earlier assessments projected (Jägermeyr et al., 2021), and in monsoon-dependent regions the coupling of yields to seasonal rainfall makes agriculture an immediate transmitter of climate variability and change. The pathway into water runs through the mountain snowpack (Pepin et al., 2022), the groundwater buffer (Scanlon et al., 2023), and the monsoon, whose combined stresses shape regional water security. The pathway into health runs through extreme heat, floods, drought, and wildfire smoke, whose burdens on population health and health systems are documented and growing (Ebi et al., 2021; Aguilera et al., 2021) and are tracked in the annual Lancet Countdown (Romanello et al., 2024).

The economic pathway integrates these physical and sectoral impacts into monetary terms, and its assessment has moved toward higher estimates of climate damage. The social cost of carbon dioxide, which aggregates the marginal damage of emissions across sectors and regions, is now assessed as substantially higher than many values in regulatory use (Rennert et al., 2022), reflecting the accumulating evidence for severe regional impacts. Energy systems are simultaneously exposed to and part of the response to climate change: the cost, environmental impact, and resilience of renewable energy under a changing climate are themselves climate-sensitive (Osman et al., 2022), so that the infrastructure of mitigation must be designed for the altered regional climate it is intended to help stabilise. These transmission pathways, taken together, mean that a regional physical change of given magnitude can produce impacts of widely varying severity depending on the exposure and adaptive capacity of the systems it encounters — the theme to which the final subsection turns.

### 3.4.4 Adaptation to regional climate change is under way but uneven, and can reduce or, if poorly designed, deepen vulnerability (*high confidence*).

Regional climate risk is produced jointly by physical hazard, by exposure, and by vulnerability, and the last of these is the most amenable to deliberate action. Adaptation — the adjustment of human and natural systems to actual or expected climate and its effects — is now occurring across regions and sectors, but its coverage is uneven and its effectiveness is not guaranteed. Assessment of adaptation interventions in developing countries finds that their effect on vulnerability is mixed: some reduce it, some have little effect, and some redistribute or even deepen vulnerability, for example by benefiting some groups at the expense of others or by entrenching the conditions that produced vulnerability in the first place (Eriksen et al., 2021). This finding is a caution against treating adaptation as an unambiguous good; the design, governance, and equity of interventions determine whether they achieve their intended reduction in risk.

The equity structure of regional risk makes this caution especially important. Flood exposure is concentrated among the poor (Rentschler et al., 2022); urban heat falls disproportionately on disadvantaged populations (Hsu et al., 2021); and the health burdens of extremes are borne most heavily by the vulnerable (Ebi et al., 2021) and are documented in the annual health assessments (Romanello et al., 2024). Adaptation that does not attend to this structure risks protecting those already best defended while leaving the most exposed no safer, or worse. The comprehensive assessment of impacts, adaptation, and mitigation emphasises that the three must be pursued together and with attention to their distributional consequences (Abbass et al., 2022), and the framework of complex, cascading risk (Simpson et al., 2021) provides the means to assess whether a given intervention reduces risk across the interacting system or merely displaces it.

Set against the possibility of well-designed adaptation is the shadow of the catastrophic tail. The systematic exploration of extreme scenarios (Kemp et al., 2022) and the transgression of multiple planetary boundaries (Richardson et al., 2023) are reminders that adaptation has limits, and that beyond some magnitude of change the coupled human-natural systems on which regions depend (Folke et al., 2021) may be pushed past thresholds that no feasible adaptation can accommodate. The assessment of regional climate and extreme events therefore concludes on two linked findings. First, the physical evidence for regional change — amplified warming in the Arctic (Rantanen et al., 2022) and mountains (Pepin et al., 2022), intensifying heat and short-duration rainfall (Fowler et al., 2021), shifting drought (Yuan et al., 2023) and megadrought (Williams et al., 2022), and compounding hazards (Abram et al., 2021; Simpson et al., 2021) — is strong and, through attribution, increasingly linked to human influence. Second, the human consequences of this change are governed as much by exposure, vulnerability, and the quality of adaptation and mitigation as by the physical hazard itself, so that the task of regional climate assessment is inseparable from the assessment of the human systems through which climate is experienced.

## 3.5 Regional Syntheses and Cross-Sectoral Transmission

### 3.5.1 The Arctic is being reorganised across its physical, ecological, and human systems at a pace unmatched elsewhere on the planet (*high confidence*).

The near-fourfold amplification of Arctic warming relative to the global mean (Rantanen et al., 2022) is not an isolated statistic but the leading edge of a systemic reorganisation. The retreat of sea ice removes a reflective surface, alters the seasonal exchange of heat and moisture between ocean and atmosphere, and lengthens the open-water season, with consequences that cascade through the marine ecosystem and into the atmospheric circulation. On land, warming thaws permafrost, destabilising the ground on which infrastructure rests and mobilising stores of previously frozen organic carbon. The seasonality of the change compounds its impact: because Arctic amplification is concentrated in the cold season, the winter climate that has historically defined the region is changing fastest, shortening the period of stable sea ice and frozen ground on which both ecosystems and communities depend.

The implications reach far beyond the Arctic itself. Changes in the temperature contrast between the warming Arctic and the lower latitudes have been proposed as a mechanism by which Arctic change could influence the mid-latitude circulation, potentially altering the frequency or persistence of weather patterns that produce heat, cold, and precipitation extremes at lower latitudes. The magnitude and even the sign of such teleconnections remain contested, and this uncertainty is one of the more consequential open questions in regional climate science, because it links the most rapidly changing region to the extremes experienced by the majority of the world's population. What is not contested is that the Arctic has become a bellwether: the region where the coupled response of the climate system to forcing is expressed most rapidly and most completely, and where the interaction of physical change with ecosystems, carbon, and human communities can be observed in advance of its appearance elsewhere. The condition of the Arctic is, in this sense, an early reading of the trajectory of the wider biosphere (Folke et al., 2021) and of the planetary boundaries the Earth system has begun to transgress (Richardson et al., 2023).

### 3.5.2 The world's mountains transmit high-elevation warming into lowland water insecurity, coupling a cryospheric signal to the livelihoods of billions (*high confidence*).

Elevation-dependent warming (Pepin et al., 2022) acquires its human importance through the hydrological role of mountains. The snow and ice stored at altitude constitute a natural reservoir that captures winter precipitation and releases it as meltwater through the warm, dry months, sustaining the rivers of Asia, the Americas, and Europe precisely when rainfall is scarce and demand is high. Accelerated high-elevation warming attacks this reservoir from several directions at once: it raises the elevation at which precipitation falls as rain rather than snow, reducing the volume of the seasonal snowpack; it advances the timing of melt, shifting river flow earlier in the year and away from the dry-season months of greatest need; and it drives the long-term retreat of glaciers, eroding the multi-year buffer that stabilises flow in the driest years.

The downstream consequence is a growing reliance on the groundwater that complements surface water in sustaining dry-season supply, and this buffer is itself under pressure from over-extraction and from a changing climate that alters recharge (Scanlon et al., 2023). Where the diminishing snowpack, retreating glaciers, and stressed aquifers of a mountain-fed basin coincide, the resilience of the water system can be eroded even where annual precipitation totals are little changed, because it is the seasonal and interannual buffering, not the annual mean, that governs security of supply. The interaction of these mountain and groundwater signals with the monsoon rainfall discussed below means that some of the most populous river basins on Earth face simultaneous pressure on every component of their water balance. This is a paradigmatic case of the coupled, cross-system transmission through which regional physical change becomes human risk.

### 3.5.3 Dryland and Mediterranean-type regions are experiencing a self-reinforcing convergence of heat, drought, and fire (*high confidence*).

A distinct regional syndrome is emerging in the world's dryland and Mediterranean-type climates, where the physical coupling of heat, drought, and fire produces a self-reinforcing spiral. Warming raises evaporative demand, which deepens drought by drawing moisture from soils and vegetation; the resulting desiccation lowers evaporative cooling, which raises temperatures further; and the accumulated dryness converts ignition into extensive, intense fire. The southwestern North American megadrought exemplifies the drought pole of this syndrome, having intensified into one of the most severe multi-decadal dry episodes in a centuries-long paleoclimate record, with a substantial anthropogenic contribution (Williams et al., 2022). The global shift toward flash droughts (Yuan et al., 2023) adds a temporal dimension, compressing the onset of dryness and reducing the warning available to water and agricultural managers.

The fire pole of the syndrome is illustrated by the extreme forest fires of southeastern Australia, whose extent and intensity were connected to climate change and variability (Abram et al., 2021). The consequences of such fires extend far beyond the burned area: wildfire smoke transports fine particulate matter across entire regions and has been shown to harm respiratory health more than an equivalent mass of particulate matter from other sources (Aguilera et al., 2021), so that a fire in one location becomes a public-health event across a much larger domain. The convergence of these hazards in dryland regions is a leading example of the compound and cascading dynamics that single-hazard assessment misses and that a framework of complex, interacting risk is designed to capture (Simpson et al., 2021). It is also a domain in which the equity dimension is acute, because the health burdens of heat and smoke fall most heavily on the vulnerable (Ebi et al., 2021) and are tracked in the annual assessments of health and climate change (Romanello et al., 2023).

### 3.5.4 Densely populated coastal and low-lying regions face compounding flood hazard from rainfall, rivers, surge, and rising seas (*high confidence*).

Coastal and low-lying regions concentrate population, economic activity, and exposure, and they are subject to a distinctive compounding of flood hazards. Intensifying short-duration rainfall (Fowler et al., 2021) raises the risk of pluvial and flash flooding within cities; river flooding responds to rainfall across upstream catchments; storm surge is driven by cyclones and other storms; and rising sea level lifts the baseline on which all coastal water levels act. When these hazards coincide — a cyclone delivering extreme rainfall while its surge meets a high river stage on a raised sea level — the resulting compound flood can far exceed what any single driver would produce, precisely the dynamic that a complex-risk framework is intended to represent (Simpson et al., 2021). The regional cyclone records that document the storms driving surge and rainfall, such as the western North Pacific database (Lu et al., 2021), are essential to characterising this hazard, even as the changing intensity and rainfall of cyclones under warming reshape it.

The human consequences of coastal flood hazard are governed by exposure and vulnerability as much as by the physical drivers. A large share of the global population exposed to serious flood hazard lives in poverty (Rentschler et al., 2022), and this concentration is often most acute in the informal settlements of rapidly growing coastal cities, where drainage, defence, and recovery resources are least available. The same compound flood therefore produces radically different outcomes across communities separated by only a few kilometres, according to their exposure and their capacity to prepare and recover. Coastal regions thus combine the highest concentration of compounding physical hazard with some of the sharpest gradients of vulnerability, making them a priority for the integrated assessment of physical change, exposure, and adaptive capacity.

### 3.5.5 Regional climate change is transmitted into food, water, health, energy, and disease through tightly coupled pathways, none of which can be assessed in isolation (*high confidence*).

The regional physical changes catalogued in this chapter reach human welfare through a set of transmission pathways that are themselves coupled. The food pathway is increasingly well quantified: climate impacts on global crop production emerge earlier in the latest generation of coupled climate and crop models than in previous assessments (Jägermeyr et al., 2021), and in monsoon- and mountain-fed agricultural regions the coupling of yields to seasonal water availability makes food production an immediate transmitter of climatic variability. The water pathway runs through the mountain snowpack (Pepin et al., 2022), the groundwater buffer (Scanlon et al., 2023), and monsoon rainfall, whose combined and often simultaneous stresses shape regional water security. The health pathway operates through extreme heat, floods, drought, and wildfire smoke, whose burdens on population health and health systems are documented and growing (Ebi et al., 2021) and are tracked in successive annual assessments (Romanello et al., 2024).

Two further pathways deserve emphasis. The first is disease: a changing climate alters the distribution, seasonality, and transmission of infectious disease, and the broader era of global environmental change is reshaping the landscape of infectious risk (Baker et al., 2021), so that regional warming and altered precipitation can shift the geography of vector-borne and water-borne disease. The second is energy, which is both affected by and central to the response to climate change; the cost, environmental impact, and resilience of renewable energy systems are themselves sensitive to the changing climate in which they must operate (Osman et al., 2022), meaning that the infrastructure on which mitigation depends must be designed for the regional climate it is intended to help stabilise. Integrating across these pathways is the aggregate economic signal captured by the social cost of carbon dioxide, now assessed as substantially higher than many regulatory values in use (Rennert et al., 2022) as evidence for severe, compounding regional impacts has accumulated. Comprehensive review of climate impacts, adaptation, and mitigation confirms that these pathways cannot be addressed independently (Abbass et al., 2022): an intervention in one sector reverberates through the others, and the coupled human-natural system (Folke et al., 2021) is the appropriate unit of assessment.

## 3.6 Uncertainties, Observational Needs, and Assessment Frontiers

### 3.6.1 The largest regional uncertainties lie in the dynamical and convective processes that govern precipitation, monsoons, and sub-daily extremes (*high confidence*).

The confidence attached to regional projections varies systematically with the physics involved. Thermodynamic responses — the intensification of heat and, at the daily-and-longer scale, of heavy precipitation — are robust across models and assessed with high confidence. Dynamical responses — changes in circulation, storm tracks, monsoon behaviour, and the sub-daily convective extremes that drive flash flooding — are less well constrained, because they depend on processes that current models resolve only partially. The strongest and most hazardous short-duration rainfall extremes arise from convection at scales below the typical resolution of climate models, which is one reason their anthropogenic intensification is assessed through a combination of observational and modelling evidence rather than from models alone (Fowler et al., 2021). Monsoon projection inherits this difficulty, because monsoon rainfall depends on convection, land-atmosphere coupling, and aerosol-cloud interaction, each imperfectly represented, and because the competing thermodynamic and aerosol influences on the monsoon can offset one another to leave a small residual with large fractional uncertainty.

Reducing these uncertainties is a central frontier of regional assessment. Higher-resolution modelling, which begins to resolve convection explicitly, is one avenue; the coordinated experimental frameworks that structure projection (Tebaldi et al., 2021) provide the comparative basis for evaluating progress. The emergence of skilful data-driven weather prediction (Bi et al., 2023) and of data-driven precipitation nowcasting (Ravuri et al., 2021) points toward computational tools that may eventually allow larger ensembles and finer resolution at reduced cost, potentially sharpening the statistics of the convective extremes that current physics-based models resolve poorly. Until such advances mature, the honest communication of regional projection uncertainty — clearly distinguishing the robust thermodynamic signals from the less certain dynamical ones — is itself a scientific obligation, because regional adaptation decisions must be taken under exactly the uncertainty that the assessment quantifies.

### 3.6.2 Observational records of adequate length, quality, and homogeneity are the foundation on which all regional assessment and attribution rest (*high confidence*).

Every quantitative statement in this chapter depends ultimately on observations, and the quality, length, and homogeneity of the observational record is therefore a first-order determinant of confidence. Detecting a trend in an extreme requires a record long enough to characterise the rarity of the event against the background of natural variability; attributing that trend to human influence requires, in addition, that changes in observing practice not be mistaken for changes in climate. The regional cyclone databases illustrate both the value and the difficulty: a long, carefully constructed record such as the western North Pacific database (Lu et al., 2021) makes trend analysis possible, but the evolution of observing technology over the decades such records span must be accounted for before a genuine climate signal can be isolated. Similar considerations apply to the records of heat, precipitation, and drought on which the assessment of those extremes depends.

The observational challenge is most acute precisely where the human stakes are highest. Many of the regions most exposed and most vulnerable — including parts of the tropics, the drylands, and rapidly urbanising areas — have the sparsest and least homogeneous long-term records, so that confidence in regional change is often weakest where it matters most. The urban dimension adds a further layer, because characterising the heat to which urban populations are actually exposed requires observations at the scale of neighbourhoods, not the coarse grids of global datasets; the finding that urban heat-island intensity is distributed inequitably across major cities (Hsu et al., 2021) rests on such fine-scale analysis. Sustaining and extending high-quality observational networks, and constructing the carefully homogenised long-term records that attribution requires, is therefore not a supporting activity but a precondition for regional climate assessment, and gaps in the observing system translate directly into gaps in what can be assessed and defended.

### 3.6.3 The frontier of regional assessment is the integration of physical change with exposure, vulnerability, and the assessment of catastrophic tail risk (*high confidence*).

The trajectory of regional climate assessment is toward integration. The physical assessment of hazard — warming, heat, precipitation, drought, fire, cyclones — is necessary but not sufficient to characterise risk, because the human and ecological consequences of a given hazard depend on who and what is exposed and on their vulnerability and capacity to respond. The concentration of flood exposure among the poor (Rentschler et al., 2022), the inequitable distribution of urban heat (Hsu et al., 2021), and the finding that adaptation interventions can deepen as well as reduce vulnerability (Eriksen et al., 2021) all demonstrate that the human outcome is co-produced by physical and social processes. A framework that treats climate risk as complex, interacting, and cascading (Simpson et al., 2021) provides the structure for this integration, allowing physical hazard, exposure, and vulnerability to be assessed together rather than in sequence.

Integration must also extend to the catastrophic tail that expected-value assessment omits. Much of the distribution of regional outcomes is amenable to projection and, for past events, to attribution; but the low-probability, high-impact tail — the possibility of abrupt or extreme change with consequences at the scale of societies — is precisely the region that standard methods struggle to bound. The systematic exploration of catastrophic scenarios has been advanced as a necessary complement to the assessment of central outcomes (Kemp et al., 2022), and the transgression of multiple planetary boundaries (Richardson et al., 2023) raises the prospect of interacting, nonlinear responses that lie outside the range constrained by the attribution of past events. Regional assessment therefore faces a dual task: to quantify, with appropriate and clearly communicated confidence, the expected regional changes and their unevenly distributed human consequences; and to flag, without overstating what can be quantified, the tail risks whose management may ultimately matter most. Meeting both is the frontier of the assessment enterprise, and it is inseparable from the assessment of the coupled human-natural systems (Folke et al., 2021) through which regional climate is, in the end, experienced.


## 3.7 Regional Extremes in Depth: Heat, Water, and the Monsoon

### 3.7.1 Extreme heat is the deadliest and most attributable of the regional extremes, and its human toll is governed by humidity, urban form, and social vulnerability (*high confidence*).

Among the classes of extreme assessed in this chapter, heat combines the clearest physical attribution with among the largest and most direct effects on human life. Because warming shifts the entire distribution of daily temperatures toward higher values, the probability of crossing any fixed high threshold rises disproportionately, so that events once considered rare become routine and previously unobserved extremes become possible. The physiological danger of heat, however, is not captured by air temperature alone. High humidity impairs the body's ability to shed heat through the evaporation of sweat, so that the combination of heat and humidity — not temperature in isolation — determines the physiological limit. Regions where extreme heat coincides with high humidity therefore face a hazard qualitatively more dangerous than dry heat of the same temperature, and the geography of this combined hazard does not coincide with the geography of temperature alone.

The urban dimension sharpens the hazard further. The urban heat island raises temperatures in built-up areas relative to their surroundings, with the greatest effect often at night, when elevated minimum temperatures deny the body the recovery that cooler nights would otherwise provide. The intensity of this effect, and therefore the excess heat exposure it imposes, is distributed inequitably across major cities, falling disproportionately on particular populations (Hsu et al., 2021). The consequences for health are documented and growing: extreme weather and climate events impose measurable burdens on population health and on the capacity of health systems to respond (Ebi et al., 2021), and successive annual assessments record rising exposure of vulnerable groups to dangerous heat, together with heat-related mortality and lost labour capacity (Romanello et al., 2022; Romanello et al., 2023; Romanello et al., 2024). The people most at risk — the elderly, the chronically ill, the very young, and those who work or live without access to cooling — are frequently the same people most exposed to the intensified urban heat island, so that physical and social vulnerability compound. Heat is thus the extreme in which the coupling of physical hazard to social vulnerability is most direct, and in which the difference between the physical signal and the human disaster is most starkly a function of exposure and adaptive capacity.

Heat also acts as a multiplier on other hazards. It raises evaporative demand, deepening drought (Yuan et al., 2023); it desiccates fuels, priming landscapes for fire (Abram et al., 2021); and it stresses energy and water systems at the moment of peak demand, when the failure of cooling can itself become lethal. The tendency of heat to co-occur with and intensify other hazards makes it a central node in the compound-event problem, and any assessment of regional heat risk that stops at the physical hazard omits both the humidity that governs its lethality and the social structure that governs who is harmed.

### 3.7.2 Water is the medium through which most regional climate change is ultimately felt, from drought and flood to the seasonal reliability of supply (*high confidence*).

If heat is the deadliest of the extremes, water is the medium through which the widest range of regional climate impacts is transmitted. The same warming that intensifies heat also intensifies the water cycle, but unevenly and in ways that increase both flood and drought hazard, sometimes in the same region in different seasons. On the flood side, the intensification of short-duration rainfall extremes (Fowler et al., 2021) raises the risk of flash and urban flooding, and the concentration of flood exposure among the poor (Rentschler et al., 2022) ensures that the human costs fall disproportionately on the vulnerable. On the drought side, the shift toward rapidly developing flash droughts (Yuan et al., 2023) and the emergence of severe multi-year megadroughts (Williams et al., 2022) increase the frequency and severity of water shortage.

Between these extremes lies the equally important question of the seasonal and interannual reliability of supply, which depends on the natural buffers that store water between the times it falls and the times it is needed. The mountain snowpack is one such buffer, and elevation-dependent warming (Pepin et al., 2022) erodes it by shifting precipitation from snow to rain and advancing the timing of melt. Groundwater is another, and it is under simultaneous pressure from over-extraction and from a changing climate that alters recharge (Scanlon et al., 2023). The security of a region's water supply depends less on its annual precipitation total than on the integrity of these buffers, because it is the storage of water across seasons and years — not the annual mean — that carries a region through its dry periods and its droughts. Where the snowpack diminishes, the glaciers retreat, and the aquifers deplete simultaneously, the buffering capacity of the water system can be exhausted even without a change in annual rainfall, converting ordinary dry spells into crises. Water is therefore the domain in which the interaction of the several regional signals assessed in this chapter — mountain warming, groundwater stress, shifting drought and flood, and the monsoon considered next — is most consequential, and in which integrated assessment across those signals is most necessary.

### 3.7.3 The monsoon integrates the regional water signal for a majority of humanity, and its projection is a leading assessment priority (*medium confidence*).

For the majority of the world's population, the regional water balance is organised by the monsoon, whose seasonal reversal of winds and associated wet season deliver the bulk of annual rainfall across large parts of Asia, Africa, Australia, and the Americas. The monsoon integrates many of the processes assessed separately in this chapter: it is driven by the land-sea thermal contrast and the distribution of atmospheric moisture, both affected by anthropogenic forcing; it delivers rainfall through convection, whose sub-daily extremes drive flood hazard (Fowler et al., 2021); and it feeds the rivers and aquifers whose stresses shape water security (Scanlon et al., 2023). Its behaviour under warming is shaped by the competition between greenhouse-gas forcing, which tends through increased atmospheric moisture to strengthen monsoon rainfall, and aerosol forcing, which has historically tended to weaken several monsoon systems by cooling the surface and altering atmospheric stability. The regional balance between these opposing influences differs by basin and evolves as emissions change, which is one reason monsoon projection carries large uncertainty even where the direction of the thermodynamic component is agreed.

The coordinated scenario experiments that structure regional projection (Tebaldi et al., 2021) provide the framework within which monsoon change is assessed, but the translation of a given global forcing into monsoon rainfall depends on regional processes — convection, land-atmosphere coupling, aerosol-cloud interaction — whose representation still differs across models, leaving substantial spread in projected monsoon change. The stakes of this uncertainty are high because the systems that depend on the monsoon are so sensitive to it. Agriculture is the most immediate: the emergence of climate impacts on global crop production in the latest generation of coupled climate and crop models (Jägermeyr et al., 2021) is felt with particular force in monsoon regions, where food systems are tightly coupled to the seasonal rains. A monsoon that becomes more erratic — even without a large change in its seasonal total — can undermine the reliability on which rain-fed agriculture depends, and when superimposed on diminished snowpack (Pepin et al., 2022) and depleted groundwater (Scanlon et al., 2023) can compound water insecurity across some of the most populous regions on Earth. Improving the projection of regional monsoon change, and communicating its uncertainty honestly, is therefore among the highest priorities of regional climate assessment, both for its intrinsic scientific difficulty and for the number of people whose water and food security it governs.

### 3.7.4 Tropical cyclones concentrate regional hazard, and warming reshapes their intensity, rainfall, and coastal impact even where frequency trends remain uncertain (*medium confidence*).

Tropical cyclones warrant a final focused treatment because they concentrate several of the most destructive regional hazards into single events and because their response to warming illustrates the general principle that thermodynamic changes are better constrained than dynamical ones. The evidentiary basis for assessing cyclone behaviour is the set of long-term regional best-track records, of which the western North Pacific database maintained over decades is an example (Lu et al., 2021); these records make trend analysis possible but require careful accounting for the evolution of observing technology before a genuine climate signal can be separated from changes in observation. Where the physics is thermodynamic, expectations are relatively clear. A warmer ocean supplies more energy and a warmer atmosphere holds more moisture, favouring higher peak intensities and heavier cyclone rainfall even if the total number of storms does not increase. Because a warmer atmosphere intensifies short-duration rainfall in general (Fowler et al., 2021), the freshwater flooding produced by intense or slow-moving cyclones is a growing and distinct component of their hazard, separate from wind and surge.

The coastal impact of cyclones is amplified by rising sea level, which lifts the baseline on which storm surge acts so that a cyclone of unchanged intensity drives higher coastal water levels and reaches further inland than it would have in the past. When intense cyclone rainfall coincides with storm surge on an elevated sea level and with high river flow from the same or preceding storms, the result is a compound coastal flood that can far exceed what any single driver would produce — the kind of interacting hazard a complex-risk framework is designed to represent (Simpson et al., 2021). The human consequences, as for other flood hazards, are governed by exposure and vulnerability: the concentration of flood exposure among the poor (Rentschler et al., 2022), often most acute in the informal settlements of rapidly growing coastal cities, means that the compounding physical hazard of cyclones meets some of the sharpest gradients of vulnerability. Basin-wide frequency trends remain uncertain, but the reshaping of cyclone intensity, rainfall, and coastal reach under warming, combined with the concentration of exposure at vulnerable coasts, makes tropical cyclones a priority for the integrated assessment of physical hazard, exposure, and adaptive capacity that this chapter has argued is the necessary form of regional climate assessment.


## 3.8 Synthesis: The Regional Character of a Changing Climate

### 3.8.1 The evidence for amplified, spatially structured regional change is strong and, through attribution, increasingly linked to human influence (*high confidence*).

The central finding of this chapter is that climate change is experienced regionally, not as a uniform global increment, and that the regional signals are large, physically distinct, and increasingly attributable. The Arctic has warmed at close to four times the global rate since the late 1970s (Rantanen et al., 2022), and the world's mountains are warming in an elevation-dependent manner that outpaces adjacent lowlands (Pepin et al., 2022); these are not scaled-down versions of the global mean but distinct phenomena driven by sea-ice, snow-albedo, and lapse-rate feedbacks. Hot extremes have intensified across most inhabited regions and are amplified in cities by an urban heat island whose burden falls inequitably (Hsu et al., 2021). Short-duration rainfall extremes are intensifying, in places beyond simple thermodynamic scaling (Fowler et al., 2021). Drought is shifting in character toward rapid onset (Yuan et al., 2023) and toward severe multi-year megadroughts (Williams et al., 2022), while the groundwater that buffers drought is under simultaneous stress (Scanlon et al., 2023). Fire regimes are intensifying in dryland and Mediterranean-type regions (Abram et al., 2021), with smoke carrying health impacts across far wider areas (Aguilera et al., 2021). These changes increasingly co-occur and interact, requiring a framework of complex, cascading risk to assess them adequately (Simpson et al., 2021).

The maturation of event attribution has transformed these physical findings into statements about human influence, by comparing the likelihood or intensity of events in the observed climate with their likelihood in a counterfactual climate without anthropogenic forcing. Attribution is most confident where the physical mechanism is thermodynamic and strongly expressed, as for heat and for short-duration rainfall (Fowler et al., 2021), and it has produced landmark results such as the quantification of the human contribution to the severity of the southwestern North American megadrought (Williams et al., 2022). The same physical understanding underwrites the economic valuation of climate damage through the social cost of carbon dioxide, now assessed as substantially higher than many values in regulatory use (Rennert et al., 2022), and motivates explicit attention to the catastrophic tail risks that attribution of past events cannot bound (Kemp et al., 2022). The coordinated modelling frameworks that support projection also support attribution (Tebaldi et al., 2021), and emerging data-driven methods (Bi et al., 2023) point toward larger ensembles and finer resolution that may sharpen the assessment of the convective extremes current models resolve poorly.

### 3.8.2 The human consequences of regional change are co-produced by physical hazard and by exposure, vulnerability, and adaptation, so that assessment of the two is inseparable (*high confidence*).

The second central finding is that the human significance of regional physical change is governed as much by exposure, vulnerability, and the quality of response as by the hazard itself. The concentration of flood exposure among the poor (Rentschler et al., 2022), the inequitable distribution of urban heat (Hsu et al., 2021), and the documented health burdens of extremes borne most heavily by the vulnerable (Ebi et al., 2021) and tracked in the annual Lancet Countdown (Romanello et al., 2024) all demonstrate that identical physical hazards produce radically different human outcomes according to who is exposed and how well they can prepare, absorb, and recover. Adaptation is occurring, but its coverage is uneven and its effects are not uniformly benign: interventions intended to reduce vulnerability have in documented cases redistributed or deepened it (Eriksen et al., 2021), a caution against treating adaptation as an unambiguous good and an argument for attention to the design, governance, and equity of response.

Regional change is transmitted into human welfare through coupled pathways — food (Jägermeyr et al., 2021), water (Pepin et al., 2022), groundwater (Scanlon et al., 2023), health (Ebi et al., 2021), disease (Baker et al., 2021), and energy (Osman et al., 2022) — none of which can be assessed in isolation, because an intervention or a shock in one reverberates through the others. The coupled human-natural system (Folke et al., 2021), operating within an Earth system that has transgressed the majority of its planetary boundaries (Richardson et al., 2023), is therefore the appropriate unit of regional assessment, and comprehensive review confirms that impacts, adaptation, and mitigation must be pursued together (Abbass et al., 2022). The task of assessing regional climate and extreme events is, in the end, inseparable from the assessment of the human and ecological systems through which climate is experienced — a physical science of hazard joined to an understanding of the exposure and vulnerability that convert hazard into consequence. This chapter has argued that only the integration of the two, under a framework of complex and cascading risk (Simpson et al., 2021) and with honest communication of the uncertainties that remain largest for the dynamical and convective processes governing regional precipitation, can support the decisions that regional adaptation and mitigation now require.
