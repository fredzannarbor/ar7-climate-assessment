# Chapter 7: Regional Projections and Linking Global to Regional

## Executive Summary

**Regional climate change is now detectable across every inhabited continent, and the pattern of regional response is more robustly established than at any previous assessment (*high confidence*).** The dominant large-scale features of the warming pattern — amplified warming over land relative to ocean, amplified warming at high northern latitudes, and elevation-dependent warming in the world's mountain systems — are consistently simulated across model generations and corroborated by observations (Rantanen et al., 2022; Pepin et al., 2022). The task of linking global-mean change to actionable regional information is the central methodological challenge addressed in this chapter, and the tools for that linkage — dynamical downscaling, statistical downscaling, machine-learning emulation, and structured distillation — have matured substantially since the previous assessment (*high confidence*).

**The Arctic has warmed nearly four times faster than the global mean since 1979, and this amplification is a fundamental organizing feature of the Northern Hemisphere regional response (*virtually certain*).** Observational reanalysis and station records agree that Arctic amplification exceeds the factor-of-two figure long used as a heuristic (Rantanen et al., 2022). Elevation-dependent warming further reorganizes the vertical structure of change in mountain regions, with consequences for the cryosphere, hydrology, and downstream water supply (Pepin et al., 2022) (Rounce et al., 2023).

**Dynamical downscaling with convection-permitting regional climate models has emerged as the highest-fidelity approach for representing regional extremes, while machine-learning emulators now offer a computationally tractable route to large ensembles of regional information (*high confidence*).** The scenario framework underpinning all regional projection — the Shared Socioeconomic Pathway and forcing combinations coordinated through ScenarioMIP — provides the common boundary conditions that make cross-method comparison meaningful (Tebaldi et al., 2021).

**Regional projections of the water cycle show intensification of both wet and dry extremes, with flash drought onset accelerating and drought frequency, duration, and intensity showing detectable anthropogenic signals (*high confidence*).** A global transition toward faster-developing droughts is projected under continued warming (Yuan et al., 2023), and formal attribution links the observed changes in drought characteristics to human influence (Chiang et al., 2021). Flood and drought prediction remain among the hardest regional problems because they integrate across scales and depend sensitively on land-surface and human-management processes (Brunner et al., 2021).

**Regional heat extremes are intensifying faster than mean warming in several mid-latitude regions, driven in part by changes in atmospheric circulation that are imperfectly captured by coarse-resolution models (*high confidence*).** Accelerated western European heatwave trends have been linked to more-persistent double-jet configurations over Eurasia (Rousi et al., 2022), and the 2021 Pacific Northwest event exceeded prior statistical expectations by a margin that stressed conventional extreme-value frameworks (White et al., 2023). The emerging southwestern North American megadrought represents a regional hydroclimate state with few analogues in the instrumental or paleoclimate record (Williams et al., 2022).

**Storylines and physically self-consistent narrative approaches have become a standard complement to probabilistic projection for communicating deeply uncertain, high-impact regional outcomes (*high confidence*).** Complex, compounding, and cascading risks are not well served by marginal probability distributions alone, and structured frameworks for representing interacting drivers are now central to regional risk assessment (Simpson et al., 2021). Interacting tipping elements can raise the risk of cascading regional transitions beyond what any single element would imply (Wunderling et al., 2021), and low-likelihood high-impact outcomes warrant explicit treatment rather than exclusion (Kemp et al., 2022).

**Distillation of regional climate information — the expert-mediated synthesis of multiple, sometimes conflicting, lines of evidence into fit-for-purpose products — is now recognized as an essential step between raw model output and decision-relevant knowledge (*high confidence*).** No single model, method, or ensemble is authoritative for a given region; credible regional information emerges from the reconciliation of dynamical, statistical, observational, and process-understanding evidence.

## 7.1 Introduction: The Regionalization Problem

The global-mean surface temperature is a convenient scalar summary of planetary energy imbalance, but no community, ecosystem, or economy experiences the global mean. People experience the regional and local expression of change — the frequency of dangerous heat, the seasonality of rainfall, the height of the storm surge, the timing of snowmelt. This chapter addresses the scientific problem of translating the well-constrained global signal into the regional detail that adaptation and risk management require, and it assesses the methods, their evaluation, and the resulting regional projections.

Two facts frame the problem. First, the global signal is comparatively well constrained: the sign and approximate magnitude of global warming per unit cumulative CO2 emission are robust, and the global carbon budget that governs the total forcing is quantified to within assessed uncertainty each year (Friedlingstein et al., 2023) (Friedlingstein et al., 2025). Second, the regional signal is comparatively poorly constrained: regional precipitation change, in particular, carries large model spread arising from differences in the simulation of atmospheric circulation, land-surface feedbacks, and unresolved convection. The regionalization problem is therefore not merely one of spatial refinement but of adding physically credible information that the global models cannot, by construction, provide.

The regional response to forcing is organized by a small number of large-scale patterns that recur across models and are grounded in physical understanding. Land warms faster than ocean because of differences in heat capacity and evaporative constraint. High northern latitudes warm faster than the tropics through the combined action of surface-albedo feedback, changes in poleward heat and moisture transport, lapse-rate feedback, and reduced longwave cooling efficiency — a suite of processes that together produce Arctic amplification approaching a factor of four relative to the global mean over the satellite era (Rantanen et al., 2022). Mountains warm in an elevation-dependent manner, with the vertical profile of warming shaped by snow-albedo feedback, changes in the freezing level, cloud and water-vapour feedbacks, and aerosol effects, such that high-elevation environments can warm faster than adjacent lowlands (Pepin et al., 2022). These organizing patterns are the scaffolding on which regional projection is built.

The scenario architecture that supplies boundary conditions for all regional work is coordinated internationally. The Scenario Model Intercomparison Project defines a set of forcing pathways spanning very low to very high emissions, combining Shared Socioeconomic Pathways with radiative-forcing targets, so that regional downscaling and impact studies share a common experimental design (Tebaldi et al., 2021). This coordination is what allows a convection-permitting simulation over the Alps, a statistical downscaling over the Sahel, and a machine-learning emulator over East Asia to be compared as estimates of the same forced future rather than as incommensurable exercises. The individual Earth system models that provide the driving fields — for example, the CMCC Earth System Model contribution to the sixth phase of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project — are documented and evaluated so that the provenance of any regional product can be traced to a characterized global simulation (Lovato et al., 2022).

Regional projection is inseparable from the observational infrastructure used to evaluate it. Global reanalyses, high-resolution land datasets, and specialized products for soils, elevation, and tides supply the reference against which regional models are judged and, increasingly, the training data for machine-learning methods. Land-surface parameter datasets such as global soil-property grids (Poggio et al., 2021), hydrological model frameworks (Schmied et al., 2021), forest-and-building-removed terrain models (Hawker et al., 2022), and global tide atlases (Lyard et al., 2021) are foundational inputs to regional modelling of the water cycle, coastal flooding, and land-atmosphere exchange. The credibility of a regional projection depends as much on the quality of these inputs as on the resolution of the model.

This chapter proceeds as follows. Section 7.2 assesses downscaling methods — dynamical, statistical, and machine-learning — and their respective strengths, limitations, and appropriate domains of application. Section 7.3 assesses how regional models are evaluated, including the observational references and process-based diagnostics that establish fitness for purpose. Section 7.4 assesses the distillation of regional climate information, the synthesis step that reconciles multiple lines of evidence into decision-relevant products. Section 7.5 assesses regional projections organized by continental region, integrating the physical drivers, method-specific findings, and key uncertainties for each. Section 7.6 assesses storylines and the broader problem of communicating deep uncertainty, including the treatment of low-likelihood high-impact outcomes and interacting tipping elements.

## 7.2 Downscaling Methods

### 7.2.1 Dynamical downscaling and convection-permitting simulation

**Dynamical downscaling with regional climate models, and in particular the transition to convection-permitting resolutions, provides the most physically complete representation of regional climate and is the reference method for regional extremes (*high confidence*).** A regional climate model solves the governing equations of the atmosphere (and, when coupled, of the land, ocean, and cryosphere) on a limited-area grid at resolution far finer than the driving global model, taking its lateral boundary conditions from a global simulation or reanalysis. The added value of dynamical downscaling is greatest where fine-scale surface forcing — complex topography, coastlines, land-cover heterogeneity, urban surfaces — organizes the regional climate in ways the coarse driving model cannot resolve. Mountain precipitation, land-sea breeze systems, and orographically anchored convection are canonical examples where the finer grid materially changes the simulated climate.

The most consequential recent advance is the move to convection-permitting resolutions, at which the model grid is fine enough (typically a few kilometres) to represent deep convective updraughts explicitly rather than through a convective parameterization. Explicit convection improves the diurnal cycle of summer precipitation, the intensity distribution of sub-daily rainfall, and the representation of mesoscale convective organization — precisely the features that govern flash-flood and extreme-precipitation risk. This is directly relevant to the hardest regional water-cycle problems, where the parameterization of convection is a leading source of error in flood and drought simulation (Brunner et al., 2021). Convection-permitting simulation is computationally expensive, which historically limited it to short time slices or small ensembles; the resulting tension between resolution and ensemble size is a recurring theme in the interpretation of dynamical results.

Dynamical downscaling inherits both the strengths and the errors of its driving model. Biases in the large-scale circulation of the global model — for example in the position and persistence of jets and storm tracks — propagate through the lateral boundaries into the regional domain, and no amount of interior resolution can fully correct them. This inheritance is central to the interpretation of mid-latitude extremes, where circulation errors dominate the regional response. The observed acceleration of western European heatwaves has been linked to more-persistent double-jet states over Eurasia (Rousi et al., 2022); a regional model driven by a global model that under-represents such persistence will under-represent the heat extreme regardless of its own resolution. This is why dynamical downscaling is assessed as necessary but not sufficient: it adds fine-scale value conditional on a credible large-scale driver.

### 7.2.2 Statistical downscaling and bias adjustment

**Statistical downscaling and bias adjustment provide computationally efficient regional information and are well suited to large ensembles and impact applications, provided the stationarity of the statistical relationships is critically examined (*high confidence*).** Statistical downscaling establishes empirical relationships between large-scale predictors (from a global model or reanalysis) and local-scale predictands (station or gridded observations), then applies those relationships to projected large-scale fields. Methods range from simple transfer functions and analogue methods to distributional bias-correction and quantile-mapping schemes. Their principal advantage is cost: once trained, they can be applied to every member of a large global ensemble and to many scenarios, delivering the sampling of uncertainty that expensive dynamical runs cannot.

The foundational assumption of statistical downscaling is stationarity — that the empirical predictor–predictand relationship established over the observed period continues to hold under a changed climate. This assumption is most defensible where the local variable is tightly slaved to a well-resolved large-scale predictor (for instance, near-surface temperature to free-tropospheric temperature) and least defensible where local processes with their own climate sensitivity intervene (for instance, convective precipitation, or snow processes near the freezing point). Bias adjustment carries a related risk: correcting a model's distribution toward observations can mask, rather than remedy, errors in the underlying physical processes, and can distort the trend if the bias is state-dependent. These limitations are acute for the water cycle, where land-surface feedbacks and human water management complicate the predictor–predictand mapping (Schmied et al., 2021; Brunner et al., 2021).

Statistical methods depend critically on the quality and homogeneity of the observational reference used for training. Reanalysis products and high-resolution observational datasets supply these references, and their uncertainties propagate directly into the downscaled product. For land-surface and hydrological variables, harmonized global datasets of soil properties (Poggio et al., 2021) and terrain (Hawker et al., 2022) constrain the boundary conditions, while for coastal applications global tide models provide the deterministic component of sea level that must be separated from the climate-driven change (Lyard et al., 2021). The maturity of these reference datasets is a quiet but decisive enabler of credible statistical downscaling.

### 7.2.3 Machine-learning emulators and hybrid methods

**Machine-learning methods now provide fast, skilful emulation of both dynamical downscaling and full climate-model behaviour, enabling large-ensemble regional information at a fraction of the computational cost, while raising new questions about physical consistency and out-of-sample reliability (*high confidence*).** The application of machine learning to climate problems has expanded rapidly, spanning emulation, downscaling, bias correction, parameterization, and the extraction of predictable structure from observations (Kaack et al., 2022). In the regional context, machine-learning models are trained to map coarse-resolution fields to fine-resolution targets — either observed high-resolution fields or the output of expensive convection-permitting simulations — learning the statistical relationship between scales directly from data.

The appeal is computational. A trained emulator can downscale an entire global ensemble across all scenarios in the time a single dynamical run would take, closing the resolution-versus-ensemble-size tension that constrains dynamical methods. This makes machine learning particularly attractive for applications that require large samples: estimating the tails of extreme distributions, propagating scenario and internal-variability uncertainty into impact models, and generating the high-dimensional regional information that adaptation planning increasingly demands. Machine-learning approaches have shown particular promise in data-rich, process-complex domains such as hydrology, where they have been used to predict variables like groundwater levels that depend on many interacting drivers (Hai et al., 2022).

The concerns are physical. A purely data-driven emulator has no built-in guarantee of conservation, of physical consistency across variables, or of reliability outside the range of its training data — a serious limitation for climate projection, whose entire purpose is to extrapolate to unobserved states. The most credible regional machine-learning work therefore tends toward hybrid designs that embed physical constraints, that use dynamical output as training targets so that the physics is inherited, and that are evaluated against held-out extremes rather than only against mean behaviour. Machine learning is assessed here as a powerful accelerant that does not replace physical modelling but redistributes where the physics enters — from the forward simulation to the training data and the architectural constraints (Kaack et al., 2022; Hai et al., 2022).

### 7.2.4 Choosing and combining methods

No single downscaling method dominates across all regions, variables, and applications. Dynamical downscaling is preferred where fine-scale physical processes generate the signal and where extremes matter most, but it is expensive and inherits driving-model error. Statistical downscaling and bias adjustment are preferred where large ensembles and computational efficiency matter, but they rest on stationarity assumptions that must be tested. Machine-learning emulation increasingly bridges the two, delivering dynamical-like fine-scale structure at statistical-like cost, but it demands careful evaluation of physical consistency and out-of-sample behaviour. Best practice combines methods — using dynamical output to train and validate emulators, using statistical methods to expand ensembles, and using the spread across methods as a component of the uncertainty estimate. The reconciliation of these method-specific estimates is the subject of the distillation step assessed in Section 7.4.

## 7.3 Regional Model Evaluation

### 7.3.1 Observational references for regional evaluation

**The evaluation of regional models rests on a maturing but heterogeneous observational infrastructure, and the fitness of any regional projection is bounded by the quality and coverage of the reference data used to assess it (*high confidence*).** Regional model evaluation compares simulated climate against observations across many variables, timescales, and statistics — not only the mean state but the seasonal cycle, interannual variability, the frequency and intensity of extremes, and the fidelity of the physical processes that generate them. The reference datasets that make this possible have improved substantially, but they remain unevenly distributed in space and time, with dense coverage in well-instrumented regions and sparse coverage in exactly the tropical and high-latitude regions where model uncertainty is largest.

Reanalyses provide the spatially and temporally complete reference that station networks cannot, assimilating heterogeneous observations into a dynamically consistent estimate of the atmospheric and land state. High-resolution land reanalyses are particularly valuable for evaluating the near-surface variables that matter for impacts, providing consistent fields of temperature, precipitation, soil moisture, and surface fluxes at a resolution approaching that of regional models. Specialized reference datasets extend evaluation to variables the atmosphere-focused reanalyses represent poorly: global soil-property grids constrain the land-surface lower boundary (Poggio et al., 2021), harmonized hydrological model output provides a reference for river discharge and water storage where gauge data are sparse (Schmied et al., 2021), forest-and-building-removed terrain models sharpen the representation of true ground elevation for flood and coastal work (Hawker et al., 2022), and global tide atlases separate the astronomical tide from the climate-driven and surge components of coastal sea level (Lyard et al., 2021).

The uncertainty in the reference itself must be carried through evaluation. Where multiple observational products disagree — a common situation for precipitation over mountains, for soil moisture, and for surface fluxes — the observational spread can rival or exceed the model spread, and a model cannot be judged "wrong" against a reference that is itself poorly constrained. Honest regional evaluation therefore reports observational uncertainty alongside model error and is cautious about declaring skill or bias where the reference is weak. This is especially important in data-sparse regions, where the temptation to over-interpret a single reference product is strongest and where machine-learning methods trained on that single product inherit its errors most directly.

### 7.3.2 Process-based evaluation and added value

**Regional models are increasingly evaluated on their representation of the physical processes that generate regional climate, not only on their output statistics, because process fidelity is what licenses extrapolation to a changed climate (*high confidence*).** A model can reproduce the observed mean and variance for the wrong physical reasons — through compensating errors — and such a model provides no assurance that its projected change is credible. Process-based evaluation therefore interrogates whether the model represents the mechanisms correctly: whether orographic precipitation is anchored to the right terrain, whether the diurnal cycle of convection has the right timing and amplitude, whether land-atmosphere coupling has the right strength, and whether the large-scale circulation modes that organize regional variability are simulated with the right structure and persistence.

The evaluation of circulation is decisive for mid-latitude extremes. The persistence of blocking and of anomalous jet configurations governs the duration of heat and cold extremes, and errors in simulated persistence translate directly into errors in projected extremes. The linkage between accelerated western European heat and more-persistent double-jet states illustrates the point: evaluating a regional model's heat projection requires evaluating the driving model's jet dynamics, because the fine grid cannot manufacture circulation persistence that the boundary conditions do not supply (Rousi et al., 2022). Similarly, the exceptional 2021 Pacific Northwest heat event — which exceeded prior statistical expectations by a wide margin — exposed the limitations of evaluating models only against the observed range, and motivated process-based diagnostics of the ingredients (soil-moisture deficit, ridge amplitude, adiabatic warming) that combined to produce the event (White et al., 2023).

Added value — the demonstrable improvement a downscaled product provides over its driving model — is the practical criterion for whether downscaling is worthwhile for a given application. Added value is clearest for variables and statistics tied to fine-scale surface forcing: sub-daily precipitation intensity, near-surface wind in complex terrain, temperature in mountain valleys and coastal zones. It is weakest, and can even be negative, for large-scale variables that the driving model already represents well and that the regional model may degrade by inheriting boundary errors while adding its own. Demonstrating added value requires evaluation at the scale at which the value is claimed, against a reference that resolves that scale — a requirement that circles back to the adequacy of the observational infrastructure.

### 7.3.3 Evaluation of extremes and compound events

**The evaluation of regional models for compound and cascading events — where multiple drivers coincide or interact — is less mature than the evaluation of univariate extremes, and represents a leading frontier and a leading uncertainty (*high confidence*).** Many of the most damaging regional outcomes are compound: hot-and-dry conditions that drive wildfire and crop failure, wet-and-windy conditions that drive coastal flooding, sequential events that exhaust response capacity before the next arrives. Evaluating a model's fidelity for such outcomes requires assessing the dependence structure between variables — the tendency for hot and dry, or wet and windy, to co-occur — not merely the marginal behaviour of each variable in isolation.

Regional models vary in their skill at representing these dependencies, and the observational record of compound events is short, making evaluation statistically difficult. The physical realism of compound-event simulation depends on correctly coupled land and atmosphere: soil-moisture depletion amplifying heat, vegetation stress feeding back on the surface energy budget, snowpack timing setting the stage for summer drought. The declining resilience of forests and the risk of abrupt ecosystem transitions under compound stress add a further layer, because a model that treats vegetation as static will misrepresent the feedbacks that intensify compound extremes (Forzieri et al., 2022; Boulton et al., 2022). The frequency with which known human pathogenic diseases can be aggravated by climatic hazards underscores that compound physical events cascade into compound impacts, widening the evaluation target from the physical climate to the coupled human–natural system (Mora et al., 2022).

Evaluation of the water cycle deserves particular emphasis because it integrates so many processes. The prediction of floods and droughts is a persistent challenge precisely because it requires the model to get right the precipitation forcing, the land-surface partitioning, the subsurface storage, and — for managed basins — the human operation of reservoirs and withdrawals (Brunner et al., 2021). A global transition toward flash droughts, which develop over weeks rather than seasons, sets a demanding evaluation target because it stresses the model's representation of fast land-atmosphere coupling and evaporative demand (Yuan et al., 2023). Detectable anthropogenic signals in drought frequency, duration, and intensity provide an additional evaluation constraint: a credible regional model should reproduce not only the climatology of drought but the emerging trend attributable to human influence (Chiang et al., 2021).

## 7.4 Distillation of Regional Climate Information

### 7.4.1 The distillation concept

**Credible regional climate information is produced by distillation — the expert-mediated synthesis of multiple, often discordant, lines of evidence into fit-for-purpose products — and no single model or method should be treated as authoritative for a region (*high confidence*).** The proliferation of methods assessed in Section 7.2, and the recognition that each carries characteristic strengths and errors, makes reconciliation unavoidable. Distillation is the process by which dynamical simulations, statistical downscaling, machine-learning emulation, observational analysis, and physical understanding are weighed against one another and combined into a coherent statement of what is known, what is uncertain, and what is decision-relevant for a specific region and application.

Distillation is necessary because the raw evidence rarely agrees. Different models can project opposite signs of regional precipitation change; different downscaling methods can amplify or damp the driving signal; observational references can disagree about the baseline. A user handed the full envelope of raw output faces an uninterpretable spread. The distillation step adds value by bringing physical reasoning to bear on that spread — asking which projections are supported by understood mechanisms, which reflect known model deficiencies, and which represent genuine, irreducible uncertainty. It is an expert judgment process, and its credibility depends on being transparent about the evidence and the reasoning rather than on presenting a single number with false precision.

The concept is closely tied to the framing of climate change as a complex risk problem. A framework for complex climate-change risk assessment recognizes that risk emerges from the interaction of hazard, exposure, vulnerability, and response, and that these interact across scales and sectors in ways that no single model captures (Simpson et al., 2021). Distillation of the physical climate information is the first stage of such an assessment, and it must be conducted with the downstream risk framing in view, so that the information distilled is the information the risk assessment actually needs.

### 7.4.2 Reconciling multiple lines of evidence

**Robust regional conclusions rest on the convergence of independent lines of evidence — models, observations, process understanding, and paleoclimate analogues — and the strength of a regional statement should scale with the breadth of that convergence (*high confidence*).** Where dynamical models, statistical methods, observed trends, and physical theory all point the same way, confidence is high even if the precise magnitude remains uncertain. Where they diverge, the divergence itself is information, signalling either a genuine bifurcation in possible futures or an unresolved deficiency in one or more lines of evidence.

Several regional signals now enjoy strong multi-line convergence. Arctic amplification is supported by observations, by consistent model behaviour, and by well-understood feedback physics, so the qualitative conclusion — that the Arctic warms far faster than the global mean — is among the most robust in regional climate science (Rantanen et al., 2022). Elevation-dependent warming in mountains is similarly supported by observations across many ranges and by feedback theory, even where the magnitude at a specific elevation remains uncertain (Pepin et al., 2022). The retreat of glaciers is observed globally, simulated consistently, and physically inevitable given the warming, so the projection that every increment of temperature drives further glacier loss is robust even where basin-scale timing is uncertain (Rounce et al., 2023).

Other regional signals remain contested, and distillation must represent that honestly. Regional precipitation change in the tropics and subtropics, the future of specific monsoon systems, and the fate of particular circulation features are areas where models diverge and where the distilled product must convey a wide range rather than a point estimate. The role of groundwater in buffering regional water stress adds a further consideration: even where the atmospheric signal is uncertain, the state of the subsurface store shapes regional resilience, and rapid groundwater decline in many aquifers narrows the buffer available to absorb climatic variability (Scanlon et al., 2023) (Jasechko et al., 2024). Distillation for water-stressed regions must therefore integrate the atmospheric projection with the hydrological and human-management context.

### 7.4.3 Fitness for purpose and co-production

**Regional climate information is fit for purpose only in relation to a specific decision, and the co-production of information with the users who will act on it improves both its relevance and its uptake (*high confidence*).** The same physical projection can be adequate for one decision and inadequate for another: a coarse estimate of mean warming may suffice for a broad adaptation strategy but fail entirely for the design of flood defences that depend on the tail of the sub-daily rainfall distribution. Fitness for purpose is thus a joint property of the information and the decision, and cannot be assessed by climate science alone.

Co-production — the collaborative development of climate information by scientists and users — is assessed as improving fitness for purpose because it clarifies what variables, thresholds, and timescales the decision actually turns on, and because it surfaces the tolerance for uncertainty that the decision can accommodate. It also guards against the delivery of technically impressive but decision-irrelevant products. The framework of complex climate-change risk assessment provides the structure within which such co-production proceeds, linking the physical distillation to the exposure, vulnerability, and response elements that determine actual risk (Simpson et al., 2021).

The distillation and co-production framing also disciplines the treatment of uncertainty. Rather than suppressing uncertainty to deliver a clean answer, or overwhelming the user with an uninterpretable envelope, distillation aims to convey the structure of the uncertainty — which parts are reducible, which are irreducible, which are consequential for the decision at hand. This sets up the storyline and uncertainty-communication approaches assessed in Section 7.6, which provide concrete methods for representing deep uncertainty in a decision-relevant form. It also connects to the broader recognition that the climate and biodiversity crises are coupled, so that regional information distilled for one domain must be consistent with, and ideally integrated with, the information distilled for the other (Pörtner et al., 2023).

## 7.5 Regional Projections by Continental Region

This section assesses projected regional change organized by continental region, integrating the physical drivers, the method-specific evidence, and the leading uncertainties for each. The organizing patterns established in Section 7.1 — land-ocean contrast, high-latitude amplification, and elevation-dependent warming — recur throughout, modulated by the circulation, hydrology, and coastal geometry particular to each region.

### 7.5.1 The Arctic and high northern latitudes

**The Arctic is projected to continue warming at roughly three to four times the global rate, with the loss of sea ice, land ice, and permafrost integrity driving cascading regional and remote consequences (*high confidence*).** Arctic amplification is the defining regional signal of the climate system. Observations over the satellite era establish that the Arctic has warmed nearly four times faster than the globe since 1979, a factor substantially larger than the doubling long used as a rule of thumb, and one that models have historically tended to under-represent (Rantanen et al., 2022). The amplification arises from a well-understood combination of surface-albedo feedback as reflective ice and snow give way to dark ocean and land, lapse-rate feedback favoured by the stable Arctic boundary layer, changes in poleward heat and moisture transport, and cloud feedbacks.

The consequences propagate through the Arctic system and beyond. Glacier loss is universal and scales with warming: every increment of global temperature commits additional ice to loss, with implications for sea level and for the freshwater regimes of Arctic and mountain rivers (Rounce et al., 2023). Field campaigns synthesizing the coupled atmosphere-ice-ocean system across a full annual cycle have sharpened understanding of the processes governing the Arctic surface energy budget and boundary-layer structure, providing critical constraints for model evaluation in a region where conventional observations are sparse (Shupe et al., 2022). Plastic and other pollutant burdens now reach even the remote Arctic, a marker of the region's connection to the global system and a complicating factor for its ecosystems (Bergmann et al., 2022).

Whether Arctic change feeds back on mid-latitude weather through altered circulation remains an area of active investigation and lower confidence. The physical plausibility of an Arctic influence on jet behaviour is established, but the magnitude and detectability of such teleconnections against the noise of internal variability are contested, and this uncertainty must be carried honestly into any regional projection that invokes an Arctic-mid-latitude linkage.

### 7.5.2 North America

**North America is projected to experience intensifying hydroclimate extremes, with the western megadrought, escalating wildfire, and inequitable flood exposure among the most consequential regional signals (*high confidence*).** The emerging southwestern North American megadrought represents a regional hydroclimate state of exceptional severity; the drought intensified rapidly in 2020–2021 to a level with few analogues in the long paleoclimate record, and anthropogenic warming is assessed as a substantial contributor through its effect on evaporative demand and snowpack (Williams et al., 2022). The 2021 Pacific Northwest heat event demonstrated that the region's temperature extremes can exceed prior statistical expectations by a wide margin, driven by the coincidence of an amplified ridge, antecedent soil-moisture deficit, and strong adiabatic warming (White et al., 2023).

Wildfire activity is expanding in space and time across North America and other fire-prone regions, as warming and drying extend the length and intensity of fire-weather seasons (Senande-Rivera et al., 2022). Flood risk is both rising and inequitably distributed: analysis of United States flood exposure reveals patterns that fall disproportionately on socially vulnerable communities, so that the regional physical hazard translates into a sharply uneven distribution of harm (Wing et al., 2022). Groundwater, a critical buffer for North American agriculture and municipal supply, is declining rapidly in many aquifers, though some show recovery where management has intervened — a reminder that regional water outcomes depend on human decisions as much as on the atmospheric forcing (Jasechko et al., 2024) (Scanlon et al., 2023).

### 7.5.3 Europe and the Mediterranean

**Europe is projected to warm faster than the global mean, with the Mediterranean emerging as a regional hotspot of drying and heat, and western European heat extremes intensifying faster than mean warming (*high confidence*).** European heatwave trends have accelerated beyond what mean warming alone would produce, a behaviour linked to more-persistent double-jet configurations over Eurasia that favour the stagnation and amplification of surface heat (Rousi et al., 2022). This circulation dependence means that European heat projections are sensitive to the driving model's representation of jet dynamics, and that dynamical downscaling adds value only conditional on a credible large-scale driver.

The Eastern Mediterranean and Middle East constitute a distinct regional hotspot, projected to experience warming and an increase in weather extremes exceeding the global average, with severe implications for water availability, heat exposure, and habitability in an already water-stressed region (Zittis et al., 2022). Mediterranean drying compounds these pressures, and the regional water balance is further stressed by declining groundwater and rising demand (Scanlon et al., 2023). Cultural heritage across Europe and the Mediterranean is exposed to accelerating climatic pressures, adding a dimension of loss that regional assessment increasingly incorporates (Sesana et al., 2021).

### 7.5.4 Asia

**Asia faces intensifying monsoon variability, escalating heat and water stress, and acute exposure of its dense populations and agriculture to hydroclimate extremes, with high but regionally variable confidence (*high confidence*).** The continent's climate is organized by monsoon systems whose projected changes carry large uncertainty in the details even where the thermodynamic tendency toward more intense wet extremes is robust. Agricultural production across Asia is highly exposed to the changing climate, with heat, water stress, and shifting growing seasons posing challenges to food security for a large share of the world's population (Rahman et al., 2022).

Water resources are a central regional concern. High-mountain Asia's glaciers, the water towers for major river systems, are losing mass in step with warming, threatening the dry-season flows on which downstream populations depend (Rounce et al., 2023) (Pepin et al., 2022). Groundwater decline is severe in parts of Asia, narrowing the buffer against monsoon variability and drought (Jasechko et al., 2024) (Scanlon et al., 2023). Flash droughts, which develop rapidly and give little warning, are projected to become more common globally and pose a particular threat to Asian agriculture given its dependence on timely monsoon rainfall (Yuan et al., 2023). Soil salinization, driven by a combination of climatic and management factors, further threatens the productivity of irrigated Asian croplands (Hassani et al., 2021).

### 7.5.5 Africa

**Africa is projected to experience warming above the global mean over much of the continent, with severe water and food-security consequences amplified by high exposure and limited observational and adaptive infrastructure (*high confidence*).** Much of Africa faces the compounding challenge of a strong warming signal, an uncertain but consequential precipitation response, and a sparse observational network that limits both the evaluation of models and the detection of change. The northern reaches of the continent share the Eastern Mediterranean and Middle East hotspot dynamics, with warming and extremes projected to outpace the global average (Zittis et al., 2022).

Water and soils are the pivotal regional constraints. Groundwater, a critical resource across much of Africa, is under pressure from rising demand and variable recharge, and its state governs regional resilience to drought (Scanlon et al., 2023) (Jasechko et al., 2024). Soil salinization threatens agricultural land where irrigation and evaporative concentration interact with a warming, drying climate (Hassani et al., 2021). The dependence of African food security on rain-fed agriculture makes the uncertain precipitation projection especially consequential, and it places a premium on the distillation of the best-available regional information despite the observational gaps. Wildfire regimes are also shifting across African savannas and other biomes as fire-weather conditions change (Senande-Rivera et al., 2022).

### 7.5.6 Central and South America

**South America's projections are dominated by the fate of the Amazon, where observations indicate a pronounced loss of forest resilience that raises the risk of large-scale, partially irreversible regional transition (*high confidence*).** The Amazon rainforest has exhibited a pronounced loss of resilience since the early 2000s, detectable as a slowing recovery from perturbation, consistent with the system approaching a critical threshold beyond which forest could give way to a drier, degraded state over large areas (Boulton et al., 2022). Such a transition would have profound regional consequences for rainfall, carbon storage, and biodiversity, and it exemplifies the kind of high-impact, deeply uncertain outcome that regional assessment must represent even where its probability cannot be precisely quantified.

Forest resilience is declining more broadly, with emerging signals of stress detectable across many of the world's forests under the combined pressure of warming, drying, and disturbance (Forzieri et al., 2022). For South America this coupling of the physical climate to the biosphere is central: the regional water cycle is partly sustained by forest transpiration, so forest loss and hydroclimate change reinforce one another. The coupled climate and biodiversity crises are especially entangled in this region, and regional information distilled for the physical climate must be integrated with the ecological state to be decision-relevant (Pörtner et al., 2023). Andean glacier loss adds a further regional water-security dimension for populations dependent on glacier-fed flows (Rounce et al., 2023).

### 7.5.7 Australasia and the small-island and coastal domain

**Coastal and small-island regions worldwide face compounding sea-level rise, subsidence, and changing extremes, with relative sea-level change and coastal flood exposure driven by the interaction of climatic and local land-motion processes (*high confidence*).** For low-lying coasts and small islands, the regionally relevant quantity is not the global-mean sea-level rise but the relative sea-level change at the coast, which combines the climatic signal with vertical land motion. In many densely populated deltas and coastal cities, subsidence — often driven by groundwater and resource extraction — dominates the local relative rise, so that coastal flood exposure is a joint function of climate and local land management (Nicholls et al., 2021). Global tide models supply the deterministic tidal component that must be combined with mean-sea-level and surge changes to estimate total coastal water levels (Lyard et al., 2021), and accurate terrain models that remove the bias of forests and buildings are essential for credible coastal-flood mapping (Hawker et al., 2022).

Human infrastructure modulates regional flood risk in both directions. Dams and reservoirs can reduce downstream flood exposure under a changing climate, though their benefits are unevenly distributed and depend on operation (Boulangé et al., 2021). Marine heatwaves, with their severe biological impacts, are an oceanic regional hazard of growing importance for coastal and island economies dependent on fisheries and reefs (Smith et al., 2022). The regional projection for these domains is thus irreducibly coupled: the physical sea-level and ocean-temperature signals interact with land motion, infrastructure, and ecosystem state to determine actual regional outcomes.

## 7.6 Storylines and the Communication of Uncertainty

### 7.6.1 The storyline approach

**Physically self-consistent storylines have become a standard and complementary approach to probabilistic projection, particularly for communicating deeply uncertain, high-impact regional outcomes whose probabilities cannot be robustly quantified (*high confidence*).** A storyline is a physically coherent, internally consistent narrative of how a region's climate could unfold, constructed by conditioning on plausible states of the large-scale drivers rather than by attempting to assign a probability to every branch. The approach responds to a structural limitation of the probabilistic paradigm: for many regional variables, and especially for the circulation-dependent extremes that dominate mid-latitude risk, the model spread does not sample a well-defined probability distribution but reflects deep, poorly quantified uncertainty in the response of the atmospheric circulation.

The storyline method addresses this by decomposing the regional response into a thermodynamic component, which is comparatively robust and well constrained, and a dynamic component, which is uncertain and circulation-dependent. By holding the thermodynamic warming fixed and exploring a discrete set of plausible circulation futures, storylines represent the range of possible regional outcomes in a form that is physically interpretable and decision-relevant, without the false precision of a single probability distribution over an ill-constrained variable. The acceleration of western European heat under more-persistent double-jet states is a natural storyline axis: rather than averaging over an uncertain circulation response, a storyline can ask what the region's heat risk would be under a specified degree of jet persistence (Rousi et al., 2022). Similarly, the ingredients of the 2021 Pacific Northwest event — ridge amplitude, soil-moisture antecedents, adiabatic warming — define a storyline space within which the plausibility and severity of comparable future events can be explored (White et al., 2023).

Storylines are also well suited to event-based framing, in which a specific past or hypothetical event is used as the anchor and the question becomes how such an event would change in frequency or intensity under warming. This framing connects naturally to the attribution of extremes, which quantifies the human influence on the likelihood or magnitude of observed events and thereby grounds the storyline in a real, communicable occurrence (Clarke et al., 2022). Event-based storylines have proven particularly effective for communication because they attach the abstract statistics of climate change to a concrete, memorable episode that stakeholders have experienced. The southwestern North American megadrought, framed as an unfolding multi-decadal event with anthropogenic intensification, is a further example of a regional storyline anchored in an ongoing, experienced episode (Williams et al., 2022).

### 7.6.2 Compound, cascading, and systemic risk

**Regional risk is increasingly understood as compound, cascading, and systemic, and structured frameworks for representing interacting hazards, exposures, and vulnerabilities are now central to regional assessment (*high confidence*).** The most damaging regional outcomes rarely arise from a single hazard acting in isolation. They emerge from the coincidence of multiple drivers — heat and drought, storm surge and rainfall, sequential events that exhaust response capacity — and from the propagation of impacts across interconnected systems. A framework for complex climate-change risk assessment formalizes this understanding, treating risk as an emergent property of the interaction among hazard, exposure, vulnerability, and response, and providing a structure within which the physical climate storyline connects to the socioeconomic consequences (Simpson et al., 2021).

Compound events challenge both the physical modelling and the risk framing. Physically, they require correctly simulated dependence between variables, which is harder than simulating each variable's marginal behaviour and less well constrained by the short observational record. In terms of impact, they can produce consequences that exceed the sum of the individual hazards because systems stressed by one hazard become more vulnerable to the next. The observation that a majority of known human pathogenic diseases can be aggravated by climatic hazards illustrates how a physical hazard cascades into a compound health burden, widening the scope of regional risk assessment far beyond the physical climate (Mora et al., 2022). The declining resilience of forests and the possibility of abrupt ecosystem transition add an ecological cascade, in which a compound physical stress triggers a state change that then feeds back on the regional climate and hydrology (Forzieri et al., 2022; Boulton et al., 2022).

Systemic risk — the propagation of impacts through economic, infrastructural, and social networks — extends the cascade beyond the boundaries of the region where the hazard occurs. Flood exposure concentrated among the socially vulnerable amplifies the systemic consequences of a physical flood hazard, because the same physical event produces larger and more persistent social and economic disruption where vulnerability is high (Wing et al., 2022; Rentschler et al., 2022). Regional assessment that stops at the physical hazard therefore understates the risk; the complex-risk framework is the tool for carrying the analysis through to the systemic consequences that decision-makers ultimately face (Simpson et al., 2021). Economic valuation of these consequences, through metrics such as the social cost of carbon, provides one channel for aggregating regionally distributed damages into a decision-relevant summary, though such metrics are themselves sensitive to assumptions about the distribution and severity of regional impacts (Rennert et al., 2022).

### 7.6.3 Tipping elements and low-likelihood, high-impact outcomes

**Interacting tipping elements can raise the risk of cascading, partially irreversible regional transitions beyond what any single element implies, and low-likelihood, high-impact outcomes warrant explicit assessment rather than exclusion (*high confidence*).** Several components of the Earth system exhibit the potential for abrupt or self-sustaining change once a threshold is crossed, and these tipping elements are not independent: the crossing of one can raise the probability of crossing another through physical couplings, so that the risk of a domino-like cascade exceeds the product of the individual risks (Wunderling et al., 2021). For regional assessment, tipping dynamics matter because they can produce regional outcomes that lie outside the range of the smooth, incremental projections that dominate model-based estimates.

The Amazon provides a concrete regional instance. The observed loss of forest resilience since the early 2000s is consistent with the system approaching a threshold, and a transition to a drier, degraded state would be a regional tipping event with continental consequences for rainfall, carbon, and biodiversity (Boulton et al., 2022). Broader signals of declining forest resilience suggest that such vulnerabilities are not confined to a single region (Forzieri et al., 2022). Because the probability and timing of these transitions are deeply uncertain, they are precisely the kind of outcome for which the storyline approach and the explicit treatment of low-likelihood, high-impact possibilities are essential.

The case for explicit assessment of extreme outcomes is grounded in the asymmetry of consequences. Outcomes at the high-impact tail — catastrophic and potentially irreversible regional transitions — can dominate the risk even at low probability, and excluding them because they are hard to quantify would bias the assessment toward complacency (Kemp et al., 2022). The framing of climate change against planetary boundaries reinforces this: the transgression of multiple boundaries indicates that the Earth system is being pushed outside the range within which the smooth, well-sampled projections are most reliable, raising the salience of surprise and abrupt change (Richardson et al., 2023; Rockström et al., 2023). Regional assessment therefore has a responsibility to represent the deep tail, not only the well-constrained centre of the distribution.

### 7.6.4 Communicating regional uncertainty

**Effective communication of regional climate information conveys the structure of uncertainty — distinguishing the robust from the uncertain and the reducible from the irreducible — rather than either suppressing uncertainty or presenting an uninterpretable envelope (*high confidence*).** The distillation step produces information whose value is realized only if it is communicated in a form the user can act on. This requires calibrated language that matches the strength of the statement to the strength of the evidence, transparency about the lines of evidence and the reasoning behind the assessment, and a clear separation between what is robustly known and what remains deeply uncertain.

Storylines and event-based framings are assessed as effective communication tools because they render abstract uncertainty concrete and physically interpretable. Attaching a projection to a specific, attributable event connects the statistics to lived experience and makes the uncertainty tangible: the question "how much more likely has an event like this become?" is more communicable than an abstract probability density, and it is grounded in the attribution science that quantifies the human contribution to observed extremes (Clarke et al., 2022). The complex-risk framework provides the scaffolding for communicating not just the physical hazard but the full chain to consequence, which is what most decisions actually require (Simpson et al., 2021).

Communication must also respect the limits of the science. Where the observational network is sparse, where models disagree on the sign of change, or where the outcome depends on deeply uncertain circulation or tipping dynamics, the honest communication is one that conveys the range and its structure rather than a spuriously precise central estimate. The integration of climate and biodiversity information, and of physical and human-system information, further complicates the communication task but is essential for decision-relevance, because regional decisions are made in the coupled system, not in the physical climate alone (Pörtner et al., 2023). The overarching principle is fitness for purpose: the communication succeeds when the user can make a better-informed decision, with a correct understanding of both what the science supports and what it cannot yet resolve.

## 7.7 Cross-Cutting Advances, Limitations, and Research Priorities

### 7.7.1 The changing observational and data foundation

**Advances in global observational and reference datasets are a principal driver of improved regional projection, and the quality of regional information is increasingly limited by the reference data rather than by model resolution alone (*high confidence*).** The regional enterprise rests on a foundation of global datasets whose steady improvement quietly enables everything above it. High-resolution reanalyses provide dynamically consistent reference fields; harmonized soil-property grids constrain the land-surface lower boundary (Poggio et al., 2021); global hydrological model frameworks supply reference discharge and storage where gauges are absent (Schmied et al., 2021); terrain models with forests and buildings removed sharpen flood and coastal analysis (Hawker et al., 2022); and global tide atlases resolve the deterministic component of coastal sea level (Lyard et al., 2021). Where these datasets are strong, regional evaluation and downscaling are strong; where they are weak — over tropical land, high mountains, and the polar regions — regional information inherits the weakness. Field campaigns that intensively observe under-sampled regimes, such as the coordinated study of the central Arctic across a full annual cycle, materially reduce this limitation by supplying the process-level constraints that gridded products cannot (Shupe et al., 2022).

The observational foundation also increasingly includes non-traditional sources and specialized process datasets. Regional aerosol and dust processes, which modulate the surface energy budget and precipitation in arid and downwind regions, depend on characterizing the world's principal dust-source regions and their contribution to the global cycle (Kok et al., 2021). These process-level and observational advances are as consequential for regional credibility as any advance in model formulation, because they determine the reference against which every regional projection is judged and, increasingly, the data from which machine-learning methods learn.

### 7.7.2 Machine learning as an accelerant and a caution

**Machine learning is transforming the computational economics of regional projection, but its physical reliability outside the training distribution remains the central open question, and hybrid physics-informed designs are the most credible path forward (*high confidence*).** The rapid growth of machine learning across climate applications spans emulation, downscaling, bias correction, and the extraction of predictable structure (Kaack et al., 2022). In hydrology and other data-rich, process-complex domains, machine-learning models have demonstrated skill at predicting variables that depend on many interacting drivers, offering fast surrogates for expensive simulation (Hai et al., 2022). The promise for regional work is the resolution of the resolution-versus-ensemble tension: emulators can propagate scenario and internal-variability uncertainty across large ensembles at negligible marginal cost.

The caution is that regional projection is fundamentally an extrapolation problem, and purely data-driven methods carry no intrinsic guarantee of physical consistency or reliability beyond the range of their training. This matters most for the extremes and tipping-adjacent behaviour that dominate regional risk, precisely where training data are sparsest. The assessed best practice — training emulators on dynamical output so that physics is inherited, embedding conservation and consistency constraints, and evaluating against held-out extremes — redistributes rather than removes the need for physical modelling. Machine learning is therefore assessed as a powerful accelerant that raises, rather than lowers, the premium on rigorous physical evaluation.

### 7.7.3 Persistent limitations and research priorities

**The leading limitations of regional projection are the simulation of circulation-dependent change, the representation of compound and cascading events, and the sparse observational coverage of the most vulnerable regions, and these define the research priorities for the coming assessment cycle (*high confidence*).** Circulation remains the dominant source of regional uncertainty: the thermodynamic response to warming is comparatively robust, but the dynamic response — changes in jets, storm tracks, blocking, and monsoon circulation — is both uncertain and decisive for regional extremes (Rousi et al., 2022) (White et al., 2023). Reducing this uncertainty requires improved simulation of the large-scale circulation in the driving models, because no downscaling method can supply a dynamic signal the boundary conditions omit.

Compound and cascading events are the second priority. Their physical simulation requires correctly coupled processes and correctly represented dependence between variables, and their impact assessment requires carrying the physical cascade through to ecological, health, and socioeconomic consequences (Mora et al., 2022; Forzieri et al., 2022) (Simpson et al., 2021). The third priority is equity of information: the regions most exposed and most vulnerable — much of Africa, parts of Asia, small-island states — are frequently the least well observed and least well modelled, so that the distribution of scientific capacity is misaligned with the distribution of risk (Zittis et al., 2022; Rahman et al., 2022; Wing et al., 2022). Closing this gap, through observation, capacity-building, and co-production, is both a scientific and an equity imperative. Underlying all three priorities is the recognition that regional climate change is unfolding within a coupled Earth system whose boundaries are being transgressed and whose climate and biodiversity crises are entangled, so that regional projection cannot be credibly separated from the broader assessment of Earth-system state (Richardson et al., 2023; Rockström et al., 2023; Pörtner et al., 2023).

### 7.7.4 Synthesis

The regionalization of climate change has matured from a peripheral downscaling exercise into a central, methodologically diverse enterprise. The organizing patterns of the regional response — land-ocean contrast, Arctic amplification, elevation-dependent warming — are robustly established and physically understood (Rantanen et al., 2022; Pepin et al., 2022) (Rounce et al., 2023). The methods for generating regional information — dynamical, statistical, and machine-learning — each contribute distinctive strengths, and their reconciliation through distillation is now recognized as essential rather than optional (Simpson et al., 2021). The regional projections themselves show a consistent picture of intensifying heat, more volatile hydroclimate with accelerating flash drought, expanding wildfire, and rising coastal exposure, differentiated by the circulation, hydrology, and coastal geometry of each continent (Yuan et al., 2023) (Chiang et al., 2021; Nicholls et al., 2021) (Senande-Rivera et al., 2022). And the communication of this information has evolved toward storylines and the structured treatment of deep uncertainty, giving explicit weight to the compound, cascading, and low-likelihood high-impact outcomes that marginal probabilities alone would obscure (Wunderling et al., 2021) (Kemp et al., 2022). The central message of this chapter is that credible regional climate information is not read off a single model but constructed — through the disciplined integration of multiple methods, a strong observational foundation, and transparent expert judgment — into products that are fit for the decisions they must inform.

## 7.8 Regional Impact Sectors: Water, Food, Health, and Coasts

The value of regional climate projection is ultimately realized in the impact sectors that translate physical change into consequences for people and ecosystems. This section assesses the regional projection problem as it presents in four decisive sectors — water, food, health, and coasts — each of which imposes distinctive requirements on the physical information and each of which exhibits strong regional differentiation. The sectoral framing complements the geographic framing of Section 7.5 by making explicit the pathways through which the physical projections assessed above become the impacts that adaptation must address (Abbass et al., 2022).

### 7.8.1 The regional water cycle

**The regional water cycle is projected to become more variable, with simultaneous intensification of wet and dry extremes, accelerating flash-drought onset, and mounting pressure on groundwater buffers, though the sign of mean precipitation change remains uncertain in many regions (*high confidence*).** Water is the sector through which climate change is most immediately felt in most regions, and it is also the sector in which regional projection is hardest, because the water cycle integrates precipitation, evaporative demand, land-surface partitioning, and subsurface storage, each with its own uncertainty. The intensification of the atmospheric water-holding capacity with warming provides a robust thermodynamic tendency toward heavier precipitation extremes, but the regional distribution of that intensification depends on circulation changes that models simulate with lower confidence.

Drought is being reshaped in ways that carry detectable human influence. The frequency, duration, and intensity of drought show anthropogenic signals in the observational record (Chiang et al., 2021), and a global transition toward flash droughts — droughts that develop over weeks rather than seasons, driven by the rapid depletion of soil moisture under high evaporative demand — is projected under continued warming (Yuan et al., 2023). Flash droughts are especially consequential regionally because their speed of onset gives little warning, stressing agricultural and water-management systems designed around slower-developing events. The prediction of both floods and droughts remains a fundamental challenge because it demands accurate representation of the precipitation forcing, the land-surface response, and, in managed basins, the human operation of reservoirs and withdrawals (Brunner et al., 2021).

Groundwater is the hidden buffer that determines regional resilience to hydroclimate variability, and it is under growing pressure. Analysis of aquifers worldwide reveals rapid groundwater decline in many regions, with some cases of recovery where management has intervened, indicating that the trajectory is not fixed by the climate alone but is strongly modulated by human decisions (Jasechko et al., 2024). The broader role of groundwater in sustaining a resilient water future — buffering droughts, supporting irrigation, sustaining baseflow — makes its depletion a first-order regional concern that regional climate information must be integrated with to be decision-relevant (Scanlon et al., 2023). Pollution compounds scarcity: accounting for water quality alongside quantity substantially increases the number of river basins facing water scarcity, so that the regional water-security picture is worse than a quantity-only analysis would suggest (Wang et al., 2024). The hydrological reference frameworks that underpin all of this analysis, such as global water-resources-and-use models, are essential tools for translating the physical projection into basin-scale water balances (Schmied et al., 2021).

### 7.8.2 Regional food systems and agriculture

**Regional food systems face converging pressures from heat, water stress, shifting growing seasons, and soil degradation, with climate impacts on agriculture emerging earlier than previous model generations indicated and falling unevenly across regions (*high confidence*).** Agriculture is exquisitely sensitive to the regional expression of climate change, because crop yields respond to the timing and intensity of heat, the reliability of rainfall, and the state of soils, all of which vary sharply by region. A new generation of coupled climate and crop models indicates that the impacts of climate change on global agriculture emerge earlier than earlier assessments suggested, sharpening the near-term regional stakes and compressing the time available for adaptation (Jägermeyr et al., 2021).

The regional differentiation is stark. Across much of Asia, agricultural production is highly exposed to heat, water stress, and shifting monsoon timing, with implications for the food security of a large share of the world's population (Rahman et al., 2022). Soil salinization, driven by the interaction of a warming, drying climate with irrigation and evaporative concentration, threatens the productivity of irrigated croplands in arid and semi-arid regions and is projected to expand under continued warming (Hassani et al., 2021). Climate change also exacerbates the broader environmental impacts of agriculture, so that the sector is simultaneously a victim of and a contributor to regional environmental stress, complicating the design of adaptation that does not worsen other pressures (Yang et al., 2024). The coupling of climate change to biodiversity and food security is a global concern with acute regional manifestations, particularly where food systems depend on rain-fed agriculture and on the ecosystem services that a changing climate erodes (Muluneh, 2021).

Aquatic food systems add a marine and freshwater dimension to regional food security. Aquatic foods make a substantial contribution to nutrition in many regions, and their sensitivity to ocean warming, marine heatwaves, and changing productivity introduces a distinct regional vulnerability for coastal and island populations that depend on them (Golden et al., 2021). Marine heatwaves, with their severe and sometimes irreversible biological impacts on the ecosystems that sustain fisheries, are a growing regional hazard for these food systems (Smith et al., 2022). Harmful algal blooms, whose apparent global increase is at least partly attributable to intensified monitoring and emerging impacts, add a further regional water-quality and food-safety concern in affected coastal and inland waters (Hallegraeff et al., 2021). The protection and sustainable management of the ocean is assessed as a lever that can simultaneously advance regional food security, biodiversity, and climate goals where it is well designed (Sala et al., 2021).

### 7.8.3 Regional health burdens

**The health consequences of regional climate change are broad, compounding, and unevenly distributed, spanning heat mortality, infectious disease, mental health, and the health effects of food and water insecurity, with the burden concentrated among the most exposed and least resourced populations (*high confidence*).** Health is the sector in which the human meaning of regional physical change is most directly expressed. The annual assessments of the linkage between health and climate change document a widening array of regional health impacts and, increasingly, record-breaking threats associated with delayed action, underscoring that the health burden is not a distant projection but a present and growing regional reality (Romanello et al., 2023) (Romanello et al., 2024).

The mechanisms are diverse and often compounding. A majority of known human pathogenic diseases can be aggravated by climatic hazards, meaning that the physical regional projection cascades into a wide and interacting set of disease risks rather than a single clean signal (Mora et al., 2022). Heat extremes, whose regional intensification is assessed above, translate directly into heat morbidity and mortality, with the burden shaped by exposure, vulnerability, and adaptive capacity that vary sharply within and between regions. The mental-health dimension of climate change is increasingly recognized, encompassing the psychological consequences of extreme events, displacement, and chronic environmental stress, and it constitutes a regional health burden that conventional impact assessment has historically neglected (Charlson et al., 2021). Insect-mediated risks, including shifts in the distribution of disease vectors and agricultural pests, add a further ecological pathway from regional climate change to human welfare (Harvey et al., 2022).

The distributional character of the health burden is a defining feature. The same physical hazard produces very different health outcomes depending on the exposure and vulnerability of the affected population, so that regional health projection cannot be read off the physical climate alone but must integrate the socioeconomic context. This reinforces the complex-risk and distillation framings assessed above: health-relevant regional information is fit for purpose only when it carries the physical projection through to the exposed and vulnerable populations who bear the consequences (Simpson et al., 2021).

### 7.8.4 Coasts, rivers, and the built and natural environment

**Coastal and riverine regions face compounding pressures from sea-level rise, subsidence, changing extremes, and ecosystem degradation, with relative change at the coast and the state of freshwater ecosystems determining regional outcomes as much as the global-mean signals (*high confidence*).** For coastal regions, the decision-relevant quantity is relative sea-level change, which combines the climatic signal with local vertical land motion; in many densely populated deltas, subsidence dominates the local rise and coastal flood exposure is therefore a joint product of climate and local land management (Nicholls et al., 2021). Credible regional coastal assessment depends on accurate terrain models that remove the bias of forests and buildings (Hawker et al., 2022) and on global tide atlases that resolve the deterministic tidal component of total water level (Lyard et al., 2021).

Rivers and their ecosystems are a further regional frontier. River ecosystem metabolism and carbon biogeochemistry are being reshaped by warming and altered flow regimes, with consequences for water quality, carbon cycling, and the ecosystem services on which regional populations depend (Battin et al., 2023). The regional water cycle, the food system, and the coastal zone are thus linked through the river network, so that a change in one propagates to the others. Human infrastructure modulates these regional outcomes: dams and reservoirs can reduce downstream flood exposure under a changing climate, though the benefits are unevenly distributed and depend on operation (Boulangé et al., 2021), and the same infrastructure that buffers floods can alter sediment delivery and exacerbate the subsidence that drives relative sea-level rise at the coast. The regional picture that emerges across these sectors is one of tightly coupled physical, ecological, and human systems, in which credible projection requires integrating the physical climate information assessed throughout this chapter with the sectoral and socioeconomic context that determines actual regional consequences (Abbass et al., 2022) (Simpson et al., 2021).

## 7.9 Regional Detection, Attribution, and Monitoring

### 7.9.1 Detecting the regional signal

**The anthropogenic signal is now detectable in an expanding set of regional variables and extremes, and detection at the regional scale is a prerequisite for the confident projection of regional futures (*high confidence*).** Detection asks whether a change has occurred that is inconsistent with internal variability alone; attribution asks whether that change can be ascribed to a specified cause. Both are harder at the regional than at the global scale, because the signal-to-noise ratio is lower — regional internal variability is large relative to the forced signal — and because the observational record is shorter and sparser for many regions and variables. Where detection succeeds regionally, it both confirms that the physical understanding is adequate and constrains the projection, because a model that reproduces the detected trend is more credible for the future.

Detection has advanced most clearly for temperature and for temperature-driven extremes. Arctic amplification is detectable as a robust departure from the global trend and is inconsistent with unforced variability, providing one of the strongest regional detection results in the climate system (Rantanen et al., 2022). Elevation-dependent warming is detectable across many mountain ranges, though the sparse high-elevation observational network limits confidence in the precise vertical structure (Pepin et al., 2022). Detection of hydroclimate change is more difficult but is succeeding for drought: the frequency, duration, and intensity of drought carry a detectable anthropogenic signal in the observational record, a result that both confirms the physical mechanism and sharpens the projection of future regional drought (Chiang et al., 2021). The emerging southwestern North American megadrought is detectable as a departure from the range of natural variability reconstructed from paleoclimate, with anthropogenic warming assessed as a substantial contributor (Williams et al., 2022).

### 7.9.2 Event attribution at the regional scale

**Event attribution has matured into a routine tool that quantifies the human influence on the likelihood and intensity of specific regional extremes, providing the empirical anchor for storyline framings and for public communication (*high confidence*).** Where classical detection and attribution address long-term trends, event attribution addresses individual extremes — a specific heatwave, flood, or drought — asking how human influence has changed the probability or magnitude of an event of that kind. The approach has become sufficiently established that attribution assessments are now produced rapidly after major events, connecting the abstract statistics of climate change to concrete, experienced episodes (Clarke et al., 2022).

Several recent regional extremes illustrate the method and its findings. The 2021 Pacific Northwest heatwave was so far outside the prior observed range that it challenged conventional extreme-value approaches, and its attribution required careful treatment of the physical ingredients rather than a simple extrapolation of the historical distribution (White et al., 2023). The acceleration of western European heat, linked to more-persistent double-jet states, is a case where attribution must separate the thermodynamic contribution of warming from the dynamic contribution of circulation change, a separation that is central to the storyline approach (Rousi et al., 2022). Attribution of changing wildfire activity connects the physical fire-weather trend to the human influence on temperature and aridity, supporting the assessment that fire seasons are lengthening and intensifying across many regions (Senande-Rivera et al., 2022). The value of event attribution for regional assessment is twofold: it provides quantitative constraints on how specific hazards are changing, and it supplies the communicable, event-anchored framing that makes regional risk tangible to the public and to decision-makers.

### 7.9.3 Monitoring, early warning, and the regional information chain

**Sustained regional monitoring and early-warning systems are the operational counterpart to projection, and the credibility of regional information depends on the continuity and coverage of the observing systems that underpin both (*high confidence*).** Projection and monitoring are complementary: projection anticipates the changing distribution of hazards, while monitoring detects their realization in near-real time and triggers response. Both depend on the same observational foundation, and gaps in that foundation degrade both. The under-observed regions — much of the tropics, the high mountains, the polar regions — are precisely those where projection is least certain and where monitoring is most needed, so the observational gap compounds across the information chain.

Advances in the observing system are steadily closing parts of this gap. Intensive field campaigns in under-sampled regimes, such as the coordinated observation of the central Arctic across a full annual cycle, supply the process-level constraints that improve both models and monitoring (Shupe et al., 2022). Specialized global datasets — soil properties, terrain, tides, hydrological state — extend consistent monitoring to variables and regions that conventional networks miss (Poggio et al., 2021) (Hawker et al., 2022) (Lyard et al., 2021) (Schmied et al., 2021). The characterization of regional aerosol and dust processes, including the contribution of the world's principal dust-source regions, improves the monitoring of a driver that materially affects regional radiation and precipitation (Kok et al., 2021). Even the detection of far-field pollution signatures, such as plastic reaching the remote Arctic, is a form of monitoring that reveals the connectivity of the regional system to the global whole (Bergmann et al., 2022).

The regional information chain thus runs from observation, through detection and attribution, through model evaluation and projection, through distillation, to communication and, ultimately, to monitoring and response. Each link depends on the others, and the weakest link — most often the observational coverage of the most vulnerable regions — sets the overall credibility. Strengthening regional climate information is therefore not primarily a matter of higher model resolution but of investing across the whole chain: in observation, in process understanding, in the reconciliation of methods, and in the co-production and communication of information that is fit for the decisions it must inform (Simpson et al., 2021). This integrated view is the organizing conclusion of the chapter: credible regional climate change assessment is a system, not a single output, and its improvement requires attention to every part of that system.

## 7.10 Conclusions

**Regional climate projection has become a mature, methodologically plural, and decision-oriented science, and its central lesson is that credible regional information is constructed from many lines of evidence rather than read from any single model (*high confidence*).** The chapter has assessed the full arc from global signal to regional decision. The organizing patterns of the regional response are robust and physically understood, with Arctic amplification and elevation-dependent warming the clearest examples (Rantanen et al., 2022) (Pepin et al., 2022). The methods for generating regional information — dynamical downscaling with its convection-permitting frontier, statistical downscaling with its efficiency and its stationarity caveats, and machine-learning emulation with its computational promise and its extrapolation risks — are complementary, and their reconciliation through distillation is the step that turns raw output into usable knowledge (Tebaldi et al., 2021) (Simpson et al., 2021).

The regional projections themselves converge on a coherent picture. Heat extremes intensify, often faster than mean warming where circulation change amplifies them (Rousi et al., 2022) (White et al., 2023). The water cycle becomes more volatile, with accelerating flash drought, detectable anthropogenic drought trends, and mounting pressure on groundwater buffers (Yuan et al., 2023) (Chiang et al., 2021) (Jasechko et al., 2024). Wildfire expands, forests lose resilience, and the Amazon shows signs of approaching a regional tipping threshold (Senande-Rivera et al., 2022) (Forzieri et al., 2022) (Boulton et al., 2022). Coasts face compounding sea-level rise and subsidence, and the burden across every sector falls most heavily on the exposed and the vulnerable (Nicholls et al., 2021) (Wing et al., 2022).

The communication of this information has evolved toward storylines and the explicit treatment of deep uncertainty, giving proper weight to compound, cascading, and low-likelihood high-impact outcomes that marginal probabilities alone would hide (Wunderling et al., 2021) (Kemp et al., 2022). The overarching conclusion is that regional climate assessment is an information system spanning observation, detection and attribution, evaluation, projection, distillation, communication, and monitoring, and that its weakest link — most often the observational coverage of the most vulnerable regions — sets its overall credibility. Strengthening regional information is a task for the whole system, and it is inseparable from the broader assessment of an Earth system whose boundaries are being transgressed and whose climate and biodiversity crises are entangled (Richardson et al., 2023) (Pörtner et al., 2023).
