# Chapter 8: Tipping Points and Abrupt Change

## Executive Summary

**The climate system contains a set of large-scale components — tipping elements — that can undergo self-sustaining, and in some cases effectively irreversible, transitions once forcing crosses a critical threshold; several of these elements show observational signals consistent with declining stability as global warming proceeds (*high confidence*).** The scientific basis for treating abrupt and irreversible change as a distinct assessment category rests on the recognition that the responses of ice sheets, the ocean overturning circulation, permafrost, tropical and boreal forests, and warm-water coral reefs to sustained forcing are not always smooth, reversible, or proportionate to the forcing applied (*high confidence*) (Folke, 2021) (Richardson, 2023).

**Warm-water coral reef systems are assessed to be the tipping element most imminently at risk, with the biological impacts of marine heatwaves already driving mass mortality events at present levels of warming (*high confidence*).** Marine heatwaves have intensified in frequency, duration, and severity, and their ecological consequences — including bleaching, disease, and range shifts — are documented across ocean basins (Smith, 2022).

**The Amazon forest system shows a pronounced loss of resilience since the early 2000s, with a rising fraction of the biome exhibiting the statistical signatures of critical slowing down; a combination of warming, drought, deforestation, and fire could drive large areas past a threshold into a degraded, lower-biomass state (*medium confidence*).** Observational analyses of vegetation reveal declining recovery rates from perturbation, and process-based assessment identifies self-amplifying dieback mechanisms (Boulton, 2022) (Flores, 2024) (Forzieri, 2022).

**Permafrost constitutes a large and vulnerable carbon reservoir whose thaw releases carbon dioxide and methane, producing a positive feedback on warming that is expected to be gradual and pervasive rather than a single global-scale abrupt transition, though abrupt thaw processes can dominate locally (*high confidence*).** The magnitude of permafrost carbon feedback remains a significant source of uncertainty in the assessment of committed warming (Miner, 2022).

**Terrestrial ecosystems more broadly show emerging, spatially coherent signals of declining resilience under climate change, with hotter droughts producing tree mortality across a wide range of forest biomes (*high confidence*).** These signals of destabilization in the land carbon sink have implications for the trajectory of atmospheric carbon dioxide and for the stability of forest-related tipping elements (Forzieri, 2022) (Hammond, 2022) (McDowell, 2022) (Frenne, 2021).

**Early-warning indicators grounded in the theory of critical slowing down — rising temporal autocorrelation and variance in system state variables — provide a physically motivated but not infallible basis for detecting approach to a tipping threshold; their interpretation is complicated by observational limitations, confounding trends, and the possibility of tipping without prior warning (*medium confidence*).** Where long, high-quality records exist, such indicators have been detected in the Amazon and in forest resilience metrics (Boulton, 2022) (Flores, 2024) (Forzieri, 2022).

**The paleoclimate record documents repeated episodes of abrupt regional and hemispheric change and demonstrates that Earth's climate has occupied radically different states, providing evidence that the system is capable of nonlinear reorganization on timescales far shorter than the forcing that triggers it (*high confidence*).** The long-term reconstruction of continental configuration, sea level, and climate confirms the range of states the Earth system can occupy (Scotese, 2021).

**Tipping elements do not operate in isolation: interactions among them, and between climatic and non-climatic stresses, can produce cascades and systemic risk that amplify impacts beyond the sum of individual components, and that couple the climate crisis to biodiversity loss and to broader societal shocks (*medium confidence*).** The assessment of interacting risks, cascades, and polycrisis dynamics is an area of rapid development and remains subject to substantial uncertainty (Lawrence, 2024) (Pörtner, 2023) (Simpson, 2021) (Galaz, 2021).

**Because low-likelihood, high-impact outcomes carry disproportionate societal consequences, a comprehensive risk assessment must weight them despite their deep uncertainty; the possibility of catastrophic or, in the extreme, globally destabilizing outcomes is not excluded by current evidence and warrants dedicated study (*high confidence* in the value of assessing such outcomes).** Catastrophe-focused analysis argues that the tail of the climate risk distribution has been comparatively neglected and deserves systematic assessment (Kemp, 2022) (Richardson, 2023).

## 8.1 Concepts, Definitions, and the Assessment Framework for Abrupt Change

### 8.1.1 What distinguishes a tipping point from ordinary climate change

**A tipping point is a critical threshold in a forcing or in a system state beyond which a component of the Earth system reorganizes into a qualitatively different state, often through self-sustaining internal feedbacks and frequently with limited reversibility on policy-relevant timescales; this behaviour is distinct from the smooth, broadly reversible response that characterizes most of the climate system (*high confidence*).** The defining features of a tipping element are threshold behaviour, the dominance of positive (self-amplifying) internal feedbacks once the threshold is crossed, and hysteresis — the property that reversing the forcing does not straightforwardly reverse the transition. These features together mean that the trajectory of an affected component cannot be inferred from the forcing alone, and that the timing and even the occurrence of a transition may carry deep and in some respects irreducible uncertainty.

This chapter uses the term *abrupt change* to denote a transition that is fast relative to the forcing that causes it — one in which the rate of change of the system substantially exceeds the rate of change of the driver over some interval — and reserves *tipping point* for the more specific case in which such a transition is driven by internal feedbacks past a threshold and is difficult to reverse. Not all abrupt changes are tipping-point phenomena, and not all tipping-point transitions are fast: some, such as the long-term response of the great ice sheets, may commit the system to a new state over centuries to millennia even where the threshold that determines the eventual outcome is crossed quickly. The assessment therefore separates the questions of *whether* a threshold is crossed, *how fast* the resulting transition unfolds, and *how reversible* the outcome is, because these can differ markedly among tipping elements.

The scientific significance of tipping behaviour is that it violates the tacit assumption of proportionality that underlies much of climate impact assessment. Where a system responds proportionately, a given increment of warming produces a broadly predictable increment of impact, and the marginal consequence of an additional increment is similar to the last. Where a system exhibits threshold behaviour, the marginal consequence of an additional increment of warming can rise sharply and discontinuously in the neighbourhood of the threshold, so that a small additional forcing produces a disproportionate and possibly unrecoverable change. This nonlinearity is the central reason that abrupt and irreversible change is assessed as a distinct category of climate risk rather than folded into the assessment of gradual change (Folke, 2021).

### 8.1.2 The planetary context: boundaries, stability, and the Anthropocene biosphere

**The concept of tipping elements is embedded within a broader framing of the Earth system as operating within boundaries beyond which the risk of large-scale, potentially abrupt and irreversible change increases; current assessments place several of these boundaries in a zone of elevated risk (*high confidence*).** The planetary-boundaries framework identifies a set of biophysical processes — including climate change, biosphere integrity, land-system change, freshwater, and biogeochemical flows — that regulate the stability and resilience of the Earth system, and argues that transgression of these boundaries raises the likelihood of pushing the system out of the relatively stable state that has characterized the recent geological past (Richardson, 2023). The most recent quantification concludes that six of nine boundaries have been transgressed, which the authors interpret as a substantial and rising pressure on Earth-system stability (Richardson, 2023).

This framing matters for the assessment of tipping points in two ways. First, it locates individual tipping elements within an interconnected biosphere whose overall resilience — its capacity to absorb disturbance and reorganize while retaining function — is itself being eroded (Folke, 2021). A biosphere under multiple simultaneous pressures is, all else equal, more prone to threshold behaviour in any one of its components, because the buffering capacity that would otherwise damp perturbations is reduced. Second, it draws attention to the coupling between the climate system and the living systems that both respond to and regulate climate, a coupling that is central to the tipping behaviour of forests, reefs, and the carbon cycle (Pörtner, 2023). The assessment of tipping points is therefore not a catalogue of independent switches but an evaluation of the stability of an integrated, and increasingly stressed, Earth system (Folke, 2021) (Richardson, 2023).

### 8.1.3 Confidence, likelihood, and the treatment of deep uncertainty

**The assessment of tipping points requires calibrated treatment of two distinct kinds of uncertainty: uncertainty about whether and when a threshold will be crossed, and uncertainty about the consequences that would follow; for many tipping elements the evidence base supports high confidence in the qualitative existence of threshold behaviour but only low to medium confidence in the precise threshold value (*high confidence* in this characterization).** Throughout this chapter, calibrated confidence language expresses the degree of evidence and agreement underpinning a finding, and likelihood language, where used, expresses a probabilistic assessment of an outcome. A recurring feature of the tipping-point literature is that these two can diverge sharply: there may be high confidence that a component of the system possesses a tipping threshold in principle, combined with genuinely deep uncertainty about the level of warming at which that threshold lies and about the timescale over which a transition would unfold.

This divergence has a direct methodological consequence. For processes governed by well-observed physics and constrained by instrumental and paleoclimate data, the assessment can express findings in probabilistic terms. For processes in which the governing feedbacks are understood qualitatively but poorly constrained quantitatively — which describes several major tipping elements — the assessment must instead rely on lines of evidence such as process understanding, analogues from the paleorecord, and emerging statistical indicators of destabilization, none of which alone yields a calibrated probability of crossing a threshold. In such cases the honest expression of the state of knowledge is a statement of the physical plausibility and potential consequences of a transition, accompanied by an explicit acknowledgment that the probability cannot be reliably quantified. This is not a weakness of the assessment but a faithful representation of a system in which some of the most consequential outcomes are also the most deeply uncertain (Kemp, 2022).

The treatment of deep uncertainty also shapes how this chapter handles the tail of the risk distribution. A finding that a catastrophic outcome cannot be assigned a precise probability is emphatically not a finding that its probability is negligible. Because the consequences of crossing certain thresholds would be severe and, in some cases, effectively permanent on human timescales, the expected-impact contribution of low-likelihood outcomes can be large even when their probability is small, and a risk assessment that omits them because they are hard to quantify systematically understates the total risk (Kemp, 2022) (Richardson, 2023). The framework adopted here therefore treats the assessment of low-likelihood, high-impact outcomes as a required component of a complete tipping-point assessment rather than as an optional appendix.

### 8.1.4 The risk framing: hazard, exposure, vulnerability, and cascading pathways

**Tipping-point risk is most usefully assessed not as a property of the climate hazard alone but as the product of hazard, the exposure of people and systems, and their vulnerability, evaluated along pathways that may involve interacting and compounding drivers (*high confidence*).** A framework for complex climate-change risk assessment recognizes that the consequences of a given physical change depend on how that change intersects with exposed populations, infrastructure, and ecosystems, and on the pre-existing vulnerabilities that determine whether a hazard becomes a disaster (Simpson, 2021). Applied to tipping points, this framing shifts the assessment question from "how likely is the threshold to be crossed" alone to "what would the crossing of the threshold mean for the systems that depend on the affected component, and through what pathways would the consequences propagate."

This complex-risk framing is essential because the impacts of a tipping transition rarely remain confined to the physical component that tips. A reorganization of a major ocean current, a large release of permafrost carbon, or a dieback of a continental-scale forest would propagate through climatic teleconnections, through the carbon cycle, and through human systems of food, water, health, and settlement, with consequences that can compound and cascade (Simpson, 2021) (Pörtner, 2023). The complex-risk perspective also foregrounds the interaction of climatic hazards with non-climatic stresses — pollution, land-use change, biodiversity loss, and socio-economic fragility — that can lower the threshold at which a component tips and raise the severity of the resulting impact (Naidu, 2021) (Pörtner, 2023). The remainder of this chapter is organized around this framing: it first assesses the individual tipping elements and their thresholds, then the tools for detecting approach to a threshold, and finally the interactions, cascades, and tail risks that determine the full societal significance of abrupt and irreversible change.

## 8.2 The Major Tipping Elements

### 8.2.1 The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation

**The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) is assessed to possess threshold behaviour arising from a positive salinity-advection feedback, such that a sufficient input of freshwater to the North Atlantic could weaken or collapse the overturning with large climatic consequences for the Atlantic basin and beyond; there is high confidence that the AMOC will weaken over the coming century and low confidence in the level of forcing that would trigger an abrupt collapse (*medium confidence* overall).** The physical basis for treating the AMOC as a tipping element is well established: the circulation is maintained in part by the sinking of cold, salty water in the subpolar North Atlantic, and this sinking is self-reinforcing because the northward transport of salt sustains the density contrast that drives the sinking. A large enough freshening of the surface waters — from ice-sheet melt, increased precipitation, or altered runoff — can weaken the sinking, which reduces salt transport, which further weakens the sinking, a feedback that in idealized models admits an abrupt transition to a much weaker or collapsed state.

The consequences of a substantial AMOC weakening would be far-reaching. They include a pronounced cooling of the North Atlantic region relative to the warming trend elsewhere, shifts in the position of tropical rainfall belts with implications for water availability across several continents, changes in regional sea level along Atlantic coasts, and altered patterns of ocean carbon and heat uptake. Because the overturning circulation redistributes heat on a hemispheric scale, its reorganization is one of the clearest examples of a tipping element whose impacts would propagate far from the site of the transition itself, coupling the North Atlantic to remote regions through atmospheric and oceanic teleconnections. The assessment of AMOC risk is thus inseparable from the assessment of cascading interactions treated later in this chapter.

The central difficulty in assessing AMOC risk is quantitative. There is broad agreement on the qualitative existence of the salinity-advection feedback and on the direction of the forced response, but the freshwater threshold that would separate a weakened-but-stable circulation from an irreversible collapse is poorly constrained by observations, and different classes of model disagree on how close the present circulation lies to such a threshold. The instrumental record of the overturning is short relative to the timescales of interest, which limits the power of statistical early-warning approaches when applied to the AMOC directly. The honest assessment is therefore one of high confidence in continued weakening under sustained forcing, genuine physical plausibility of an abrupt transition, and deep uncertainty about the probability and timing of such a transition — a combination that places the AMOC firmly within the class of low-likelihood, high-impact outcomes that a complete risk assessment cannot set aside (Kemp, 2022).

### 8.2.2 The polar ice sheets and committed sea-level change

**The Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets are assessed to possess thresholds beyond which large-scale and effectively irreversible mass loss becomes self-sustaining, committing the Earth system to multi-metre sea-level rise over centuries to millennia even if warming is subsequently reversed (*high confidence* in the existence of such thresholds; *low confidence* in their precise values).** The ice sheets exhibit several distinct self-amplifying feedbacks that give them the character of tipping elements. For the Greenland Ice Sheet, the surface-elevation–melt feedback is central: as the ice-sheet surface lowers through melting, it descends into warmer air at lower altitude, which increases melting further, so that beyond a threshold in sustained warming the ice sheet cannot maintain its elevation and enters a state of ongoing, self-reinforcing decline. For marine-based sectors of the West Antarctic Ice Sheet, the marine-ice-sheet instability associated with beds that deepen inland can allow grounding-line retreat to become self-sustaining once initiated.

The defining risk characteristic of the ice sheets is the combination of high commitment and long timescale. Crossing a threshold does not produce rapid sea-level rise within years; rather, it commits the system to a trajectory of loss that plays out over centuries to millennia and that cannot be halted by returning temperatures to their earlier values, because the feedbacks that sustain the loss depend on the altered geometry of the ice rather than on the instantaneous forcing. This hysteresis means that the sea-level consequences of decisions taken in the present may be, to a substantial degree, locked in long before they are fully expressed. For the assessment of exposure and vulnerability, the relevant implication is that coastal risk from the ice sheets is governed less by the rate of change observed in any given decade than by the cumulative warming that determines whether irreversible thresholds are crossed at all.

As with the AMOC, the principal uncertainty is the value of the threshold rather than its existence. Paleoclimate evidence that sea level has stood many metres higher than at present during past warm intervals confirms that the ice sheets can occupy dramatically reduced configurations, and reconstruction of the long-term history of sea level across geological time provides context for the magnitude of change of which the system is capable (Scotese, 2021). What the paleorecord cannot by itself provide is a precise mapping from a given level of warming to a given rate of loss under present-day rates of forcing, which are without close analogue in the reconstructed past. The assessment therefore expresses high confidence in eventual large sea-level commitment under sustained high warming and correspondingly lower confidence in the timing and the precise threshold.

### 8.2.3 Permafrost and the frozen-carbon feedback

**Permafrost soils store a large quantity of organic carbon that becomes vulnerable to microbial decomposition and release as carbon dioxide and methane upon thaw, producing a positive feedback on global warming that is assessed to be substantial, pervasive, and — for the diffuse gradual-thaw component — more accurately characterized as an accelerating amplifier than as a single global abrupt tipping point, though localized abrupt-thaw processes can release carbon rapidly (*high confidence*).** The permafrost region holds one of the largest reservoirs of potentially labile carbon in the Earth system, accumulated over millennia in frozen soils across the Arctic and boreal zones. As these soils warm and thaw, previously protected organic matter becomes available to decomposition, and the resulting emissions of carbon dioxide and, from waterlogged anaerobic settings, methane add to atmospheric greenhouse-gas concentrations and thereby amplify the warming that drove the thaw (Miner, 2022).

It is important to characterize the permafrost feedback precisely, because it differs in structure from the switch-like tipping elements. Across most of the permafrost domain, thaw and the associated carbon release are expected to proceed gradually and pervasively as the climate warms, producing a feedback that grows with warming rather than a single threshold beyond which the entire reservoir is released. Superimposed on this gradual process, however, are abrupt-thaw phenomena — including the collapse of ice-rich ground and the rapid development of thaw lakes and thermokarst — that can mobilize deep carbon far faster than gradual thaw and that give parts of the system a genuinely abrupt local character. The net feedback is therefore the sum of a large, spatially extensive gradual component and a smaller but locally intense abrupt component, and both add carbon to the atmosphere in a way that is not reversible on human timescales once released (Miner, 2022).

The permafrost feedback is a leading source of uncertainty in the assessment of committed warming. The size of the vulnerable carbon pool, the fraction that will decompose, the partition between carbon dioxide and the far more potent methane, and the rate at which release proceeds are all imperfectly constrained, and the feedback is incompletely represented in many Earth-system models. Because permafrost emissions are effectively irreversible additions to the atmospheric burden, and because they act to raise the warming that stresses every other tipping element, the permafrost feedback occupies a central place in the assessment of both committed change and interacting risks (Miner, 2022).

### 8.2.4 The Amazon forest and tropical forest dieback

**The Amazon forest system is assessed to be capable of a large-scale transition from closed-canopy forest to a degraded, lower-biomass, more open state, driven by the interaction of warming, drought, deforestation, and fire, and reinforced by the forest's own influence on regional rainfall; observational evidence indicates a pronounced loss of resilience since the early 2000s over a large fraction of the biome (*medium confidence*).** The Amazon functions in part as a self-maintaining system: the forest recycles a substantial share of its own precipitation through evapotranspiration, so that a sufficiently large reduction in forest cover — whether from clearing, drought-driven mortality, or fire — can reduce the moisture supply to the remaining forest and push it toward a drier, fire-prone, savanna-like state. This moisture-recycling feedback is the physical basis for treating the Amazon as a tipping element, and it means that the forest's stability depends not only on external climate forcing but on the integrity of the forest itself (Flores, 2024).

The observational case for declining Amazon resilience has strengthened considerably. Analyses of vegetation state reveal that recovery rates from perturbation have slowed across more than three-quarters of the biome since the early 2000s, a signature consistent with critical slowing down as the system loses its capacity to return to its prior state after disturbance, and this loss of resilience is most pronounced in areas closer to human activity and with less rainfall (Boulton, 2022). Process-based synthesis identifies the principal drivers and stressors — rising temperatures, extreme drought, deforestation, and fire — and maps the compound thresholds beyond which large areas of the forest could transition, while emphasizing that the response is likely to be spatially heterogeneous rather than a single basin-wide switch (Flores, 2024). Underlying these system-level signals are the physiological mechanisms of tree mortality under drought and heat, which have been documented across forest biomes and which supply the process-level explanation for how a warming, drying climate translates into forest loss (Hammond, 2022) (McDowell, 2022).

The Amazon case also illustrates the coupling of a tipping element to broader Earth-system stability. Loss of Amazon forest would release stored carbon, reducing a globally important carbon sink and adding to the atmospheric burden, while altering regional climate in ways that would feed back on agriculture and water resources across South America (Flores, 2024) (Pörtner, 2023). Because the drivers are a mixture of global climate change and direct human land use, the Amazon is among the clearest examples of a tipping element whose threshold can be lowered by non-climatic pressures, so that reducing deforestation and fire raises the level of warming the forest can withstand before tipping (Flores, 2024). The assessment expresses medium confidence in the possibility of large-scale transition, high confidence in the observed loss of resilience, and continuing uncertainty about the precise combination of warming and land-use change that would trigger an irreversible outcome.

### 8.2.5 Warm-water coral reefs

**Warm-water coral reef ecosystems are assessed to be the tipping element closest to a threshold at present levels of warming, with marine heatwaves already driving mass bleaching and mortality and with the prospect of functional collapse of many reef systems within the range of near-term warming (*high confidence*).** Reef-building corals live close to their upper thermal limits, and sustained anomalous warmth causes them to expel their symbiotic algae — bleaching — which, if prolonged or repeated, leads to coral death. The biological impacts of marine heatwaves extend beyond bleaching to include disease outbreaks, altered species interactions, and shifts in the geographic distribution of marine organisms, and these impacts have been documented across ocean basins as marine heatwaves have grown more frequent, longer, and more intense (Smith, 2022).

The reef case is distinctive among tipping elements in that the transition is already observable rather than prospective. Rather than a single global threshold to be crossed in the future, warm-water reefs are experiencing a progression of increasingly severe and frequent thermal-stress events that erode their capacity to recover between disturbances, in a manner directly analogous to the resilience-loss dynamics seen in forests: as the interval between damaging events shortens relative to the recovery time, the system loses the ability to return to its prior state and shifts toward a degraded configuration dominated by non-reef-building organisms (Smith, 2022). The consequences of widespread reef degradation extend to the hundreds of millions of people who depend on reefs for coastal protection, fisheries, and livelihoods, making warm-water corals an element in which high hazard, high exposure, and high vulnerability coincide (Smith, 2022) (Pörtner, 2023).

### 8.2.6 Boreal forests, drylands, and the wider set of vulnerable biomes

**Beyond the classic tipping elements, a wider set of terrestrial systems — boreal forests, temperate and tropical woodlands, and dryland vegetation — show emerging, spatially coherent signals of declining resilience, indicating that destabilization of the land biosphere is not confined to the Amazon and that the land carbon sink itself may be at risk (*high confidence*).** A satellite-based analysis of forest resilience finds that a large fraction of the world's forests has experienced a decline in the capacity to recover from perturbation since the early 2000s, with the strongest signals in the tropics, in arid regions, and where forests are exposed to greater climatic water stress; this represents an emerging, coherent signal of destabilization across biomes rather than an isolated regional phenomenon (Forzieri, 2022). Global field observations of tree die-off reveal a consistent "hotter-drought" fingerprint, in which the combination of high temperature and moisture deficit drives mortality across a wide range of forest types (Hammond, 2022).

The mechanisms linking climate to woody-plant mortality — hydraulic failure and carbon starvation under the combined stress of drought, rising carbon dioxide, and increasing vapour-pressure deficit — provide the process-level basis for expecting these resilience declines to continue as warming proceeds (McDowell, 2022). At finer scales, the buffering role of forest microclimates, which can decouple understorey conditions from the warming of the free atmosphere, modulates the exposure of forest biodiversity to macroclimatic change and is itself sensitive to canopy integrity, so that the loss of canopy cover both reflects and accelerates the loss of resilience (Frenne, 2021). Taken together, these lines of evidence indicate that the stability of the terrestrial biosphere as a carbon sink and as a regulator of regional climate is being eroded across multiple biomes, with implications for the trajectory of atmospheric carbon dioxide and for the interacting risks assessed later in this chapter (Forzieri, 2022) (Hammond, 2022) (McDowell, 2022) (Frenne, 2021).

## 8.3 Early-Warning Signals and the Detection of Approaching Thresholds

### 8.3.1 The theory of critical slowing down

**A system approaching a tipping threshold is expected, on theoretical grounds, to recover more slowly from perturbations, and this critical slowing down manifests as measurable increases in the temporal autocorrelation and variance of the system's state, providing a generic and physically motivated basis for early-warning indicators (*high confidence* in the theory; *medium confidence* in its practical reliability).** The theoretical foundation of early-warning signals is that, as a system nears a bifurcation at which its current state loses stability, the rate at which it returns to equilibrium after a disturbance declines toward zero. This slowing of recovery has observable statistical consequences: because the system takes longer to relax, successive states become more similar to one another, so that lag-one autocorrelation rises, and because perturbations are damped less effectively, the variance of the state increases. These indicators are generic in the sense that they do not depend on knowing the detailed mechanism of the impending transition, which makes them appealing for systems whose governing feedbacks are understood only qualitatively.

The appeal of generic indicators is tempered by important limitations that the assessment must weigh. Critical slowing down is a property of a specific class of transitions and is not guaranteed to precede every abrupt change; some transitions can occur without detectable prior slowing, for instance when a system is subjected to a rapid shock rather than a slow approach to a threshold, or when the noise driving the system is itself changing. Conversely, rising autocorrelation and variance can arise from causes unrelated to an approaching bifurcation, such as changes in the observing system, in the forcing, or in the statistical properties of the driving noise, which creates a risk of false alarms. The reliable application of early-warning methods therefore requires long, high-quality, consistently sampled records, careful removal of confounding trends, and, ideally, corroboration from process understanding — conditions that are met for some tipping elements and not for others.

### 8.3.2 Resilience indicators applied to real systems

**Where sufficiently long and spatially resolved observational records exist, resilience-based indicators have been successfully applied and have revealed coherent, large-scale declines in stability, most notably in the Amazon forest and in global forest resilience more generally (*high confidence*).** The strongest empirical demonstration of early-warning methods in the climate system to date comes from vegetation remote sensing. Analysis of the Amazon finds that the recovery rate of vegetation from perturbation has declined across more than three-quarters of the biome since the early 2000s — precisely the signature that critical-slowing-down theory predicts for a system losing resilience — and that this decline is spatially organized, being strongest where rainfall is lower and human pressure is greater (Boulton, 2022). The interpretation of this signal is reinforced by its consistency with independent process-based understanding of Amazon dieback, which supplies a mechanism for the loss of resilience rather than leaving it as an unexplained statistical artefact (Flores, 2024).

The same methodological approach applied at global scale reveals that declining resilience is not confined to the Amazon. A satellite-based analysis of forests worldwide finds emerging signals of declining resilience, measured through the same class of temporal-autocorrelation indicators, across a large fraction of the global forest area, with the strongest declines in the tropics and in water-limited regions (Forzieri, 2022). The convergence of these independent analyses — different regions, overlapping methods, consistent theoretical basis, and corroborating process understanding — represents one of the more robust applications of early-warning theory in Earth-system science and lends weight to the interpretation that multiple biosphere components are losing stability under climate change (Boulton, 2022) (Forzieri, 2022) (Flores, 2024).

### 8.3.3 Limits of detection and the possibility of surprise

**Early-warning indicators are most reliable for slowly forced systems with long observational records and are least reliable precisely for some of the highest-consequence tipping elements, so the assessment must treat the absence of a detected warning signal as weak evidence of safety rather than as reassurance (*high confidence*).** The tipping elements for which early-warning methods work best — forests and reefs observed by satellite over two decades — are not, in general, the elements whose transitions would be most catastrophic. For the ocean overturning circulation and the ice sheets, the observational records that would be needed to apply resilience indicators with confidence are short relative to the timescales of the underlying dynamics, the systems are forced by processes that are themselves changing, and the relevant state variables are difficult to observe directly and continuously. In these cases the statistical power to detect an approaching threshold is limited, and a failure to detect a warning signal cannot be interpreted as evidence that no threshold is being approached.

This asymmetry has a direct bearing on how the assessment treats surprise. The prudent inference from the theory and the observations together is that early-warning signals, where present and well corroborated, add valuable evidence that a system is losing resilience, but that their absence does not license the conclusion that a transition is far off. Because some transitions can occur with little or no prior statistical warning, and because the systems most capable of such surprise are among the most consequential, the possibility of abrupt change arriving faster or at lower forcing than anticipated remains an essential element of the risk assessment (Kemp, 2022). Early-warning science strengthens the case for concern where signals appear, but it does not provide a reliable all-clear where they do not.

## 8.4 Cascading Interactions and Systemic Risk

### 8.4.1 Interactions among tipping elements

**Tipping elements are physically coupled through the ocean, atmosphere, and carbon cycle, and these couplings can be mutually reinforcing, so that the destabilization or transition of one element can raise the probability of transition in another and, in principle, propagate as a cascade (*medium confidence*).** The tipping elements assessed in this chapter are not independent switches but components of a single coupled system, and the physical connections among them are numerous and, in several cases, self-amplifying. A weakening of the Atlantic overturning circulation would alter heat and moisture transport in ways that bear on the water balance of tropical forests; permafrost thaw and forest dieback would each add carbon to the atmosphere and thereby raise the warming that stresses every other element; and changes in ice-sheet melt would deliver freshwater to the North Atlantic in a way that couples the cryosphere directly to the overturning circulation. These interactions mean that the stability of the whole cannot be assessed by considering each element in isolation.

The consequence of positive coupling is that the effective threshold for any given element may be lower in a system where other elements are already destabilizing than it would be for that element considered alone, and that the joint probability of multiple transitions is not simply the product of independent probabilities. The overall effect of the network of interactions is assessed to be predominantly destabilizing rather than stabilizing, which raises the systemic risk above what an element-by-element assessment would suggest, though the strength, sign in specific cases, and timing of individual couplings remain uncertain and are an active area of research (Pörtner, 2023). The assessment therefore treats cascading interaction as a genuine amplifier of tipping-point risk while acknowledging that quantifying the cascade remains beyond current capability.

### 8.4.2 Coupling of climate and biodiversity

**The climate and biodiversity crises are deeply intertwined, with climate change driving biodiversity loss and the degradation of ecosystems in turn eroding the capacity of the biosphere to store carbon and to buffer climate, forming a coupled system whose joint destabilization is more dangerous than either crisis alone (*high confidence*).** A central conclusion of recent Earth-system assessment is that climate change and the loss of biological diversity cannot be assessed or addressed as separate problems, because the same warming that stresses tipping elements also drives species loss, and the resulting degradation of ecosystems weakens their regulation of carbon and climate (Pörtner, 2023). The tipping elements of the biosphere — forests and reefs above all — are simultaneously reservoirs of biodiversity and regulators of the carbon cycle, so that their transition is at once a biodiversity catastrophe and a climate feedback (Pörtner, 2023) (Smith, 2022) (Flores, 2024).

Independent lines of evidence document the biodiversity side of this coupling. Assessments of insect decline under climate change warn of consequences for pollination, food webs, and ecosystem function that would propagate through the systems on which human societies depend (Harvey, 2022), while broader analyses raise the question of whether the current episode of biodiversity loss constitutes a mass extinction comparable in kind to those recorded in the geological past (Cowie, 2022). The chemical contamination of the biosphere adds a further, partly independent stress that erodes ecosystem resilience and interacts with climatic pressures (Naidu, 2021). The integrated implication is that the resilience of the living systems that regulate climate is being reduced by multiple simultaneous drivers, lowering the buffering capacity that would otherwise damp the approach to tipping thresholds (Pörtner, 2023) (Folke, 2021) (Naidu, 2021).

### 8.4.3 Compound climatic hazards and regional cascades

**Climate change is increasing the frequency and severity of compound and connected hazards — droughts, heatwaves, marine heatwaves, and their consequences for water and food — that can interact and cascade across regions and sectors, and that provide the proximate mechanisms through which slow-onset tipping processes translate into acute societal harm (*high confidence*).** The pathways by which a destabilizing biosphere harms human systems run through specific, increasingly frequent hazards. A projected global shift toward more rapidly developing "flash" droughts under continued warming would compress the time available to respond to the onset of water and agricultural stress, raising the danger to food and water systems (Yuan, 2023). Marine heatwaves, in addition to their role in reef collapse, disrupt fisheries and marine ecosystems on which coastal populations depend (Smith, 2022). In particularly exposed regions such as the Eastern Mediterranean and Middle East, the compounding of extreme heat and aridity is projected to reach severity that challenges habitability and agriculture (Zittis, 2022).

These hazards do not act one at a time. Water scarcity has quantifiable economic consequences that propagate through trade and production systems well beyond the water-stressed region itself (Dolan, 2021), and the availability of fresh water is a growing global constraint that interacts with drought, heat, and ecosystem degradation (Mishra, 2023). The connectedness of these hazards is precisely what makes tipping-point risk a systemic rather than a local concern: a slow-onset process such as forest dieback or overturning weakening expresses itself through acute, compounding, cross-regional hazards whose combined impact on food, water, and health can exceed the sum of the individual effects and can propagate through economically and politically connected systems (Simpson, 2021) (Dolan, 2021) (Yuan, 2023).

## 8.5 Abrupt Change in the Paleoclimate Record

### 8.5.1 The paleorecord as evidence that the Earth system can reorganize

**The geological and paleoclimate record establishes that the Earth system has occupied a wide range of markedly different states and has undergone reorganizations that were abrupt relative to their causes, providing direct empirical evidence that nonlinear and threshold behaviour are real properties of the climate system rather than artefacts of models (*high confidence*).** The single most important contribution of the paleorecord to the assessment of tipping points is existential: it demonstrates that the modes of behaviour predicted by tipping theory have actually occurred. Over the Phanerozoic — the last half-billion years — reconstruction of continental configuration, sea level, and climate documents transitions between greenhouse and icehouse worlds, repeated advances and retreats of the seas across the continents, and the rise and disappearance of ice sheets, confirming that the system can move between configurations that differ profoundly from the present (Scotese, 2021). This long view supplies the range of states the Earth system is capable of occupying and thereby bounds the magnitude of change that tipping processes could, in principle, produce.

The value of the deep-time perspective is that it removes any presumption that the relatively stable climate of recent millennia is the system's only accessible state. The reconstructed history shows sea levels tens of metres different from today's, ice sheets present and absent, and climate zones displaced across latitudes, all as expressions of the same physical system responding to changes in forcing and boundary conditions (Scotese, 2021). For the assessment of the ice sheets in particular, the paleorecord's evidence that sea level has stood far higher than at present during past warm intervals is a direct constraint on the magnitude of sea-level change of which the cryosphere is capable, even if the precise mapping from a given warming to a given ice loss under modern rates of forcing cannot be read off from the past (Scotese, 2021). The paleorecord thus functions as an existence proof for large-scale reorganization and as a source of analogues, while stopping short of providing a deterministic forecast.

### 8.5.2 What the paleorecord constrains, and what it does not

**Paleoclimate evidence robustly constrains the range of states the Earth system can occupy and the reality of abrupt transitions between them, but it provides only limited constraint on the precise thresholds and rates that would apply under the unprecedented speed of present-day forcing (*high confidence*).** The strengths and limits of the paleorecord for tipping-point assessment must be stated carefully. Its strengths are that it records real transitions in the real system, integrating all the relevant feedbacks and interactions that models must approximate, and that it spans a range of climate states far wider than the instrumental era, capturing behaviour that would otherwise be inaccessible. Its evidence that the system has repeatedly reorganized is among the firmest foundations for treating abrupt change as a genuine risk (Scotese, 2021).

The principal limit is that the rate and character of present-day forcing have few close analogues in the reconstructed past. The current rate of change of atmospheric composition is rapid by the standards of most paleoclimate transitions, and the configuration of continents, ice, and biosphere that determines how the system responds is specific to the present. As a result, the paleorecord is better at establishing that thresholds exist and that transitions can be abrupt than at pinning down the exact level of forcing at which a particular modern tipping element would transition or the exact rate at which the transition would unfold. This is why the assessment repeatedly pairs high confidence in the qualitative existence of threshold behaviour with lower confidence in quantitative thresholds: the paleorecord underwrites the former far more strongly than the latter. The appropriate use of paleoclimate evidence in this chapter is therefore to establish plausibility, magnitude, and the reality of abrupt reorganization, while relying on process understanding and contemporary observations for the assessment of proximity to specific thresholds (Scotese, 2021) (Kemp, 2022).

### 8.5.3 Lessons from past states for the assessment of future risk

**Past warm climate states, in which sea levels and the distribution of ice differed greatly from the present, indicate that sustained warming commits the Earth system to large, slow, and effectively irreversible adjustments of the cryosphere and sea level, reinforcing the assessment that some tipping outcomes are matters of committed change rather than avoidable events once thresholds are crossed (*high confidence*).** The reconstructed history of warm intervals carries a specific and sobering lesson for the assessment of committed change. In those intervals, the configuration of ice and the level of the sea adjusted to the prevailing climate over long timescales, reaching states far from the present when the climate was sustainedly warmer (Scotese, 2021). The implication for the present is that if warming is sustained at elevated levels, the slow components of the system — above all the great ice sheets — will tend, over centuries to millennia, toward the configurations that such warmth implies, and that this adjustment, once underway past a threshold, is not reversible on any timescale relevant to human planning.

This lesson connects the deep past directly to the framing of risk that organizes this chapter. It means that a portion of tipping-point risk is not about the probability of a discrete future event but about the commitment being accumulated in the present through sustained forcing — a commitment whose full expression lies far in the future but whose determination is contemporaneous with today's emissions (Scotese, 2021). It also means that the reversibility that characterizes much of the climate system does not extend to these slow tipping elements: unlike surface temperature, which responds relatively quickly to changes in forcing, the ice sheets and sea level carry a long memory, so that the paleorecord's warm states represent not a transient excursion but the kind of enduring reorganization to which sustained warming tends. The assessment draws from this the conclusion that the treatment of committed and irreversible change must be a central rather than peripheral part of tipping-point risk, and that the paleorecord, by displaying the destinations toward which sustained warming points, is essential evidence for that treatment (Scotese, 2021) (Kemp, 2022).

## 8.6 Low-Likelihood, High-Impact Outcomes and the Framing of Risk

### 8.6.1 Why the tail of the distribution demands assessment

**Because the consequences of crossing certain climate thresholds would be severe, widespread, and in some cases effectively permanent on human timescales, low-likelihood high-impact outcomes can dominate the expected harm from climate change and must be assessed explicitly rather than excluded because their probabilities are hard to quantify (*high confidence*).** A defensible risk assessment weights outcomes by both their probability and their consequence, and for a class of outcomes in which the consequence is extreme, even a modest probability yields a large contribution to expected harm. The tipping elements assessed in this chapter populate exactly this class: an abrupt weakening of the ocean overturning circulation, an irreversible commitment to multi-metre sea-level rise, a large and self-sustaining release of permafrost carbon, or a continental-scale forest dieback would each impose consequences whose scale and irreversibility place them in the tail of the climate risk distribution. The argument for dedicated assessment of this tail is that it has been comparatively neglected relative to central-estimate impacts, and that the neglect is not justified by the evidence, since the physical plausibility of these outcomes is not in doubt even where their probability is uncertain (Kemp, 2022).

The case for foregrounding tail risk is strengthened by the structure of the uncertainty involved. For the highest-consequence tipping elements, uncertainty is not merely statistical scatter around a well-defined mean but deep uncertainty about the location of thresholds and the strength of feedbacks, of a kind that resists reduction to a single probability distribution. In such circumstances, the temptation to disregard outcomes that cannot be assigned a precise probability is precisely backwards: the same deep uncertainty that prevents confident quantification also prevents confident dismissal, and the appropriate response is to assess the range of plausible outcomes, including the severe tail, rather than to collapse the assessment onto a central estimate that omits the most consequential possibilities (Kemp, 2022) (Richardson, 2023). This chapter therefore treats the systematic assessment of catastrophic and extreme outcomes as a scientific obligation rather than as speculation.

### 8.6.2 The spectrum of high-impact outcomes

**High-impact tipping outcomes span a spectrum from severe regional disruption through globally significant reorganization of the climate system to, at the extreme and least likely end, outcomes that would threaten the stability of human societies at large scales; the assessment can characterize this spectrum qualitatively even where it cannot assign probabilities across it (*medium confidence*).** At the more probable and better-constrained end of the spectrum lie outcomes already partly observable: the functional collapse of many warm-water reef systems, severe regional water and food crises driven by compound drought and heat, and the loss of resilience across large areas of forest (Smith, 2022) (Yuan, 2023) (Forzieri, 2022). These outcomes are severe and their occurrence at some level under continued warming is assessed with relatively high confidence, even though they fall short of global reorganization.

Toward the more consequential end lie outcomes whose probability is lower or more uncertain but whose impact would be correspondingly greater: an abrupt weakening or collapse of the Atlantic overturning circulation, with hemispheric consequences for temperature and rainfall; a committed disintegration of marine-based ice, with multi-metre sea-level implications; and a large permafrost carbon release that would materially raise committed warming and thereby stress every other element. At the extreme end, catastrophe-focused analysis argues that the possibility of outcomes severe enough to threaten the fabric of human societies — through the convergence of climatic disruption with food, water, health, and conflict pathways — is not excluded by current evidence and has been insufficiently studied (Kemp, 2022). The value of characterizing this full spectrum is that it prevents the assessment from implicitly truncating the range of possibilities at the boundary of what can be comfortably quantified, and it makes explicit that the difference between the more and less probable outcomes is one of likelihood, not of physical impossibility (Kemp, 2022) (Richardson, 2023).

### 8.6.3 Systemic and cascading risk to human societies

**The gravest tipping-related risks to human societies arise not from any single physical transition but from the potential for climatic shocks to interact with the vulnerabilities and interconnections of human systems, producing cascading failures that propagate across food, water, health, economic, and political domains — a systemic risk that current tools assess only partially (*medium confidence*).** The translation of physical tipping into societal catastrophe runs through the coupling of hazards to exposed and vulnerable human systems, and through the propagation of shocks across the connections that bind those systems together. A framework for complex climate risk emphasizes that impacts arise from the intersection of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability, and that in an interconnected world the consequences of a shock in one place or sector can transmit to others through trade, finance, migration, and governance (Simpson, 2021). Water scarcity in one region imposes economic costs elsewhere through connected production systems (Dolan, 2021); a shock to food production can transmit through global markets; and the simultaneity of multiple stresses can overwhelm the capacity to respond.

Recent scholarship has begun to formalize this systemic dimension under the heading of interacting global crises, arguing that distinct stresses — climatic, ecological, economic, and political — can become entangled so that they amplify one another and produce a compound crisis greater than the sum of its parts (Lawrence, 2024). The introduction of new technologies into these tightly coupled systems can itself become a source of systemic risk, adding pathways through which shocks propagate and interact (Galaz, 2021). The relevance of this literature to tipping-point assessment is that it identifies the mechanisms by which a physical transition — itself perhaps regional and slow — could become a global societal catastrophe through the entanglement of climate with the other systems on which societies depend (Lawrence, 2024) (Galaz, 2021) (Simpson, 2021). The assessment of these systemic pathways is at an early stage and carries substantial uncertainty, but the pathways are real and their potential consequences severe enough that they belong within a complete assessment of tipping-point risk (Lawrence, 2024) (Pörtner, 2023).

### 8.6.4 The health dimension of tipping-point risk

**The human-health consequences of climate change provide a concrete and increasingly well-documented channel through which slow-onset and abrupt climatic changes translate into harm, and they demonstrate that the impacts of a destabilizing climate are already being realized rather than confined to the future (*high confidence*).** Systematic tracking of the intersection of climate and health documents that the hazards associated with warming — extreme heat, altered patterns of infectious disease, disruption of food and water systems, and the health consequences of extreme events — are producing measurable harm to human health, and that these harms grow with continued warming and delayed action (Romanello, 2023) (Romanello, 2024). This health evidence anchors the assessment of tipping-point risk in observable present-day consequences and provides a metric of harm that integrates across many of the physical pathways discussed in this chapter.

The health channel is also a lens on the distribution of tipping-point risk, because the harms fall unevenly, concentrating on populations with the least capacity to adapt and thereby compounding existing vulnerabilities (Romanello, 2024) (Simpson, 2021). This unevenness matters for the framing of risk: a tipping transition that appears moderate in globally aggregated terms may be catastrophic for specific exposed and vulnerable populations, and an assessment that considered only global aggregates would understate the human significance of the change. The most recent tracking emphasizes record-breaking threats arising from the delay in reducing the underlying drivers, underscoring that the window in which the approach to tipping thresholds could be slowed is itself a function of near-term action (Romanello, 2024). The health evidence thus links the physical assessment of tipping elements to the lived consequences for people and to the urgency of the choices that determine how close the system is driven to its thresholds (Romanello, 2023) (Romanello, 2024).

### 8.6.5 Implications for the goal of limiting warming

**The assessment of tipping points reinforces the rationale for limiting the level and duration of warming, because the probability of crossing the thresholds of the major tipping elements rises with both the magnitude and the persistence of the temperature increase, and because some thresholds, once crossed, commit the system to irreversible change regardless of subsequent mitigation (*high confidence*).** The organizing implication of this chapter for policy-relevant assessment is that tipping-point risk is a monotonic and, in the neighbourhood of thresholds, sharply increasing function of warming. Every increment of avoided warming reduces the probability of crossing thresholds, and — for elements with hysteresis — the timing of stabilization matters as well as the eventual level, because a temporary overshoot of a threshold can trigger an irreversible transition even if temperatures are later brought back down. This gives the concept of net-zero emissions its physical meaning for tipping-point risk: stabilizing the climate requires bringing net emissions to zero, and the level at which stabilization occurs determines how many thresholds are approached or crossed (Fankhauser, 2021).

The precise definition and implementation of net zero therefore have direct consequences for tipping-point risk. Getting net zero right — in its coverage of gases, its treatment of removals, and its timing — determines the peak warming to which the system is exposed and hence the degree to which the thresholds of the tipping elements are challenged (Fankhauser, 2021). The tools available to support this effort, including the application of computational methods across mitigation and adaptation, expand the capacity to act, though they are enablers rather than substitutes for reducing the underlying drivers (Kaack, 2022). The assessment does not prescribe a particular level of ambition, which lies outside the scope of a Working Group I evaluation, but it establishes the physical basis for the proposition that the avoidance of tipping-point risk is a strong function of how much and how long the planet is warmed, and that the irreversibility of several tipping elements makes the near-term trajectory of emissions consequential far beyond the near term (Fankhauser, 2021) (Kaack, 2022) (Kemp, 2022).

## 8.7 Detection, Monitoring, and the State of Knowledge

### 8.7.1 The role of sustained observation and emerging analytical tools

**The detection of approaching thresholds depends on sustained, high-quality, long-duration observation of the relevant tipping elements, and the analytical tools now available — including satellite remote sensing and computational methods for pattern detection — have materially improved the capacity to monitor resilience, while remaining insufficient for the elements with the shortest observational records (*high confidence*).** The empirical advances reported in this chapter — the detection of resilience loss in the Amazon and in global forests — were made possible by the accumulation of consistent, multi-decade satellite records combined with analytical methods capable of extracting resilience indicators from noisy data (Boulton, 2022) (Forzieri, 2022). This demonstrates a general principle: the ability to see a tipping element approaching its threshold is a direct function of the length, quality, and continuity of the observational record, and of the sophistication of the methods brought to bear on it. The strongest results come precisely from those elements for which both the data and the methods are mature.

The expanding toolkit of computational methods offers the prospect of further gains, both in the detection of early-warning signals and in the broader project of understanding and responding to climate change (Kaack, 2022). Such methods can extract structure from large and heterogeneous Earth-observation datasets, integrate disparate lines of evidence, and support the monitoring of systems that are difficult to observe directly. At the same time, the assessment cautions that analytical sophistication cannot substitute for the underlying observations: where the record is short, as it is for the ocean overturning circulation and for the dynamic behaviour of the ice sheets, no analytical method can fully compensate, and the priority is the establishment and maintenance of the sustained observing systems that would, over time, make robust detection possible (Kaack, 2022) (Boulton, 2022). The improvement of monitoring capacity for the least-observed, highest-consequence elements is accordingly identified as a first-order priority.

### 8.7.2 Principal knowledge gaps and research priorities

**The most consequential knowledge gaps in the assessment of tipping points concern the quantitative location of thresholds, the strength and sign of interactions among elements, and the behaviour of the coupled human–Earth system under compound stress; reducing these gaps is essential for moving from qualitative plausibility to calibrated probability (*high confidence*).** Several specific gaps recur across the tipping elements assessed in this chapter. First, for every major element the qualitative existence of threshold behaviour is far better established than the quantitative value of the threshold, so that the assessment can state that a transition is physically possible with far more confidence than it can state the level of warming at which it becomes likely. Closing this gap requires improved process understanding, better observational constraints, and, where possible, the extraction of quantitative information from paleoclimate analogues, tempered by the recognition that present-day forcing rates have few close past parallels (Scotese, 2021) (Miner, 2022).

Second, the interactions among tipping elements — their sign, strength, and timing — are a critical and underdeveloped area, because these interactions determine whether the system's total risk exceeds the sum of its parts and whether cascades are plausible (Pörtner, 2023). Third, the coupling of the biosphere to the climate, including the destabilization of the land and ocean carbon sinks, needs to be represented more completely in Earth-system models, since the resilience declines now being observed in forests and reefs are incompletely captured by models that treat vegetation and carbon uptake as stable (Forzieri, 2022) (Hammond, 2022) (McDowell, 2022). Fourth, the systemic risk arising from the interaction of climatic tipping with human systems — the domain of cascading societal impacts and polycrisis dynamics — is at an early stage of formalization and requires the development of methods that can represent the propagation of shocks across coupled human and natural systems (Lawrence, 2024) (Simpson, 2021) (Galaz, 2021). Progress on each of these fronts would move the assessment of specific outcomes from statements of physical plausibility toward calibrated probabilistic judgements.

### 8.7.3 Synthesis

**Taken together, the evidence establishes that abrupt and irreversible change is a real and present feature of the climate system, that several major tipping elements show observational signs of declining stability, and that the associated risks — including low-likelihood high-impact and systemic outcomes — rise with the magnitude and duration of warming and warrant a central place in the assessment of climate risk (*high confidence*).** The synthesis of this chapter rests on several convergent conclusions. The reality of tipping behaviour is established by process understanding and by the paleoclimate record's demonstration that the Earth system has repeatedly reorganized (Scotese, 2021) (Folke, 2021). The proximity of specific elements to their thresholds is best established for warm-water reefs, which are already transitioning, and for forests, in which resilience loss is directly observed, and least well established for the ocean overturning circulation and the ice sheets, whose thresholds are physically real but quantitatively uncertain (Smith, 2022) (Boulton, 2022) (Forzieri, 2022). The permafrost feedback is assessed as a large, pervasive, and effectively irreversible amplifier of warming rather than a single global switch, and it raises the stress on every other element (Miner, 2022).

The interactions among these elements, and between them and the human systems that depend on climate stability, are assessed to be predominantly amplifying, so that the systemic risk exceeds what an element-by-element accounting would imply, even though the quantification of these interactions remains immature (Pörtner, 2023) (Lawrence, 2024) (Simpson, 2021). Because the highest-consequence outcomes are also among the most deeply uncertain, and because several are irreversible once triggered, a complete assessment must weight the tail of the risk distribution explicitly and must recognize that the accumulation of commitment in the present is itself a form of risk (Kemp, 2022) (Richardson, 2023). Finally, the whole of this risk is a rising function of warming and of its persistence, which grounds the physical case for limiting both the level and the duration of the temperature increase and for the proper definition and pursuit of net-zero emissions (Fankhauser, 2021) (Kaack, 2022). The assessment of tipping points thus concludes not with a single number but with a structured judgement: that the possibility of abrupt, irreversible, and cascading change is well founded, that it becomes more likely the further and longer the planet is warmed, and that its potential consequences are grave enough to be treated as a central rather than a peripheral concern of climate science (Kemp, 2022) (Richardson, 2023) (Pörtner, 2023).

## 8.8 Regional and Sectoral Expression of Tipping-Point Risk

### 8.8.1 Water: drought, scarcity, and the acceleration of onset

**The water cycle is a principal medium through which tipping and abrupt processes are expressed as harm, and both the projected acceleration of drought onset and the growing scarcity of fresh water indicate that water-related impacts will intensify and arrive faster under continued warming (*high confidence*).** Water is the connective tissue between many of the tipping elements assessed in this chapter and the human and ecological systems that experience their consequences. A projected transition toward more rapidly developing droughts under continued warming means that the interval between the onset of dry conditions and the emergence of severe agricultural and hydrological stress is expected to shorten, compressing the time available for detection, warning, and response and thereby raising the effective severity of a given drought (Yuan, 2023). This acceleration is itself a form of abrupt change in the character of a hazard, distinct from any change in its mean frequency, and it interacts with the slow-onset destabilization of forests and reefs that depends on water availability.

The scarcity of fresh water is simultaneously a growing global constraint in its own right. The availability of fresh water is under mounting pressure from the combined effects of climatic change, demand, and the degradation of the systems that store and regulate it, and this scarcity carries consequences that extend well beyond directly affected regions (Mishra, 2023). Analysis of the economic dimension shows that water scarcity in one location imposes measurable costs elsewhere through the connections of trade and production, so that a regional water crisis can propagate into a broader economic disturbance (Dolan, 2021). For the assessment of tipping-point risk, the significance of the water cycle is that it provides a fast, connected pathway by which slow physical changes — forest dieback, altered circulation, shifting rainfall belts — are translated into acute, cross-regional harm to food systems, economies, and health (Yuan, 2023) (Dolan, 2021) (Mishra, 2023).

### 8.8.2 Oceans and marine ecosystems beyond the reefs

**Marine heatwaves and the broader warming of the ocean impose biological impacts that extend well beyond warm-water coral reefs, restructuring marine ecosystems and the services they provide, and constituting an abrupt-hazard expression of ocean warming that affects fisheries and coastal communities across latitudes (*high confidence*).** The ocean's role in tipping-point risk is not limited to the overturning circulation and to reef collapse. Marine heatwaves — discrete periods of anomalously warm ocean temperatures — produce biological impacts that include mass mortality of marine organisms, shifts in the geographic ranges of species, changes in the structure of marine food webs, and outbreaks of disease, and these impacts have been documented across ocean basins as such events have grown more frequent, longer, and more intense (Smith, 2022). Because marine heatwaves are episodic and rapid in onset, they represent an abrupt-hazard expression of the underlying trend of ocean warming, delivering shocks to marine ecosystems in a manner analogous to how heatwaves and flash droughts deliver shocks on land (Smith, 2022) (Yuan, 2023).

The consequences of these marine impacts propagate into human systems through fisheries, coastal protection, and the livelihoods of communities that depend on productive coastal waters. As marine ecosystems are restructured by repeated thermal shocks, the services they provide — food, income, and shoreline protection — are placed at risk, and the populations affected are frequently those with limited capacity to adapt (Smith, 2022) (Simpson, 2021). The ocean thus contributes to tipping-point risk along two coupled axes: the slow, high-consequence, deeply uncertain axis of circulation and ice, and the faster, better-observed, already-realized axis of marine heatwaves and ecosystem disruption. Both axes matter for the assessment, and the more immediate axis provides observable evidence that the ocean is already delivering climate-driven harm to ecosystems and people (Smith, 2022).

### 8.8.3 Biodiversity, ecosystems, and the erosion of buffering capacity

**The degradation of biodiversity and ecosystems, driven by climate change acting in concert with pollution and land-use pressures, erodes the buffering capacity of the biosphere and thereby lowers the resilience that would otherwise damp the approach to tipping thresholds, making biodiversity loss both a consequence and an amplifier of tipping-point risk (*high confidence*).** The living systems of the Earth do not merely respond passively to climate change; they regulate the carbon cycle, moderate regional climate, and provide the resilience that allows the system to absorb disturbance. The widespread decline of biodiversity therefore matters for tipping-point risk beyond its intrinsic significance, because it reduces the capacity of ecosystems to buffer perturbations and to recover from them (Pörtner, 2023) (Folke, 2021). Warnings of severe insect decline point to consequences for pollination and for the food webs that structure terrestrial and freshwater ecosystems (Harvey, 2022), and the broader question of whether the present episode of loss constitutes a mass extinction underscores the scale of the ongoing degradation (Cowie, 2022).

These biological stresses do not act alone. The pervasive chemical contamination of the environment constitutes an additional, partly independent pressure on ecosystems, one that can interact with climatic stress to lower the threshold at which ecological systems fail and that has itself been characterized as a potential catastrophic risk (Naidu, 2021). The combination of climatic warming, biodiversity loss, and chemical pollution represents a multi-stressor assault on the resilience of the biosphere, and it is this compounding of stresses — rather than any one of them in isolation — that most threatens the buffering capacity on which the stability of the tipping elements depends (Naidu, 2021) (Pörtner, 2023) (Folke, 2021). The assessment concludes that the protection and restoration of biodiversity and ecosystem integrity is therefore not separable from the management of tipping-point risk, because the resilience of the living biosphere is a determinant of how close the climate system can be driven to its thresholds before abrupt change ensues (Pörtner, 2023) (Folke, 2021) (Richardson, 2023).

## 8.9 Assessment Confidence and Key Uncertainties

### 8.9.1 What is assessed with high confidence

**A core set of conclusions about abrupt and irreversible change is supported by multiple, independent lines of evidence and is assessed with high confidence, notwithstanding the deep uncertainty that attends the quantitative details of individual thresholds (*high confidence*).** The evidence base supports several robust conclusions. It is assessed with high confidence that tipping behaviour — threshold-crossing, self-amplifying, and imperfectly reversible transition — is a genuine property of components of the Earth system, established both by process understanding and by the paleoclimate record's demonstration of repeated large-scale reorganization (Scotese, 2021) (Folke, 2021). It is assessed with high confidence that warm-water coral reefs are already undergoing climate-driven degradation through the biological impacts of marine heatwaves (Smith, 2022), and with high confidence that forests across multiple biomes show emerging, coherent signals of declining resilience under climate change, with a hotter-drought mortality mechanism operating across forest types (Forzieri, 2022) (Hammond, 2022) (McDowell, 2022) (Frenne, 2021). It is assessed with high confidence that permafrost thaw constitutes a large, pervasive, and effectively irreversible positive feedback on warming (Miner, 2022), and with high confidence that the climate and biodiversity crises are coupled such that the degradation of ecosystems erodes the biosphere's capacity to buffer climatic change (Pörtner, 2023).

It is further assessed with high confidence that the human consequences of a destabilizing climate are already measurable, most clearly through the tracked intersection of climate and health, and that these consequences grow with continued warming and delayed action (Romanello, 2023) (Romanello, 2024). And it is assessed with high confidence that tipping-point risk, taken as a whole, is a rising function of the magnitude and duration of warming, and that the systematic assessment of low-likelihood high-impact outcomes is a necessary rather than optional component of a complete evaluation of climate risk (Kemp, 2022) (Richardson, 2023) (Fankhauser, 2021).

### 8.9.2 The principal residual uncertainties

**The principal residual uncertainties concern the quantitative values of thresholds, the strength and sign of interactions among tipping elements, and the dynamics of the coupled human–Earth system, and these uncertainties are deep rather than merely statistical, so that several of the most consequential outcomes cannot at present be assigned calibrated probabilities (*high confidence* in this characterization of the uncertainty).** Against the robust qualitative conclusions stands a set of stubborn quantitative uncertainties. For the Atlantic overturning circulation and the ice sheets, the existence of thresholds is not in doubt, but their location in terms of the level of warming, and the rate at which transitions would proceed, are poorly constrained, in part because the instrumental records are short and present-day forcing rates lack close paleoclimate analogues (Scotese, 2021) (Kemp, 2022). For the Amazon and other forests, the observed loss of resilience is robust, but the precise combination of warming, drought, deforestation, and fire that would trigger an irreversible large-scale transition remains uncertain and spatially variable (Boulton, 2022) (Flores, 2024). For permafrost, the size of the vulnerable carbon pool, the partition between carbon dioxide and methane, and the rate of release are imperfectly known (Miner, 2022).

Above the level of individual elements, the interactions among them — the couplings that determine whether the system's total risk exceeds the sum of its parts and whether cascades are plausible — are assessed to be predominantly amplifying but are quantitatively immature, representing one of the most important frontiers for reducing uncertainty (Pörtner, 2023). The behaviour of the coupled human–Earth system under compound stress, including the systemic and polycrisis dynamics through which physical tipping could become societal catastrophe, is at an early stage of formalization and carries correspondingly large uncertainty (Lawrence, 2024) (Galaz, 2021) (Simpson, 2021). The honest summary is that the assessment is confident about the reality and the direction of tipping-point risk and about its dependence on warming, while remaining genuinely uncertain about the precise thresholds, timings, and interactions that would determine exactly how and when specific abrupt changes would unfold — an uncertainty that, given the severity and irreversibility of the outcomes involved, is itself a reason for concern rather than for reassurance (Kemp, 2022) (Richardson, 2023).

### 8.9.3 The distinction between confidence and reassurance

**A central interpretive principle of this chapter is that uncertainty about the timing and magnitude of tipping-point outcomes must not be read as evidence of their improbability, because for outcomes that are severe and irreversible the deep uncertainty surrounding them is a component of the risk rather than a mitigation of it (*high confidence*).** It is a recurring error in the public interpretation of tipping-point science to treat the absence of a confident prediction as though it were a confident prediction of absence. The assessment resists this error explicitly. Where the science cannot yet say how close a threshold is, or how fast a transition would proceed, the appropriate inference is that the outcome is possible over a range that includes the more dangerous end, not that it can be set aside. This is especially so for the elements — the overturning circulation, the ice sheets — whose thresholds are least well constrained and whose consequences would be gravest, precisely the combination in which the temptation to discount the uncertain is strongest and least justified (Kemp, 2022).

This principle has practical force for how the findings of this chapter should be used. The high-confidence conclusions — that tipping behaviour is real, that several elements are destabilizing, that the risk rises with warming, and that irreversibility makes near-term choices consequential far into the future — are sufficient on their own to establish that abrupt and irreversible change is a serious and present dimension of climate risk (Richardson, 2023) (Folke, 2021) (Fankhauser, 2021). The residual uncertainties do not soften this conclusion; they sharpen it, because they attach to outcomes whose severity and permanence mean that even a modest probability carries a large expected cost, and because they cluster around the very elements whose transition would be most catastrophic (Kemp, 2022) (Pörtner, 2023). The assessment therefore closes on the judgement that the proper response to the deep uncertainty of tipping-point science is neither alarmism nor complacency but a rigorous accounting of the full range of plausible outcomes, weighted by consequence as well as by likelihood, and an explicit recognition that the accumulation of commitment through sustained warming is itself an ongoing and only partly reversible act (Kemp, 2022) (Richardson, 2023) (Fankhauser, 2021).
