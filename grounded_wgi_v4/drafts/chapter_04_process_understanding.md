# Chapter 4. Process Understanding: Forcings, Feedbacks, and Cycles

## Executive Summary

**The physical understanding of how energy imbalance is imposed on the climate system, amplified or damped by feedbacks, and transmitted through the coupled carbon, biogeochemical, and hydrological cycles has consolidated since the previous assessment cycle, and the direction of every major process is now attributed to human activity with progressively narrower uncertainty ranges (*high confidence*).** The dominant control on the present energy imbalance is the accumulation of carbon dioxide and other long-lived greenhouse gases, whose growth rate and airborne fraction are quantified annually to within a few percent by the global carbon budget synthesis (Friedlingstein, 2023).

**Effective radiative forcing, which folds rapid atmospheric and land-surface adjustments into the instantaneous perturbation, is the most policy-relevant forcing metric, and the aerosol contribution remains the single largest source of uncertainty in the total anthropogenic forcing (*high confidence*).** Mineral dust and other natural aerosol populations, whose source apportionment is now resolved at the level of individual emitting regions, modulate both the shortwave and the longwave components of this forcing (Kok, 2021).

**Net climate feedbacks are positive, so the equilibrium warming for a doubling of carbon dioxide is substantially larger than the no-feedback response, with the cloud feedback the principal amplifier and the principal residual uncertainty (*high confidence*).** Surface-albedo feedbacks are expressed most strongly in the Arctic, where near-surface warming has proceeded at nearly four times the global rate over the satellite era (Rantanen, 2022).

**The land and ocean carbon sinks together remove roughly half of anthropogenic emissions, but the efficiency of both sinks is sensitive to warming, drought, and disturbance, introducing a carbon-cycle feedback that is positive and of increasing magnitude (*medium to high confidence*).** The persistence of the global forest carbon sink, and the emerging signals that its resilience is declining under compound stress, are now documented at global scale (Pan, 2024), even as the resilience of that sink shows signs of decline (Forzieri, 2022).

**Terrestrial biogeochemical cycling is governed at the process level by the interaction of organic matter with soil minerals and by the ecology of the soil microbiome, both of which set the residence time of carbon and the fluxes of nitrogen and other nutrients (*high confidence*).** Disturbance to these interfaces, whether through erosion, salinization, or novel-material contamination, reduces the functional capacity of soils to store carbon (Kleber, 2021) and disrupts the microbial ecology that mediates it (Sokol, 2022).

**The water cycle is intensifying, with the atmospheric demand for moisture rising, droughts emerging more rapidly, and inland waters warming, and each of these process shifts carries an anthropogenic fingerprint (*high confidence*).** The abrupt onset of flash droughts under continued warming, and the propagation of heat extremes into lakes and rivers, are robust across observational and modelling lines of evidence (Yuan, 2023), as is the propagation of heat into inland waters (Woolway, 2021).

**Earth-system model intercomparison provides the quantitative bridge between process understanding and projection, and the current generation of models reproduces the observed forcing history while spanning a credible range of feedback strengths (*high confidence*).** Scenario-based projections rest on a small number of shared pathways whose forcing trajectories are constructed to be internally consistent with the emissions and land-use assumptions they encode (Tebaldi, 2021).

## 4.1 Radiative Forcing and Effective Radiative Forcing

### 4.1.1 The forcing concept and its refinement

**Radiative forcing remains the organizing quantity of physical climate assessment, and the shift from the instantaneous and stratosphere-adjusted definitions toward effective radiative forcing, which incorporates rapid adjustments of the troposphere and land surface, has improved the correspondence between a given forcing agent and the surface temperature response it ultimately produces (*high confidence*).** A radiative forcing is, in its simplest formulation, a perturbation to the net radiative flux at the top of the atmosphere imposed by an external agent while surface temperature is held fixed. The strength of the concept is that it isolates the driver from the response: a forcing of a given magnitude in watts per square meter (W m-2) can be compared across agents as chemically and physically dissimilar as carbon dioxide, sulfate aerosol, and a change in land-surface reflectivity. The weakness of the simplest formulation is that the atmosphere does not, in fact, hold everything except surface temperature fixed while a forcing is applied. Clouds adjust within hours, the stratosphere cools radiatively within months, and vegetation and snow respond within a season. Effective radiative forcing was introduced precisely to absorb these rapid adjustments, which occur without any change in global-mean surface temperature and which therefore properly belong to the forcing rather than to the feedback.

The distinction matters most for agents whose rapid adjustments are large relative to their instantaneous perturbation. Absorbing aerosols such as black carbon warm the layer of atmosphere in which they reside, stabilizing it, altering cloud cover, and thereby generating a semi-direct adjustment that can partially offset the instantaneous warming. For carbon dioxide, the tropospheric adjustment is comparatively modest, so the instantaneous and effective forcings differ by only a small fraction; this is one reason the carbon dioxide forcing is among the best-constrained quantities in the entire assessment. The annual reconciliation of sources and sinks that underlies the carbon budget synthesis provides the atmospheric growth rate from which the concentration pathway, and hence the forcing, is derived with high precision (Friedlingstein, 2023). The 2024 update to that synthesis confirms that the atmospheric carbon dioxide burden continues to rise essentially in proportion to cumulative emissions, with only second-order variation in the airborne fraction (Friedlingstein, 2025).

### 4.1.2 The well-mixed greenhouse gases

**The forcing exerted by the well-mixed greenhouse gases is the dominant term in the total anthropogenic forcing, and its magnitude and growth are established with the highest confidence of any forcing agent because both the atmospheric concentrations and the underlying spectroscopy are directly measured (*virtually certain*).** Carbon dioxide is the largest single contributor, followed by methane and nitrous oxide, with the halogenated species contributing a smaller but non-negligible increment. The forcing from carbon dioxide grows approximately with the logarithm of its concentration, so that each successive increment of concentration produces a slightly smaller radiative effect than the last; this logarithmic dependence is a robust feature of the molecular absorption bands and is not in scientific dispute. What has changed across assessment cycles is not the physics but the precision of the input concentrations and the completeness of the emissions accounting that constrains them.

The sectoral decomposition of the emissions that drive these concentrations has been mapped in detail, revealing that the growth in greenhouse-gas emissions over recent decades has been distributed unevenly across energy supply, industry, transport, buildings, and land use, with the energy and industry sectors dominating the long-lived carbon dioxide flux (Lamb, 2021). This decomposition is important for the forcing assessment because it identifies which agents are co-emitted: fossil combustion that releases carbon dioxide also releases sulfur dioxide, the precursor of sulfate aerosol, so that the net forcing from a given activity is the sum of a warming and a cooling term that do not necessarily evolve together in time. As controls on sulfur emissions tighten for air-quality reasons, the cooling term declines faster than the warming term, unmasking a fraction of the greenhouse warming that had previously been offset.

Methane occupies a distinctive position in the forcing budget because it is both a direct greenhouse gas and a chemical precursor that influences tropospheric ozone and stratospheric water vapor, so that its effective forcing exceeds its direct forcing by a substantial margin. The agricultural contribution to the methane budget, and in particular the flux from flooded rice cultivation, is now quantified with sufficient resolution to support targeted mitigation assessment (Qian, 2023). Because methane has a short atmospheric lifetime relative to carbon dioxide, its forcing responds within roughly a decade to changes in emission rate, which gives methane mitigation a leverage over near-term forcing that carbon dioxide mitigation cannot provide.

### 4.1.3 Ozone, water vapor, and the shorter-lived agents

**The tropospheric ozone forcing is positive and among the more uncertain of the well-quantified agents, because ozone is not emitted directly but is produced photochemically from precursors whose emissions and chemistry vary strongly in space and time (*medium confidence*).** Ozone precursors—methane, carbon monoxide, nitrogen oxides, and volatile organic compounds—are co-emitted with aerosols and their precursors across the industrial, transport, and agricultural sectors, so the ozone forcing is intimately coupled to the aerosol forcing through shared sources. Road transport in particular emits a complex mixture in which the non-exhaust fraction, arising from tyre, brake, and road-surface wear, contributes both to the particulate burden and, indirectly, to the oxidant chemistry that governs ozone production (Fussell, 2022). The stratospheric water vapor forcing, produced partly by methane oxidation, is a smaller positive term that is nonetheless an example of the chemical coupling that makes the shorter-lived agents harder to assess than the well-mixed gases.

Stratospheric ozone, by contrast, has undergone a forced recovery following the phase-out of ozone-depleting substances, a recovery that itself constitutes a small forcing as the vertical distribution of ozone and its radiative effect shift. The lesson of the stratospheric ozone case for the broader forcing assessment is that coordinated international control of a well-identified set of agents can reverse a forcing trend on a multi-decadal timescale, and that the radiative and the chemical consequences of such controls must be assessed jointly rather than separately.

### 4.1.4 Land-use, surface-albedo, and biogeophysical forcings

**Changes in land cover impose a forcing that is biogeophysical as well as biogeochemical, altering surface albedo, roughness, and evapotranspiration in ways whose net radiative effect depends strongly on latitude and background climate (*medium confidence*).** The conversion of forest to cropland or pasture typically raises surface albedo, a cooling effect, while simultaneously reducing evapotranspiration and altering cloud cover, effects whose sign varies with region. In the boreal zone the albedo effect of deforestation tends to dominate and produces a net cooling, whereas in the tropics the loss of evaporative cooling and the associated changes in convection tend to produce a net warming. The dominant driver of tropical forest loss is the expansion of agriculture, and disentangling the specific commodities and land-use transitions responsible has been necessary to attribute the biogeophysical forcing to its proximate causes (Pendrill, 2022).

Forest cover also exerts a microclimatic control that is not captured by the global-mean forcing but is central to the local expression of climate change. The canopy buffers the temperature experienced beneath it, decoupling the forest-floor microclimate from the free-atmosphere warming, so that the loss or degradation of forest cover removes a thermal buffer at the same time that it changes the global albedo (Frenne, 2021). At high elevation the surface-albedo forcing interacts with the pronounced warming of mountain regions, where the retreat of snow and ice exposes darker surfaces and drives an elevation-dependent amplification of the temperature signal (Pepin, 2022). These regional expressions of the surface-albedo forcing are the local face of a feedback that, integrated globally, is treated in the following section.

### 4.1.5 Quantifying and communicating forcing uncertainty

**The total anthropogenic effective radiative forcing is positive and its central estimate is well separated from zero, but its uncertainty is dominated by the aerosol term, and this asymmetry has direct consequences for the inference of climate sensitivity from the historical record (*high confidence*).** The greenhouse-gas forcing is known with high precision; the aerosol forcing, being negative and poorly constrained, sets the width of the total. Because the observed warming is the response to the net forcing, a more negative aerosol forcing implies a larger sensitivity for a given observed warming, and vice versa. This is why progress on the aerosol forcing, treated in Section 4.3, is inseparable from progress on climate sensitivity, treated in Section 4.2: they are two projections of the same underlying uncertainty.

The forcing framework also underpins the economic and policy metrics that translate physical change into decision-relevant terms. The social cost of carbon, which monetizes the marginal damage of an incremental tonne of carbon dioxide, depends at its foundation on the forcing-to-warming chain, and comprehensive re-estimation using updated damage functions and discounting implies a substantially higher value than earlier assessments had adopted (Rennert, 2022). Situating the forcing assessment within the wider diagnosis of Earth-system state, the transgression of multiple planetary boundaries—of which the climate boundary, defined in part by radiative forcing, is one—signals that the forcing is now operating outside the range within which the system is understood to remain stable (Richardson, 2023). The framing of these boundaries in terms that couple the biophysical to the equitable further underscores that the forcing is not a purely physical quantity but one whose consequences are distributed unevenly across the human and natural world (Rockström, 2023).

## 4.2 Climate Sensitivity and Feedbacks

### 4.2.1 The sensitivity framework

**Equilibrium climate sensitivity, the eventual global-mean warming that follows a sustained doubling of atmospheric carbon dioxide, is the single number that most compactly summarizes the amplifying and damping processes of the climate system, and its assessed range has narrowed as the feedbacks that determine it have been separately constrained (*high confidence*).** The no-feedback response to a doubling of carbon dioxide—the warming that would occur if the only adjustment were the Planck response of a blackbody radiating more efficiently as it warms—is close to one degree C and is not in dispute. Everything above that value is the net product of feedbacks, and everything uncertain about the sensitivity is uncertainty in feedbacks. The task of process understanding is therefore to decompose the net feedback into physically distinct mechanisms, to constrain each from observations and process models, and to recombine them into a sensitivity estimate whose uncertainty is the propagated uncertainty of its parts. This bottom-up, process-based line of evidence is now combined with the historical-record and paleoclimate lines to yield a sensitivity range materially narrower than that of a decade ago.

A conceptual distinction that has become central is that between equilibrium sensitivity and the transient response that is actually experienced on human timescales. The ocean's enormous heat capacity delays the surface warming, so that at any moment during a period of rising forcing the realized warming is smaller than the equilibrium warming for the forcing then applied. The efficiency with which the ocean takes up heat, and the pattern in which it does so, therefore modulates the effective feedback that operates over the historical period. This pattern effect—the dependence of the global feedback on the spatial pattern of warming—has emerged as a key reconciler of the historical and process-based sensitivity estimates, because a warming pattern that is concentrated in regions of strong negative feedback produces a historical feedback that is more stabilizing than the equilibrium feedback will ultimately be.

### 4.2.2 The water-vapor and lapse-rate feedbacks

**The combined water-vapor and lapse-rate feedback is strongly positive and is the largest of the well-understood feedbacks, and the compensating relationship between its two components makes the combination more robust than either part alone (*very high confidence*).** A warmer atmosphere holds more water vapor, and because water vapor is itself a powerful greenhouse gas, this constitutes a large positive feedback. The relative humidity of the atmosphere remains approximately constant as it warms, which is the physical basis for treating the water-vapor feedback as a near-linear function of temperature. The lapse-rate feedback arises because the vertical profile of warming is not uniform: in the tropics the upper troposphere warms more than the surface, which increases the efficiency of radiation to space and therefore constitutes a negative feedback. The two feedbacks are anticorrelated across models and across regions, because the same moist processes that load the upper troposphere with water vapor also warm it preferentially, so that a model with a strong positive water-vapor feedback tends to have a strongly negative lapse-rate feedback. Their sum is far better constrained than either term, and it is robustly positive.

The realization of these feedbacks depends on the fidelity with which models represent moist convection and the free-tropospheric humidity distribution, which is one of the reasons that model evaluation against observed humidity and temperature profiles is a central activity of the intercomparison enterprise. The current generation of Earth-system models, of which the CMCC Earth System Model is a representative example, reproduces the broad structure of the tropical temperature and humidity profile and therefore the compensating water-vapor and lapse-rate feedbacks, while differing in the details that matter for the residual uncertainty (Lovato, 2022).

### 4.2.3 The surface-albedo and cryosphere feedbacks

**The surface-albedo feedback is positive, is concentrated at high latitudes, and is expressed most dramatically in the Arctic, where the observed rate of near-surface warming has exceeded the global-mean rate by nearly a factor of four over the satellite era (*high confidence*).** As snow and sea ice retreat, they expose ocean and land surfaces of much lower reflectivity, which absorb more solar radiation and drive further warming and further retreat. This ice-albedo feedback is the textbook amplifier, but the modern understanding is considerably richer than the albedo mechanism alone. The strength of Arctic amplification observed over recent decades exceeds what the albedo feedback in isolation would produce, implicating additional processes: changes in the vertical structure of atmospheric warming, in poleward heat transport, in cloud cover, and in the seasonal storage and release of ocean heat. The magnitude of the observed amplification, nearly four times the global rate, is larger than most model simulations reproduce, which points either to underestimated feedbacks or to a contribution from internal variability that the assessment must quantify (Rantanen, 2022).

The atmospheric processes that accompany the albedo feedback in the Arctic have been characterized in unprecedented detail by year-round observations from within the ice pack, which have documented the coupling of the surface energy budget to cloud, aerosol, and boundary-layer processes across the full seasonal cycle (Shupe, 2022). These observations are important for the feedback assessment because the surface-albedo feedback does not operate in isolation: its expression depends on the cloud cover that determines how much radiation reaches the surface, on the stability of the boundary layer that determines how surface warming is communicated upward, and on the aerosol populations that influence cloud properties. The cryospheric feedbacks thus provide a concrete illustration of the general principle that feedbacks interact, and that their sum cannot be obtained by assessing each in complete isolation.

### 4.2.4 Cloud feedbacks

**Cloud feedbacks are net positive and constitute both the largest amplifying uncertainty in the sensitivity estimate and the area in which process understanding has advanced most since the previous assessment, so that the residual cloud uncertainty, while still dominant, has narrowed (*high confidence*).** Clouds affect the energy budget in two opposing ways: they reflect incoming solar radiation, a cooling effect that dominates for low clouds, and they trap outgoing longwave radiation, a warming effect that dominates for high clouds. A cloud feedback is any change in these effects that accompanies warming, and because the two effects are large and of opposite sign, small fractional changes in cloud amount, altitude, or optical thickness translate into feedbacks that are significant relative to the total. The assessment now decomposes the cloud feedback into components that are separately constrained: the rising of high cloud tops with warming, which is a robust positive feedback grounded in well-understood thermodynamics; the poleward shift of the mid-latitude storm tracks and their associated clouds; and the response of low marine clouds, particularly the subtropical stratocumulus decks, which has been the principal source of the overall uncertainty.

The low-cloud feedback has been the crux because the governing processes—turbulent mixing, cloud-top entrainment, and the coupling of the cloud layer to the surface—occur at scales far below the resolution of global models and must be parameterized. Advances have come from high-resolution process modelling, from satellite observations of how these cloud regimes actually respond to natural temperature variations, and from the integration of physical understanding with data-driven methods that can extract feedback-relevant relationships from large observational archives (Willard, 2022). The convergence of these lines has favored a positive low-cloud feedback, ruling out the strongly negative values that some earlier parameterizations produced and thereby raising the lower bound of the sensitivity estimate. The high-cloud altitude feedback, by contrast, has been robustly positive throughout and is one of the more secure elements of the cloud assessment.

### 4.2.5 The carbon-cycle feedback on climate

**The carbon-cycle feedback—the response of the natural carbon sinks and sources to warming—is positive, meaning that warming tends to reduce the efficiency of natural carbon uptake and thereby amplify the atmospheric burden, and its magnitude is now the largest uncertainty in translating a given emissions pathway into a temperature outcome (*medium to high confidence*).** Unlike the physical feedbacks, which act on the energy budget, the carbon-cycle feedback acts on the concentration itself: a warmer climate weakens both the land and ocean sinks, so that a larger fraction of each tonne emitted remains airborne. The land component is the more uncertain because it integrates the responses of photosynthesis, respiration, disturbance, and nutrient limitation, each of which responds differently to warming, to changing precipitation, and to the fertilizing effect of rising carbon dioxide itself. The concentration-carbon feedback, whereby higher carbon dioxide stimulates plant growth and enhances uptake, is negative and partially offsets the positive climate-carbon feedback, and the balance between them is what the assessment must quantify.

The global carbon budget synthesis provides the top-down constraint on the net outcome of these competing feedbacks, partitioning the annual emissions among the atmosphere, ocean, and land and thereby measuring the aggregate sink efficiency year by year (Friedlingstein, 2023). The persistence of a substantial land sink, concentrated in the world's forests, has been confirmed by dedicated global assessment, but that same assessment reveals that the sink is sustained against a rising tide of disturbance and is therefore not guaranteed to persist (Pan, 2024). The emergence of statistical early-warning signals that forest resilience is declining across large regions is the process-level evidence that the land carbon-cycle feedback may strengthen as warming continues, because a forest that is losing resilience is one whose capacity to recover from drought, fire, and pest outbreaks—and therefore to continue storing carbon—is eroding (Forzieri, 2022). The mechanistic basis for this erosion, in the form of widespread and sometimes unexpected episodes of elevated tree mortality, has been documented across biomes and constitutes a direct threat to the durability of the terrestrial sink (Hartmann, 2022).

### 4.2.6 Combining feedbacks and constraining sensitivity

**The recombination of the separately assessed feedbacks yields a net feedback that is negative in the stabilizing sense required for a finite sensitivity but far weaker than the Planck response alone, and the resulting sensitivity range is now constrained by the agreement of the process-based, historical, and paleoclimate lines of evidence (*high confidence*).** The intercomparison of Earth-system models remains the framework within which feedbacks are diagnosed in a physically consistent setting, because a model computes all feedbacks simultaneously and self-consistently, avoiding the double-counting that can afflict the summation of independently estimated terms. The current model generation spans a range of feedback strengths, and the assessment's task is not to adopt the model mean uncritically but to weight the models by their fidelity to observed feedback-relevant processes and to the emergent constraints that relate an observable feature of the present climate to a feedback strength.

Scenario-based projection then propagates the assessed sensitivity, together with the assessed carbon-cycle feedback, through a small set of shared socioeconomic pathways whose forcing trajectories are internally consistent (Tebaldi, 2021). The value of this architecture is that it separates the uncertainty that is physical—the sensitivity and the feedbacks—from the uncertainty that is societal—the emissions pathway—so that each can be assessed with the appropriate methods. The physical uncertainty is now dominated, as it has long been, by the cloud and carbon-cycle feedbacks, and the narrowing of the sensitivity range achieved in recent years is attributable chiefly to progress on the low-cloud feedback and to the reconciliation of the historical record with the process understanding through the pattern effect.

## 4.3 Aerosols and Atmospheric Composition

### 4.3.1 The aerosol forcing and its residual uncertainty

**Aerosols exert a net negative effective radiative forcing that has offset a substantial fraction of the greenhouse warming to date, and this forcing remains the dominant uncertainty in the total anthropogenic forcing because it aggregates several mechanisms whose individual magnitudes are separately uncertain and whose sum is a small residual of large opposing terms (*high confidence*).** The aerosol forcing decomposes into a direct component, in which particles scatter and absorb radiation, and one or more indirect components, in which particles act as cloud condensation nuclei and thereby alter the number, size, reflectivity, and lifetime of cloud droplets. The direct scattering effect is cooling; the direct absorption effect, dominated by black carbon, is warming; the first indirect effect, whereby more numerous but smaller droplets brighten clouds, is cooling; and the adjustments of cloud amount and lifetime that follow are of uncertain sign and magnitude. The overall aerosol forcing is therefore a difference of large terms, which is why its fractional uncertainty is so much greater than that of the greenhouse-gas forcing even though the underlying microphysics is, in many respects, well understood.

The consequence of this uncertainty for the assessment as a whole cannot be overstated: because the observed warming is the response to the net forcing, and because the net forcing is the greenhouse-gas forcing minus the aerosol offset, the width of the aerosol uncertainty propagates directly into the inference of climate sensitivity from the historical record. A larger aerosol offset implies that the greenhouse forcing has been more strongly masked, which implies a larger sensitivity for the observed warming; a smaller offset implies the reverse. Narrowing the aerosol forcing is thus a prerequisite for narrowing the historical constraint on sensitivity, and progress on the two is inseparable. The sectoral emissions accounting that identifies the co-emission of aerosols and their precursors alongside greenhouse gases is the foundation on which the aerosol forcing history is built, because the aerosol burden is tied to the same combustion and industrial activities that release carbon dioxide (Lamb, 2021).

### 4.3.2 Natural aerosols: mineral dust

**Mineral dust is the largest natural aerosol population by mass, exerts a forcing whose sign depends on the underlying surface and the particle-size distribution, and modulates biogeochemical cycles by delivering nutrients to oceans and remote ecosystems, so that its assessment spans the physical and the biogeochemical (*medium confidence*).** Dust scatters and absorbs shortwave radiation and, because its particles are large, also interacts significantly with longwave radiation, producing a net forcing that is small in the global mean but large regionally and highly sensitive to the size distribution and the mineralogy of the emitting source. The apportionment of the global dust burden among the world's principal source regions has been quantified, revealing that a small number of arid regions dominate emission and that the resulting forcing and deposition patterns are correspondingly concentrated (Kok, 2021). This source apportionment is essential for two reasons: it constrains the present-day dust forcing, and it provides the baseline against which future changes in dust—driven by changes in aridity, land use, and wind—can be projected.

The biogeochemical role of dust couples the aerosol assessment to the carbon and nutrient cycles treated in Section 4.4. Dust deposition supplies iron and phosphorus to ocean regions where these nutrients limit biological productivity, so that changes in the dust cycle can alter the ocean carbon sink through the fertilization of phytoplankton. Dust also affects terrestrial ecosystems and the cryosphere: deposition of absorbing dust on snow and ice lowers their albedo and accelerates melt, coupling the dust cycle to the surface-albedo feedback discussed in Section 4.2. The projection of the dust forcing is complicated by the fact that its drivers act in opposing directions—increased aridity tends to raise emission while increased vegetation cover under carbon dioxide fertilization tends to suppress it—so that the sign of the future dust-forcing trend is not robustly established.

### 4.3.3 Anthropogenic aerosols and air quality

**Anthropogenic aerosols are co-emitted with greenhouse gases from combustion and industry, so that measures which reduce air pollution for health reasons simultaneously reduce the aerosol cooling and thereby unmask greenhouse warming, creating a coupling between air-quality policy and climate that the assessment must make explicit (*high confidence*).** The dominant anthropogenic aerosol is sulfate, formed from sulfur dioxide emitted by fossil combustion, which is strongly scattering and therefore cooling. Black carbon, emitted from incomplete combustion of fossil and biomass fuels, is strongly absorbing and therefore warming, and its deposition on snow and ice adds a further warming through albedo reduction. Organic and nitrate aerosols, and the secondary aerosols formed from precursor gases, complete a chemically diverse population whose net effect is cooling but whose composition determines the balance of scattering and absorption.

Road transport is a distinctive and growing contributor because its aerosol emissions are only partly controllable by exhaust after-treatment: the non-exhaust fraction from tyre, brake, and road-surface wear is unaffected by cleaner engines and constitutes an increasing share of the traffic-derived particulate burden as exhaust controls tighten (Fussell, 2022). This non-exhaust source is significant for both the air-quality and the climate assessment because it will persist, and may grow with vehicle mass, even as combustion emissions decline. The health burden of aerosol exposure is a major driver of the emission controls that shape the aerosol forcing trajectory, and the assessment of climate and health is increasingly integrated, recognizing that the same particles that cool the climate impose a substantial mortality burden (Romanello, 2022). This integration reframes aerosol reduction not as a climate cost to be reluctantly incurred but as a health benefit whose climate consequence must be anticipated and offset by deeper greenhouse-gas reductions.

### 4.3.4 Aerosol–cloud interactions

**Aerosol–cloud interactions constitute the most uncertain element of the aerosol forcing, because the response of cloud reflectivity, amount, and lifetime to changes in aerosol loading involves microphysical and dynamical processes that operate below the resolution of global models and that observations constrain only indirectly (*medium confidence*).** The first aerosol indirect effect, in which a fixed amount of cloud water is distributed among more numerous but smaller droplets when aerosol loading rises, brightening the cloud, is comparatively well understood and robustly cooling. The subsequent adjustments—changes in cloud lifetime as smaller droplets suppress precipitation, changes in cloud amount, and changes in cloud-top height—are far less certain and can partially offset or amplify the initial brightening. Because these adjustments occur in the same low marine cloud regimes that dominate the low-cloud feedback uncertainty discussed in Section 4.2, the aerosol–cloud problem and the cloud-feedback problem are physically entangled: the same stratocumulus decks whose response to warming is uncertain also mediate the aerosol indirect effect.

Progress has come from combining satellite observations of natural aerosol–cloud covariability with high-resolution modelling and with data-driven methods that can extract robust relationships from large and noisy observational archives while respecting physical constraints (Willard, 2022). These approaches have tended to reduce the magnitude of the more extreme negative aerosol–cloud forcings that some earlier estimates supported, narrowing the aerosol forcing from its most negative tail and, by the coupling described above, tightening the historical constraint on sensitivity. The in-situ characterization of Arctic aerosol–cloud–surface coupling has further constrained how aerosol populations influence cloud radiative properties in a regime where the surface-albedo feedback makes cloud effects especially consequential (Shupe, 2022).

### 4.3.5 Novel materials in the atmospheric burden

**The atmospheric aerosol population now includes anthropogenic novel materials—microplastics and persistent synthetic compounds—whose radiative and biogeochemical roles are only beginning to be quantified but whose ubiquity, including in the remote Arctic, indicates a globally distributed and growing component of atmospheric composition (*low to medium confidence*).** Plastic debris fragments into micro- and nano-scale particles that are transported atmospherically over long distances and deposited even in regions remote from any source, so that plastic pollution is now documented throughout the Arctic environment (Bergmann, 2022). The environmental footprint of the plastics that give rise to these particles is itself climate-relevant, being driven substantially by the combustion of coal in their production, which ties the novel-materials burden back to the fossil-fuel emissions that dominate the greenhouse forcing (Cabernard, 2021).

The persistence of certain synthetic compounds compounds the concern: per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances are mobile, bioaccumulative, and effectively non-degrading, so that their environmental burden is monotonically increasing and their eventual biogeochemical consequences are, by the nature of persistent compounds, difficult to bound (Brunn, 2023). While the direct radiative forcing of these novel materials is at present assessed as small, their significance for the process understanding lies in their coupling to the biogeochemical cycles treated in the next section: microplastics alter soil carbon cycling and can perturb the terrestrial processes that govern the land sink, so that a material introduced for reasons unrelated to climate acquires a climate relevance through its interference with biogeochemistry (Rillig, 2021).

## 4.4 The Carbon and Biogeochemical Cycles

### 4.4.1 The contemporary carbon budget

**The global carbon budget is now closed to within a few percent on an annual basis, establishing that the atmosphere, ocean, and land partition anthropogenic emissions in a ratio that has remained broadly stable while the absolute fluxes have grown, and this partitioning is the single most important constraint on the coupling between emissions and climate (*high confidence*).** The budget is constructed by reconciling estimates of fossil-fuel and land-use emissions against the measured atmospheric growth rate and against independent estimates of the ocean and land sinks, with the residual providing a check on the internal consistency of the whole. The consistent finding across successive syntheses is that the ocean and the land together remove roughly half of what is emitted, so that only about half accumulates in the atmosphere; this airborne fraction is a derived quantity of central importance, because its stability under rising emissions is what has allowed the atmospheric burden to track cumulative emissions so closely (Friedlingstein, 2023). The most recent update extends this record and confirms that neither sink has yet saturated in a manner that would sharply raise the airborne fraction, even as both show signs of the sensitivity to climate that the carbon-cycle feedback anticipates (Friedlingstein, 2025).

Independent monitoring of the emission side has advanced to the point where global carbon dioxide emissions can be tracked at near-real-time resolution, using activity data and atmospheric measurements to detect the response of emissions to economic and policy perturbations within the year in which they occur (Liu, 2022). This capacity matters for the process understanding because it closes the loop between the emission drivers, mapped by sector (Lamb, 2021), and the atmospheric outcome, so that the carbon budget is no longer a retrospective reconstruction but an operational monitoring system. The convergence of top-down atmospheric constraints and bottom-up activity accounting has narrowed the emissions term of the budget and thereby sharpened the inference of the sink terms that are obtained as residuals.

### 4.4.2 The terrestrial carbon sink and its vulnerability

**The terrestrial biosphere is at present a net sink for carbon, dominated by the world's forests, but this sink is sustained against rising disturbance and its future persistence is the largest single uncertainty in the land carbon-cycle feedback (*medium to high confidence*).** The land sink arises because the fertilizing effect of elevated carbon dioxide, together with nitrogen deposition and, in some regions, a lengthening growing season, has stimulated plant growth faster than warming and disturbance have released carbon through respiration, fire, and mortality. Dedicated global assessment confirms that the forest sink has endured, but it also makes clear that its endurance is the net outcome of a growing gross uptake competing against a growing gross loss, a balance that cannot be assumed to hold as the loss terms strengthen (Pan, 2024). The unified quantification of the terrestrial biosphere's productivity from satellite observation provides the spatial and temporal detail needed to track where the sink is strengthening and where it is faltering.

The vulnerability of the sink is expressed through several converging lines of evidence. Statistical analysis of vegetation dynamics reveals a widespread decline in forest resilience—a lengthening of the time forests take to recover from perturbation—across large regions subject to warming and increasing water limitation, which is the signature of a system approaching a threshold beyond which the sink could weaken or reverse (Forzieri, 2022). The mechanistic expression of this loss of resilience is elevated tree mortality, which has been documented worldwide and which sometimes takes the form of unexpected, rapid die-off events that no incremental model of forest decline anticipated (Hartmann, 2022). Compounding the direct climatic stress, erosion degrades the soils on which forests and other terrestrial ecosystems depend, reducing the microbial diversity, network complexity, and multifunctionality that underpin nutrient cycling and carbon stabilization (Qiu, 2021). The microclimatic buffering that intact forests provide is itself eroded as canopies thin, removing the thermal insulation that had partially protected forest-floor processes from the free-atmosphere warming (Frenne, 2021).

### 4.4.3 Soil biogeochemistry and the residence time of carbon

**The residence time of carbon in soils, which determines how much of the terrestrial carbon stock is durable and how much is vulnerable to release, is governed at the process level by the interaction of organic matter with mineral surfaces and by the ecology of the microbial community that mediates decomposition, and the modern understanding has shifted decisively toward these mechanistic controls and away from the older view based on intrinsic molecular recalcitrance (*high confidence*).** The stabilization of organic carbon in soil is now understood to depend less on the chemical structure of the organic molecules themselves than on their physical and chemical association with minerals, which protects them from microbial access. The dynamic interactions at the mineral–organic matter interface—adsorption, occlusion within aggregates, and the formation of organo-mineral complexes—are the master control on how long carbon persists in soil, and disturbances that break these associations can convert a long-lived stock into a rapidly respired flux (Kleber, 2021).

The microbial community is the agent through which these controls are exercised, and its ecology—the life, death, and turnover of microbial biomass—determines both the rate of decomposition and the formation of the stable, mineral-associated compounds that constitute the durable carbon pool (Sokol, 2022). This reframing has important consequences for the carbon-cycle feedback, because it implies that the temperature sensitivity of soil carbon release depends not on a single activation energy but on the interplay of microbial physiology, substrate availability, and mineral protection, all of which respond to warming and moisture change in ways that models are only beginning to represent. Novel-material contamination adds a further perturbation: microplastics in soil alter the physical structure and the microbial processing of organic matter, changing carbon-cycling rates in directions that depend on the polymer, the concentration, and the soil type (Rillig, 2021). Salinization, projected to expand across large agricultural regions under a changing climate, degrades soil function and reduces the capacity of affected soils to support the plant growth and microbial activity on which carbon storage depends (Hassani, 2021).

### 4.4.4 The ocean carbon sink and marine biogeochemistry

**The ocean is a major and persistent sink for anthropogenic carbon, taking up carbon both through the physical solubility of carbon dioxide in seawater and through the biological pump that exports organic matter to depth, and its uptake is being reshaped by warming, stratification, and shifting biological productivity in ways that the carbon budget resolves at the aggregate level but that require process understanding to project (*high confidence*).** The solubility pump weakens as the surface ocean warms, because warmer water holds less dissolved gas, and as the accumulation of carbon in the surface layer reduces the chemical capacity of seawater to absorb more. The biological pump depends on the productivity of phytoplankton, which fix carbon in the sunlit surface and export a fraction of it to depth when they sink or are consumed. Changes in this productivity therefore feed directly into the ocean sink, and observations reveal that coastal phytoplankton blooms have expanded and intensified over recent decades, a large-scale reorganization of marine productivity with consequences for carbon export that are not yet fully quantified (Dai, 2023).

The delivery of nutrients that limit marine productivity couples the ocean carbon cycle to the atmospheric dust cycle discussed in Section 4.3, because atmospheric deposition supplies iron and other micronutrients to the open ocean regions where they are scarce (Kok, 2021). This coupling means that changes in aridity and land use, by altering the dust flux, can propagate into the ocean carbon sink through the fertilization or starvation of phytoplankton. The broader significance of the marine biogeochemical changes is that they occur alongside the physical stressors of warming, acidification, and deoxygenation, so that the ocean carbon sink is being modified by several simultaneous perturbations whose combined effect on future uptake is a principal uncertainty in the long-term carbon-cycle feedback.

### 4.4.5 Inland waters and the lateral carbon flux

**Rivers, lakes, and other inland waters are not passive conduits but active processors of carbon, respiring, storing, and outgassing a flux that is large relative to the net land sink, and the recognition of this lateral pathway has revised the understanding of how terrestrial carbon reaches the atmosphere and ocean (*high confidence*).** Carbon fixed on land is exported to inland waters, where a substantial fraction is respired back to the atmosphere or buried in sediments before the remainder reaches the sea, so that the river network functions as a biogeochemical reactor whose metabolism is a significant term in the global carbon budget (Battin, 2023). This lateral flux has historically been under-represented in carbon accounting, and its incorporation changes the interpretation of the land sink: some of what appears as terrestrial uptake is in transit through the aquatic network rather than permanently stored.

The metabolism of these inland waters is sensitive to the same climatic drivers that affect the land, and it is being perturbed by warming directly. The occurrence of heatwaves in lakes, analogous to atmospheric and marine heatwaves, has increased under warming and alters the thermal structure, oxygen content, and biological activity of these water bodies, with consequences for their carbon processing and greenhouse-gas emission (Woolway, 2021). Because inland waters emit both carbon dioxide and methane, and because methane has a disproportionate forcing effect, changes in the metabolism of the aquatic network feed back onto the atmospheric composition assessed in Section 4.3. The rice paddy, a managed inland-water system, is a further illustration of the coupling, being a major anthropogenic methane source whose emissions and mitigation potential are now quantified in detail (Qian, 2023).

### 4.4.6 Coupling across the biogeochemical cycles

**The carbon cycle cannot be assessed in isolation from the nitrogen, phosphorus, and other nutrient cycles that co-limit biological productivity, because the capacity of the land and ocean sinks to respond to rising carbon dioxide is constrained by the availability of these nutrients, and the human perturbation of the nutrient cycles is now of a magnitude comparable to the natural fluxes (*high confidence*).** The fertilizing effect of elevated carbon dioxide on plant growth, which underpins the land sink, cannot be sustained indefinitely if nitrogen or phosphorus becomes limiting, so the strength of the carbon-cycle feedback depends on the coupled dynamics of the nutrient cycles. Human activity has roughly doubled the reactive-nitrogen input to the biosphere through fertilizer production and combustion, and this nitrogen both fertilizes carbon uptake and, when it drives emissions of nitrous oxide, adds a potent greenhouse forcing of its own. The transgression of the biogeochemical-flow planetary boundaries, of which the nitrogen and phosphorus boundaries are the clearest examples, signals that these cycles are now operating far outside their pre-industrial range (Richardson, 2023).

The systemic view that emerges is one in which the carbon cycle, the nutrient cycles, the dust cycle, and the water cycle are mutually coupled, so that a perturbation to one propagates to the others. This coupling is the physical basis for the recognition that the climate and biodiversity crises are not separable problems but manifestations of a single disruption of the biosphere's regulatory processes, and that the loss of biological diversity itself undermines the functioning of the cycles that regulate the climate (Pörtner, 2023). The framing of the human enterprise as embedded within, and dependent upon, the biosphere's biogeochemical functioning makes explicit that the process understanding assembled in this chapter is ultimately an account of the life-support system on which human welfare rests (Folke, 2021).

## 4.5 Water Cycle Processes

### 4.5.1 Thermodynamic intensification of the water cycle

**The water cycle is intensifying as the atmosphere warms, following at the largest scale the thermodynamic constraint that a warmer atmosphere holds more water vapor, so that the wet-get-wetter and dry-get-drier tendency of the moisture transport is a robust expectation now increasingly confirmed by observation (*high confidence*).** The saturation vapor pressure of water rises steeply with temperature, at approximately seven percent per degree C, and because relative humidity remains near-constant, the actual water-vapor content of the atmosphere rises at a similar rate. This enhanced moisture loading has several consequences that together constitute the intensification of the cycle: the moisture available for precipitation extremes increases, the transport of water from evaporative to convergent regions strengthens, and the atmospheric demand for water—the evaporative pull on surfaces—rises. The rise in atmospheric moisture is the same process that drives the water-vapor feedback assessed in Section 4.2, so the intensification of the water cycle and the amplification of warming are two expressions of a single thermodynamic fact.

The global-mean precipitation does not rise as steeply as the water-vapor content, however, because precipitation is constrained by the energy budget rather than by moisture availability: the atmosphere can only precipitate as much as it can radiatively cool to balance the latent heat released. This energetic constraint means that global precipitation rises at a slower rate than moisture, on the order of one to three percent per degree C, which implies a lengthening of the residence time of water vapor and a tendency toward more intense but less frequent precipitation. The gap between the fast rise of extreme precipitation, governed by moisture, and the slow rise of mean precipitation, governed by energy, is a central organizing principle of the water-cycle assessment and is robust across observational and modelling evidence.

### 4.5.2 Drought, aridity, and evaporative demand

**Rising evaporative demand under warming is increasing the frequency, intensity, and speed of onset of droughts, and the anthropogenic fingerprint on the global drought record has become detectable across frequency, duration, and intensity (*high confidence*).** Drought is not a single phenomenon but a family of related conditions—meteorological drought defined by precipitation deficit, agricultural drought defined by soil-moisture deficit, and hydrological drought defined by streamflow and groundwater deficit—linked by the propagation of a water shortfall through the terrestrial system. Warming affects each stage: it raises the atmospheric demand that draws moisture from soils and vegetation, it shifts precipitation toward more intense but less frequent events that run off rather than infiltrate, and it advances snowmelt so that the seasonal water supply arrives earlier and is exhausted sooner. Formal attribution now finds evidence of anthropogenic influence on the global statistics of drought, establishing that the observed intensification is not attributable to natural variability alone (Chiang, 2021).

A qualitatively new feature of the warming climate is the flash drought, a drought that develops with unusual rapidity, driven by a combination of precipitation deficit and high evaporative demand that desiccates soils within weeks rather than months. The transition toward more frequent flash droughts under continued warming has been demonstrated, and it poses a distinct challenge because the speed of onset compresses the window for anticipation and response (Yuan, 2023). The expansion of aridity has direct consequences for terrestrial biogeochemistry through the salinization of soils, which is projected to spread across large agricultural regions as evaporative concentration of salts intensifies under drier and warmer conditions (Hassani, 2021). Drought and aridity thus link the water-cycle assessment to the carbon-cycle vulnerability discussed in Section 4.4, because water stress is a principal driver of the declining forest resilience and elevated tree mortality that threaten the land sink (Forzieri, 2022; Hartmann, 2022).

### 4.5.3 Extremes, attribution, and compound events

**The attribution of individual extreme weather and hydrological events to anthropogenic warming has matured into a quantitative science, establishing that many observed extremes have been made more likely or more intense by the human perturbation of the climate, and that compound events—the coincidence of multiple hazards—are a growing concern (*high confidence*).** The attribution of extremes proceeds by comparing the probability of an event in the present climate with its probability in a counterfactual climate without anthropogenic forcing, using large ensembles of model simulations conditioned on the observed record. Applied across a wide range of events, this approach has shown that the intensification of heat extremes, heavy precipitation, and certain droughts carries a detectable anthropogenic signal, and it provides the methodological bridge between the global process understanding and the specific events that societies experience (Clarke, 2022).

Compound events are especially consequential because their impacts exceed the sum of their parts: a heatwave coinciding with a drought, or heavy precipitation falling on a saturated catchment, produces effects disproportionate to either hazard alone. The intensification of the water cycle raises the probability of such coincidences by increasing the variance as well as the mean of the relevant variables. Regional expressions of these compound risks are pronounced in transitional climates: the eastern Mediterranean and Middle East, a region at the intersection of subtropical drying and rising heat, is emerging as a hotspot in which multiple extremes intensify together (Zittis, 2022). Mountain regions, warming faster than the global mean and central to the water supply of downstream populations through their snow and ice reservoirs, concentrate hydrological risk where the elevation-dependent amplification of warming accelerates the loss of the frozen water store (Pepin, 2022).

### 4.5.4 The frozen water store and inland waters

**The cryospheric component of the water cycle—snow, glaciers, and permafrost—functions as a water reservoir and a climate archive whose loss redistributes water in space and time and releases stored carbon, coupling the water cycle to the carbon cycle and the surface-albedo feedback (*high confidence*).** Seasonal snow and mountain glaciers store precipitation in the cold season and release it in the warm season, buffering the water supply of downstream regions; as warming shifts precipitation from snow to rain and accelerates melt, this natural regulation is degraded, with the water arriving earlier and the late-season supply diminishing. The rapid warming of the Arctic, at nearly four times the global rate, drives the loss of this frozen store at high latitudes and, through the surface-albedo feedback, accelerates the very warming responsible for the loss (Rantanen, 2022). The thaw of permafrost adds a carbon-cycle dimension, exposing long-frozen organic matter to microbial decomposition and potential release as carbon dioxide and methane, a process whose magnitude depends on the same mineral-organic controls that govern soil carbon more generally (Kleber, 2021), as mediated by the soil microbial community (Sokol, 2022).

The liquid inland waters that receive the meltwater are themselves being transformed. Lakes are warming and experiencing heatwaves that alter their stratification and biogeochemistry, changing their role in the carbon cycle and their emission of greenhouse gases (Woolway, 2021). The river networks that connect the terrestrial and marine domains process a large lateral flux of carbon whose magnitude is sensitive to the changing hydrology, so that the intensification of the water cycle propagates into the carbon budget through the altered delivery and processing of terrestrial carbon (Battin, 2023). These couplings reinforce the theme that the water cycle is not a separable subsystem but the medium through which many of the biogeochemical processes assessed in this chapter are transmitted.

### 4.5.5 Water resources and human coupling

**The intensification and redistribution of the water cycle interacts with rising human demand to produce increasing pressure on freshwater resources, and the assessment of water-cycle processes is incomplete without the coupled human system that abstracts, stores, and consumes water (*high confidence*).** Freshwater availability is a growing global challenge as population and economic demand rise against a supply that climate change is making more variable and, in many regions, scarcer (Mishra, 2023). The quantitative assessment of this pressure relies on global hydrological models that represent the terrestrial water balance and its human modification, tracking abstraction, storage, and return flows alongside the natural fluxes (Schmied, 2021). These models make explicit that the human perturbation of the water cycle, through irrigation, damming, and groundwater extraction, is now a first-order term in the terrestrial water balance of many regions, comparable to the climatic changes in its effect on water availability.

The coupling runs in both directions: water availability constrains agriculture, and agriculture is the dominant human water use, so that the intensification of the water cycle bears directly on food security through its effect on the water available for crops (Jägermeyr, 2021). The projected changes in agricultural productivity under a changing climate emerge earliest and most clearly where water stress is greatest, integrating the thermodynamic intensification of the cycle, the rise in evaporative demand, and the human demand for water into a single assessment of the pressure on the systems that sustain human populations (Yang, 2024). This human coupling closes the loop of the chapter, returning the physical process understanding to the biosphere-embedded framing with which the biogeochemical assessment concluded (Folke, 2021).

## 4.6 Integrating Processes: Earth-System Models, Thresholds, and Confidence

### 4.6.1 Earth-system models as the integrating framework

**Earth-system models are the framework within which the forcings, feedbacks, and cycles assessed in this chapter are combined into a physically consistent whole, and the current model generation reproduces the observed forcing history and the broad structure of the feedbacks while spanning a range of sensitivities that the assessment interprets rather than averages (*high confidence*).** A model of this class couples the atmosphere, ocean, land, cryosphere, and, increasingly, the interactive carbon and other biogeochemical cycles, so that a single simulation computes the greenhouse and aerosol forcing, the water-vapor, lapse-rate, albedo, and cloud feedbacks, and the response of the land and ocean carbon sinks, all interacting self-consistently. This self-consistency is the principal virtue of the modelling approach: it avoids the double-counting and internal contradiction that can arise when feedbacks estimated in isolation are summed, and it allows the emergent behavior of the coupled system—including the pattern effect and the interaction of feedbacks—to be represented. The representative model examined in the intercomparison, incorporating an interactive carbon cycle within a full Earth-system configuration, illustrates both the achievement and the residual spread of the current generation (Lovato, 2022).

The intercomparison of many such models under common experimental protocols is the mechanism by which structural uncertainty is characterized. Because the models differ in their parameterizations of the sub-grid processes that dominate the feedback uncertainty—convection, cloud microphysics, aerosol activation, and soil biogeochemistry—their spread is an estimate, if an imperfect one, of the uncertainty attributable to incomplete process representation. The assessment does not treat the multi-model mean as truth; rather, it weights the models by their fidelity to observed processes and applies emergent constraints that relate an observable feature of the present climate to a feedback or sensitivity, thereby extracting more information than the raw ensemble spread provides. The scenario framework overlaid on these models separates the physical uncertainty they characterize from the societal uncertainty of the emissions pathway, providing internally consistent forcing trajectories against which the physical response is projected (Tebaldi, 2021).

### 4.6.2 Data-driven methods and the augmentation of process models

**The integration of physical process knowledge with data-driven and machine-learning methods is emerging as a means to constrain the parameterizations that dominate the feedback uncertainty, and this hybrid approach is beginning to complement, rather than replace, the physically based models (*medium confidence*).** The processes that dominate the residual uncertainty—low-cloud response, aerosol activation, and soil-carbon turnover—are precisely those that occur below the resolution of global models and must be parameterized from limited observations. Methods that integrate scientific knowledge with machine learning can extract feedback-relevant relationships from large observational and high-resolution modelling archives while respecting the physical constraints that keep the inferences credible, offering a route to reduce the parameterization uncertainty that pure process modelling has struggled to close (Willard, 2022). The value of these methods for the assessment lies in their capacity to bring more of the available observational information to bear on the specific relationships that matter for the feedbacks, and their limitation lies in the need to ensure that the learned relationships generalize to a climate state outside the range of the training data.

The broader monitoring enterprise on which both the process models and the data-driven methods depend has been transformed by the growth of observational capacity, from the near-real-time tracking of emissions (Liu, 2022) to the unified satellite quantification of the terrestrial biosphere and the year-round in-situ characterization of the polar atmosphere (Shupe, 2022). This observational foundation is what allows the process understanding to be tested rather than merely asserted, and its continued extension is a precondition for the further narrowing of the uncertainties that this chapter has identified.

### 4.6.3 Thresholds, resilience, and the risk of abrupt change

**The process understanding assembled in this chapter identifies several elements of the climate system whose response to forcing may be abrupt or effectively irreversible on human timescales, and while the assessment does not find that any global-scale abrupt transition is imminent, it establishes that the risk of such transitions rises with the magnitude and rate of forcing (*medium confidence*).** The feedbacks that dominate the sensitivity are, for the most part, smoothly varying, but several of the processes coupled to them exhibit threshold behavior: the loss of forest resilience that could convert a carbon sink into a source (Forzieri, 2022), the thaw of permafrost that could release a large stored carbon pool, the reorganization of marine productivity implied by the expansion of coastal blooms (Dai, 2023), and the loss of ice whose albedo feedback is self-reinforcing (Rantanen, 2022). The common feature of these elements is that they involve a positive feedback strong enough, locally, to sustain a transition once initiated, so that the system does not return to its prior state when the forcing is reduced.

The assessment of these risks draws on the recognition that the Earth system has been pushed beyond several of the boundaries within which its Holocene stability was maintained (Richardson, 2023), and it takes seriously the analysis of high-end and catastrophic scenarios that, while not the most likely outcomes, carry consequences severe enough to warrant explicit assessment (Kemp, 2022). The framing of these boundaries to encompass both the biophysical thresholds and the just distribution of the remaining safe operating space reflects the maturation of the field from a purely physical science toward one that integrates the physical with the societal consequences of transgression (Rockström, 2023). The coupling of the climate and biodiversity crises, in which the loss of biological diversity degrades the very processes that regulate the climate, underscores that the thresholds of concern are not confined to the physical system but extend to the living processes on which its regulation depends (Pörtner, 2023).

### 4.6.4 Confidence, uncertainty, and the state of process understanding

**Taken as a whole, the process understanding of forcings, feedbacks, and cycles has reached a state in which the sign and approximate magnitude of every major process is established with high confidence, the residual uncertainties are concentrated in a small number of identified feedbacks and couplings, and the direction of ongoing research is set by the need to constrain those specific residuals (*high confidence*).** The greenhouse-gas forcing is known with the highest confidence of any term; the aerosol forcing remains the dominant forcing uncertainty and is coupled through the historical record to the uncertainty in sensitivity; the cloud and carbon-cycle feedbacks remain the dominant feedback uncertainties, though both have narrowed; and the water cycle's intensification is robustly established while its regional expression retains substantial uncertainty. This structure of confidence—high on the aggregate, more qualified on the specific residuals—is not a weakness of the assessment but a mark of its maturity, because it identifies precisely where further effort will yield the greatest reduction in the uncertainty of projection.

The economic and societal metrics that translate this physical understanding into decision-relevant terms inherit its uncertainty structure: the social cost of carbon, resting on the forcing-to-warming-to-damage chain, is sensitive to the sensitivity and carbon-cycle uncertainties assessed here, and its comprehensive re-estimation to a substantially higher value reflects both updated damage understanding and the physical relationships this chapter has assessed (Rennert, 2022). The integration of the climate and health assessments, recognizing that the aerosols which cool the climate also impose a mortality burden, exemplifies the coupling of the physical and the societal that the process understanding now supports (Romanello, 2022). The overarching conclusion of this chapter is that the physical processes governing the climate response to human forcing are understood well enough to support confident projection of the direction and approximate magnitude of change, that the coupled carbon, biogeochemical, and hydrological cycles transmit and amplify that change through mechanisms now resolved at the process level, and that the remaining uncertainties, while real and consequential, are bounded, identified, and the subject of active and converging lines of investigation (Folke, 2021).

## 4.7 Detection, Attribution, and the Human Fingerprint on Processes

### 4.7.1 From global-mean detection to process-level attribution

**The attribution of climate change has progressed from the detection of a warming trend in the global-mean temperature to the attribution of specific process changes—in the water cycle, the carbon cycle, and the frequency of extremes—to human forcing, and this maturation is what allows process understanding to be tested against the observed record rather than assessed only in models (*high confidence*).** Detection establishes that an observed change exceeds what internal variability alone would produce; attribution assigns the change to a cause by comparing the observed record against simulations with and without the anthropogenic forcing. Applied to the water cycle, this framework has attributed to human influence the observed changes in the global statistics of drought across frequency, duration, and intensity, moving the assessment of the hydrological response from expectation to established fact (Chiang, 2021). Applied to extremes, it has established that a wide class of heat and precipitation events has been made more likely or more intense by warming, providing the empirical counterpart to the thermodynamic reasoning of Section 4.5 (Clarke, 2022).

The attribution enterprise depends on the same Earth-system models that integrate the process understanding, because the counterfactual climate against which the observed record is compared can only be constructed by simulation. This creates a productive circularity: the models are tested by their ability to reproduce the detected changes, and the attribution is strengthened by the models' fidelity to the underlying processes. The wide-ranging review of climate change impacts, adaptation, and mitigation measures situates these attributed process changes within the fuller account of how the physical changes propagate into human and natural systems, underscoring that the process understanding of this chapter is the foundation on which impact assessment rests (Abbass, 2022).

### 4.7.2 Regional heterogeneity as a signature of process

**The regional heterogeneity of the observed changes is itself a signature of the underlying processes, because the pattern of change reflects the spatial structure of the forcings and feedbacks rather than a uniform global response, and the assessment reads this heterogeneity as evidence about mechanism (*high confidence*).** The Arctic warms fastest because the surface-albedo feedback and the associated atmospheric processes are concentrated there (Rantanen, 2022); the mountains warm in an elevation-dependent pattern because the retreat of snow and the changing energy balance vary with altitude (Pepin, 2022); and the eastern Mediterranean and Middle East emerge as a hotspot because subtropical drying and rising heat coincide in that region (Zittis, 2022). Each of these regional signals is a test of the process understanding: a model that reproduces the global mean but fails the regional pattern has captured the aggregate energetics while missing the mechanisms, and the assessment weights models accordingly.

This reading of heterogeneity extends to the biogeochemical response. The spatial pattern of the terrestrial carbon sink, resolved by satellite quantification of the biosphere, reveals where the sink is strengthening under carbon dioxide fertilization and where it is faltering under water stress and disturbance, and the pattern of declining forest resilience identifies the regions in which the land carbon-cycle feedback is most likely to strengthen first (Forzieri, 2022). The regional structure of the response is therefore not noise to be averaged away but information to be exploited, and its incorporation into the assessment is one of the advances that distinguishes the current understanding from the global-mean framing of earlier cycles.

## 4.8 From Process Understanding to Committed Change and Reversibility

### 4.8.1 Cumulative emissions, committed warming, and the net-zero concept

**The near-linear relationship between cumulative carbon dioxide emissions and global-mean warming, which emerges from the combination of the forcing and carbon-cycle processes assessed in this chapter, is the physical basis for the concept of a carbon budget and for the finding that stabilizing temperature requires reaching net-zero carbon dioxide emissions (*high confidence*).** The linearity arises from a coincidental compensation between two processes: as cumulative emissions rise, the logarithmic saturation of the carbon dioxide forcing would tend to reduce the warming per tonne, while the declining efficiency of the carbon sinks—the carbon-cycle feedback—tends to raise the airborne fraction and increase the warming per tonne. These effects approximately cancel, producing a relationship robust enough to underpin policy. The implication is that warming is controlled by the total quantity of carbon emitted rather than by the rate, and that halting the rise in temperature requires bringing net carbon dioxide emissions to zero. The precise meaning of net zero, and the conditions under which it stabilizes temperature, have been clarified in a manner that makes explicit the roles of the residual emissions and the removals that must balance them (Fankhauser, 2021).

The carbon-cycle processes assessed in Section 4.4 are central to this framing because the removals that define net zero depend on the same land and ocean sinks whose vulnerability the chapter has documented. A sink that weakens under warming reduces the natural drawdown and increases the removal effort required to reach net zero, so the carbon-cycle feedback is not merely a physical uncertainty but a determinant of the mitigation challenge. The reliance on the land sink also brings the biogeophysical processes into the policy assessment, because efforts to enhance terrestrial removal interact with the water, nutrient, and biodiversity processes that govern ecosystem functioning (Seddon, 2021).

### 4.8.2 Reversibility, irreversibility, and the asymmetry of the response

**The reversibility of the climate response to a reduction in forcing varies sharply among processes, so that some elements of the system would recover on decadal timescales while others—ice loss, permafrost carbon release, and certain ecosystem transitions—are effectively irreversible on human timescales, and this asymmetry is a central conclusion of the process understanding (*high confidence*).** The surface temperature response to a cessation of emissions is expected to stabilize rather than reverse, because the ocean continues to take up heat even as it takes up less carbon, and the two adjustments approximately offset. The fast components of the water cycle would respond to a stabilized temperature, but the slow components—the deep ocean, the great ice sheets, and the permafrost carbon pool—carry a commitment that persists long after emissions cease. The loss of ice, once the albedo feedback has driven a transition, does not reverse when the forcing is reduced, because the darker surface continues to absorb the radiation that maintains the warmer state (Rantanen, 2022).

Ecosystem transitions exhibit a similar asymmetry. A forest that has crossed a resilience threshold and transitioned to a different state does not automatically return when the stress is relieved, because the soil, hydrology, and biotic community that supported the forest have themselves been altered (Forzieri, 2022; Hartmann, 2022). This asymmetry between the ease of transgression and the difficulty of recovery is the physical basis for a precautionary stance toward the thresholds identified in Section 4.6, and it is reflected in the assessment's attention to the pathways that keep the system within the range where its recovery remains possible. The integration of the physical process understanding with the sustainable-development framing makes explicit that the choices which determine whether these thresholds are crossed are made within a narrow and closing window, and that the process understanding assembled here is what defines the boundaries of that window (Soergel, 2021).

### 4.8.3 Synthesis: a coupled system understood at the process level

**The cumulative advance represented in this chapter is the transition from a description of the climate response in terms of aggregate quantities to an account grounded in the specific, separately constrained processes that generate those quantities, and it is this process-level grounding that underwrites the confidence of the projections built upon it (*high confidence*).** The forcing is no longer a single number but a decomposition into agents whose individual histories are traced through the emissions that produce them (Lamb, 2021) and monitored through the atmospheric growth they cause (Liu, 2022). The sensitivity is no longer an empirical fit but the propagated sum of feedbacks each constrained by observation and process modelling, with the cloud feedback narrowed by the integration of physical knowledge with data-driven inference (Willard, 2022) and the carbon-cycle feedback constrained by the annual closure of the global budget (Friedlingstein, 2023). The cycles are no longer boundary conditions but interactive components whose couplings—dust to ocean productivity, water to carbon, nutrients to the land sink—are represented within the coupled models that integrate them (Lovato, 2022).

This process grounding also clarifies where the understanding remains incomplete and why. The residual uncertainties are not distributed uniformly but concentrated in the small-scale processes that must be parameterized: the microphysics of low clouds, the activation of aerosols into cloud droplets, and the microbial and mineral controls on soil carbon turnover (Kleber, 2021). Each of these is the subject of a dedicated observational and modelling effort, and the trajectory of the field is toward their progressive constraint rather than toward any wholesale revision of the framework. The observational foundation that makes this constraint possible—the near-real-time emissions monitoring, the year-round polar campaigns (Shupe, 2022), and the satellite records of the biosphere and the water cycle—has been strengthened to the point where the process understanding can be tested against the observed evolution of the system rather than asserted from theory alone.

The chapter's final judgment is therefore one of qualified confidence with a clear structure. The direction and approximate magnitude of the climate response to human forcing are established beyond reasonable scientific doubt; the coupled carbon, biogeochemical, and hydrological cycles transmit and in several respects amplify that response through mechanisms now resolved at the process level; and the risks of abrupt or irreversible change, while not assessed as imminent at the global scale, rise with the magnitude and rate of forcing and are concentrated in identifiable elements of the system (Forzieri, 2022). The economic translation of this understanding implies damages, and therefore a social cost of carbon, substantially larger than earlier assessments recognized (Rennert, 2022), and its ecological translation makes plain that the climate and biodiversity crises are a single disruption of the biosphere's regulatory processes rather than two separable problems (Pörtner, 2023). The process understanding assembled here is, in the end, an account of the mechanisms by which a habitable Earth maintains itself and of the specific ways in which those mechanisms are now being perturbed (Folke, 2021).

The forward agenda that follows from this synthesis is correspondingly well defined. Constraining the aerosol forcing, and with it the historical inference of sensitivity, requires sustained observation of aerosol–cloud interactions in the low-cloud regimes that dominate the uncertainty (Willard, 2022). Bounding the carbon-cycle feedback requires continued closure of the global carbon budget together with process-level study of the disturbance mechanisms—drought-driven mortality, loss of resilience, and permafrost thaw—that threaten the durability of the land sink (Friedlingstein, 2025). Projecting the intensification of the water cycle at the regional scale where it is experienced requires the coupled human–hydrological modelling that represents abstraction and management alongside the natural fluxes (Schmied, 2021). None of these agenda items implies a revision of the framework; each is a program for reducing a specified, bounded uncertainty within a structure whose broad outline is secure. It is on this basis that the subsequent chapters build their projections of future change, their attribution of observed impacts, and their assessment of the pathways by which the perturbation of these processes may yet be constrained (Hartmann, 2022).
