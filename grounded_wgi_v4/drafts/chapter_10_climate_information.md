# Chapter 10: Climate Information for Decisions and Services

## Executive Summary

**Climate information has moved from being a scientific by-product of Earth system assessment to a distinct, decision-facing infrastructure that mediates between physical-science understanding and the choices made by governments, firms, communities, and individuals (*high confidence*).** Across the period assessed in this Report, the quantity, resolution, and accessibility of climate information have grown rapidly, but the binding constraint on its value for decisions is no longer primarily the availability of data; it is the fidelity of the processes that translate physical-climate knowledge into forms that are salient, credible, and legitimate to specific users (*high confidence*).

**The co-production of climate information — sustained collaboration between information producers and the decision-makers who use it — measurably improves the uptake and usability of that information relative to one-directional "loading dock" delivery, but co-production is resource-intensive, unevenly distributed, and difficult to sustain (*medium confidence*).** Frameworks developed in implementation science provide transferable structure for understanding why some climate-information services are adopted and sustained while others are not (Damschroder et al., 2022) (*medium confidence*).

**The observational, monitoring, and reanalysis foundation on which decision-relevant climate information rests has strengthened substantially, and global reanalyses and satellite-derived land-surface products now provide internally consistent, decision-usable representations of the recent climate at fine spatial resolution (*high confidence*).** Continuous global land-cover and soil products at 30 m and finer resolution (Zhang et al., 2021; Poggio et al., 2021) and quantitatively reconciled global carbon accounting (Friedlingstein et al., 2022) exemplify the monitoring backbone now available to decision-makers, though systematic gaps remain over parts of the Global South and for many essential variables (*high confidence*).

**Artificial intelligence and machine-learning methods have become established components of the climate-information value chain, delivering skilful short-range prediction, computationally efficient emulation, pattern extraction from large Earth-observation archives, and new modes of downscaling and bias correction (*high confidence*).** Deep generative models now produce precipitation nowcasts that expert forecasters judge more useful than established numerical alternatives (Ravuri et al., 2021), and structured reviews document machine learning's expanding role across mitigation, adaptation, and Earth-system science (Kaack et al., 2022) (*high confidence*). These gains are accompanied by well-characterized risks — brittle out-of-distribution behaviour, opaque uncertainty (Gawlikowski et al., 2023), and the non-trivial environmental and equity costs of the computation itself (Wynsberghe, 2021) (*high confidence*).

**Robust, well-calibrated communication of uncertainty is a prerequisite for climate information to support good decisions, and the calibrated-language framework used throughout this Report is one instrument among several for conveying the state of knowledge honestly to non-specialist users (*high confidence*).** Where uncertainty is suppressed, misrepresented, or presented without decision-relevant framing, climate information can degrade rather than improve decisions (*medium confidence*).

**Regional climate risk assessment is the principal interface through which the physical-science findings of this Working Group enter the impact, adaptation, and vulnerability assessments of Working Group II, and the quality of that interface depends on shared definitions of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability and on the traceable propagation of uncertainty across the boundary (*high confidence*).** Sectoral evidence — in water resources (Scanlon et al., 2023), agriculture and food security (Jägermeyr et al., 2021), its biological hazards (Singh et al., 2023), heat-related mortality (Zhao et al., 2021), and land and soil degradation (Hassani et al., 2021) — demonstrates both the maturity of hazard characterization and the persistent difficulty of converting hazard information into locally actionable risk information (*high confidence*).

**Equity, capacity, and the inclusion of Indigenous knowledge and local communities are not peripheral to the production of decision-relevant climate information but are central determinants of whether that information is trusted and used, and of whether its benefits are distributed fairly (*medium confidence*).** Evidence on conservation and land governance indicates that outcomes are more effective and more equitable where Indigenous peoples and local communities are recognized as knowledge-holders and decision-partners rather than as passive recipients (Dawson et al., 2021) (*medium confidence*).

## 10.1 Introduction: The Purpose and Scope of Climate Information for Decisions

**Climate information for decisions is the subset of climate knowledge that has been selected, processed, contextualized, and delivered specifically to inform a choice under an uncertain climate, and it is distinguished from climate data and from climate science by the presence of an identified decision and an identified decision-maker (*high confidence*).** This chapter assesses the state of the science and practice underlying that translation. It does not re-assess the physical basis of climate change, which is the subject of earlier chapters; instead it assesses how the outputs of that physical-science enterprise — observations, reanalyses, projections, and their associated uncertainties — are converted into information that can be acted upon, and what determines whether that conversion succeeds.

The distinction matters because the pathways by which climate knowledge fails to support good decisions are frequently not failures of the underlying science. A projection may be physically sound, internally consistent, and appropriately bounded by uncertainty, and yet be unusable because it is delivered at the wrong spatial scale, framed against the wrong baseline, expressed in units foreign to the user, delivered after the decision window has closed, or accompanied by uncertainty language that the user cannot interpret. Conversely, information that is scientifically modest — a seasonal tendency, a simple threshold exceedance frequency — may be highly decision-relevant when it is co-designed with the user, delivered on the user's timescale, and embedded in the user's existing decision process. The central assessment of this chapter is therefore that the value of climate information is a joint property of the information and the decision context, and cannot be evaluated by reference to scientific quality alone (*high confidence*).

### 10.1.1 What counts as a decision-relevant climate service

**A climate service is the sustained provision of climate information, in a usable form, to support decisions, and it is defined by the persistence of the provision and the usability of the product rather than by any particular scientific method (*high confidence*).** The concept spans a wide range of institutional arrangements: national meteorological and hydrological services issuing seasonal outlooks; regional climate outlook fora; commercial providers packaging reanalysis and projection data for the finance, energy, and agriculture sectors; and community-based monitoring arrangements that couple local observation with external forecast products. What unites them is a commitment to ongoing service rather than one-off assessment, and an orientation toward a defined user.

The maturation of climate services over the assessed period reflects three convergent developments. First, the underlying information base has become richer and more openly accessible, as global reanalyses, satellite Earth-observation archives, and standardized climate-model output have become routinely available. Second, the community has accumulated substantial practical experience about why climate information is or is not used, much of it consistent with insights from implementation science and the broader literature on evidence uptake (Damschroder et al., 2022). Third, computational methods — including machine learning — have lowered the cost of transforming raw information into tailored products, enabling forms of downscaling, emulation, and prediction that were previously infeasible (Kaack et al., 2022). Each of these developments is assessed in detail in the sections that follow.

### 10.1.2 The decision-first framing

**Organizing climate information around decisions rather than around scientific outputs — a "decision-first" framing — systematically changes what information is judged relevant and how its quality is evaluated (*high confidence*).** Under a science-first framing, the natural question is "what does the climate system tell us?" and quality is assessed by fidelity to the physical system. Under a decision-first framing, the question is "what does this decision need to know, and how sensitive is the decision to what we do not know?" and quality is assessed by decision-relevance and by the robustness of the decision to residual uncertainty.

The decision-first framing has several consequences that recur throughout this chapter. It privileges information about thresholds, tails, and rates of change over information about central tendencies, because many consequential decisions are governed by the crossing of critical values rather than by mean states. It elevates the importance of the baseline and the reference period, because most decisions are framed against expectations calibrated on the past. And it makes the communication of uncertainty a first-order design problem rather than a caveat, because a decision-maker's optimal action often depends more on the shape and framing of the uncertainty than on the central estimate. These themes — thresholds, baselines, and uncertainty framing — are developed in Sections 10.3, 10.5, and 10.6.

### 10.1.3 Scope, structure, and relationship to other chapters

This chapter is structured to follow the climate-information value chain from the human and institutional processes that make information usable, through the observational foundation, to the computational methods that transform it, to the communication of uncertainty, and finally to the regional risk-assessment interface with Working Group II. Section 10.2 assesses climate services and the co-production of actionable information. Section 10.3 assesses the observational, monitoring, and reanalysis foundation. Section 10.4 assesses the role of artificial intelligence and machine learning. Section 10.5 assesses the communication of uncertainty and calibrated confidence. Section 10.6 assesses regional climate risk assessment and its interface to Working Group II. Section 10.7 assesses cross-cutting questions of equity, capacity, and knowledge systems.

Throughout, the chapter maintains the calibrated-language conventions of this Report, and it is explicit about where the evidence supports strong statements and where it does not. Because the practice of climate services is comparatively young and the evidence base is heterogeneous, many of this chapter's assessments carry *medium confidence*, and the chapter is candid about the limits of what can currently be concluded.

## 10.2 Climate Services and the Co-Production of Actionable Information

**The co-production of climate information — a sustained, iterative collaboration in which producers and users jointly frame the problem, design the product, and interpret the results — improves the salience, credibility, and legitimacy of climate information and thereby its uptake, relative to one-directional delivery, though the evidence base is dominated by case studies and the effect is difficult to quantify in aggregate (*medium confidence*).** The central claim of the co-production literature is that usability is not a property that can be added to information after it is produced; it must be built in from the framing stage, because the choices that determine usability — spatial scale, variables, baseline, format, timing, and uncertainty representation — are made early and are expensive to reverse.

### 10.2.1 From the loading-dock model to co-production

The historically dominant mode of climate-information provision has been described as a "loading dock" model, in which scientific institutions produce information according to their own priorities and make it available for users to collect. This model implicitly assumes that the gap between information and use is an accessibility gap: if the information exists and can be reached, it will be used. The accumulated experience of the assessed period contradicts this assumption. Highly accessible, scientifically excellent information is frequently not used, and the reasons are rarely reducible to accessibility. They include mismatches of scale, mismatches of timing, misalignment with the decision-maker's mental model, distrust of the source, and the absence of institutional pathways through which the information can enter a decision.

Co-production reframes the problem as one of joint knowledge construction. In its stronger forms it involves users in problem definition, in the selection of variables and metrics, in the evaluation of prototype products, and in the interpretation of results, with iteration across these stages. The transferable insight from implementation science is that the adoption and sustainment of any evidence-based practice depend on characteristics of the innovation, the users, the inner and outer institutional settings, and the process of implementation itself, and that neglecting any of these domains predicts failure (Damschroder et al., 2022). Climate-information services that are technically sound but institutionally unsupported, or that impose high adoption costs on users, are predictably under-used regardless of scientific quality (*medium confidence*).

### 10.2.2 What co-production changes about the information

**Co-production tends to shift climate information toward finer spatial specificity, toward decision-relevant thresholds and impact-proximate variables, and toward explicit treatment of the baseline against which change is judged (*medium confidence*).** When users participate in framing, they tend to ask for information about the variables that directly enter their decisions — often impact-proximate quantities such as growing-degree-days, consecutive dry days, flood-relevant return periods, or heat-exposure indices — rather than the raw climate variables that models most directly produce. This pulls the information base toward integration with sectoral models and toward the derivation of tailored indices.

The agricultural sector illustrates the pattern. Decision-relevant agricultural climate information is rarely a temperature or precipitation projection in isolation; it is a projection filtered through crop physiology, pest and pathogen dynamics, soil-water balance, and management options. The assessed literature documents the sensitivity of crop outcomes to climate through coupled climate-crop modelling (Jägermeyr et al., 2021), the dependence of agricultural risk on shifting pest and pathogen pressure (Singh et al., 2023), and the interaction of climate stress with soil degradation and salinization (Hassani et al., 2021; Malhi et al., 2021). Co-produced agricultural climate services integrate these strands so that the delivered information corresponds to a decision — what to plant, when to plant, how to allocate water — rather than to a climate variable (*medium confidence*).

### 10.2.3 The costs and limits of co-production

**Co-production is resource-intensive, does not scale straightforwardly, and is unevenly available, so that its benefits accrue disproportionately to well-resourced users and regions, raising equity concerns that recur throughout this chapter (*medium confidence*).** Sustained collaboration requires time, trust, boundary-spanning personnel, and stable institutional relationships, all of which are scarce and unevenly distributed. A service co-produced with a single well-resourced user may not transfer to others, and the intensive engagement that makes co-production effective is precisely what makes it difficult to deliver at population scale.

This tension — between the demonstrated benefits of deep engagement and the need to serve many users — is one of the central unresolved problems of climate-service design. Two partial responses are visible in the assessed practice. The first is the development of intermediary or "boundary" organizations that specialize in translation and can amortize the cost of engagement across many users. The second is the use of computational methods, including machine learning, to lower the marginal cost of tailoring information once a co-produced template exists (Kaack et al., 2022). Neither response fully resolves the tension, and the chapter assesses with *medium confidence* that equitable access to co-produced climate information remains a significant unmet need, particularly in regions with weak national climate-service institutions.

### 10.2.4 Trust, credibility, and the social context of climate information

**Whether climate information is used depends heavily on whether its source is trusted, and trust is a social and institutional property that scientific quality alone does not confer (*high confidence*).** The salience-credibility-legitimacy framing that recurs in the climate-services literature captures this: information must be relevant to the decision (salient), believed to be accurate (credible), and produced through a process the user regards as fair and unbiased (legitimate). Legitimacy in particular is a property of process, not product, and is a principal reason that co-production improves uptake — the act of participating in production confers legitimacy that cannot be added afterward.

The social context also determines whose knowledge counts. Evidence from conservation and land governance indicates that information systems that recognize and incorporate the knowledge of Indigenous peoples and local communities produce outcomes that are both more effective and more equitable than systems that treat those communities only as recipients (Dawson et al., 2021). This finding, developed further in Section 10.7, implies that the design of legitimate climate services is inseparable from questions about which knowledge systems are admitted and on what terms (*medium confidence*).

## 10.3 Observations, Monitoring, and Reanalysis as the Foundation for Decision-Relevant Climate Information

**All decision-relevant climate information ultimately rests on observation, and the strengthening of the global observing, monitoring, and reanalysis enterprise over the assessed period has materially expanded what can be reliably said about the recent and current state of the climate at scales useful for decisions (*high confidence*).** Decisions are made in the present about the future, but they are anchored in an understanding of the recent past; a decision-maker's confidence in a projection is inseparable from confidence in the observational record against which models are evaluated and against which change is measured. This section assesses the observational foundation with particular attention to the properties that matter for decisions: spatial resolution, temporal continuity, internal consistency, and the honest characterization of gaps.

### 10.3.1 Reanalysis and the consistent representation of the recent climate

**Global reanalyses now provide gridded, dynamically and physically consistent estimates of many climate variables spanning decades, and they have become a primary source of decision-relevant information about the recent climate, particularly in regions where direct observations are sparse (*high confidence*).** By assimilating heterogeneous observations into a numerical model, reanalysis produces a spatially and temporally complete estimate that is internally consistent across variables — a property that raw observations, with their gaps and inhomogeneities, do not possess. For decisions, this consistency is valuable: an energy planner assessing joint wind-and-temperature extremes, or a water manager assessing the co-occurrence of high evapotranspiration and low precipitation, requires variables that are mutually consistent at each location and time.

Reanalysis is not observation, and its estimates inherit both the strengths and the biases of the assimilating model, with quality varying by variable, region, and epoch as the observing system itself changes through time. For decision use, these caveats are consequential: apparent trends in a reanalysis can reflect changes in the observing system rather than in the climate, and the reliability of derived quantities such as surface fluxes is generally lower than that of directly assimilated quantities such as upper-air temperature. The assessment is therefore that reanalyses are an indispensable but not infallible foundation for decision-relevant climate information, and that their fitness for a given decision must be evaluated against the specific variables and scales the decision requires (*high confidence*).

### 10.3.2 Earth observation and the monitoring of the land surface

**Satellite Earth observation now supports continuous, globally consistent monitoring of the land surface at spatial resolutions of tens of metres, providing decision-relevant information on land cover, land-cover change, cropland extent, soil properties, and vegetation condition (*high confidence*).** Global land-cover products at 30 m resolution derived from multi-temporal satellite imagery (Zhang et al., 2021) and globally consistent soil-property estimates with quantified spatial uncertainty (Poggio et al., 2021) exemplify the monitoring capabilities now routinely available. These products underpin a wide range of decisions: assessing exposure of land uses to climate hazards, monitoring the encroachment of agriculture onto vulnerable lands, and providing the surface characterization that hydrological and agricultural models require.

The decision value of these products depends on properties beyond nominal resolution. Temporal continuity determines whether change can be detected and attributed; documented and quantified uncertainty determines whether apparent changes are real; and consistency of classification through time determines whether a time series reflects the world or the evolving classifier. The assessed products increasingly meet these standards — for example, by providing per-pixel uncertainty (Poggio et al., 2021) — but many operational land-monitoring streams do not, and users are cautioned that a change detected in an inconsistent product may be an artefact of the monitoring system (*high confidence*).

### 10.3.3 Monitoring land-cover and land-use change for decisions

**Global land-use and land-cover change has been substantially larger and more dynamic over recent decades than earlier estimates suggested, and improved monitoring has revised upward the assessed pace of change, with direct implications for exposure and vulnerability assessment (*high confidence*).** Reconstructions drawing on long satellite archives indicate that global land-use change over recent decades is several times greater than previously estimated (Winkler et al., 2021), and that cropland expansion has accelerated in the twenty-first century, with much new cropland established on land of relatively low agricultural potential and often at the expense of natural ecosystems (Potapov et al., 2021). For decision-makers, these findings reframe the exposure side of risk: the human footprint that is exposed to climate hazards is itself changing rapidly, so that static exposure assumptions will misstate risk.

This dynamism interacts with climate hazard in ways that decision-relevant information must capture. Expansion of agriculture onto marginal land increases exposure to drought, heat, and soil degradation; conversion of natural ecosystems alters the land-surface properties that mediate local climate and hydrology; and the concentration of new cropland in particular regions changes the geography of food-system vulnerability (Potapov et al., 2021; Winkler et al., 2021). Climate information that treats exposure as fixed will systematically misjudge these coupled hazard-exposure dynamics, and the assessment is that decision-relevant risk information increasingly requires the joint monitoring of climate and land change (*high confidence*).

### 10.3.4 Monitoring the carbon cycle and constraining the trajectory

**Quantitative, regularly updated accounting of the global carbon budget provides decision-makers with an authoritative, uncertainty-bounded measure of the emissions trajectory against which mitigation decisions can be evaluated (*high confidence*).** The reconciliation of anthropogenic emissions with the growth of atmospheric carbon dioxide and the uptake by land and ocean sinks (Friedlingstein et al., 2022) constitutes one of the most policy-relevant monitoring products in climate science: it converts the abstract goal of emissions reduction into a measured quantity that can be tracked annually and attributed to sources. For decisions about mitigation, this monitoring is the equivalent of a financial account against which progress is audited.

The carbon-budget framing also structures the meaning of long-term goals. The interpretation of "net zero" — what it requires, over what boundary, and with what treatment of residual emissions and removals — is a question about how monitored quantities map onto policy commitments, and the assessed literature emphasizes that ambiguity in these definitions can undermine the integrity of the goals themselves (Fankhauser et al., 2021). Decision-relevant carbon information therefore requires not only measurement but agreed accounting conventions, and the assessment is that the monitoring infrastructure is now sufficient to track the global trajectory with useful precision, while the translation of that trajectory into accountable commitments remains contested (*high confidence*).

### 10.3.5 Observing gaps and the geography of information poverty

**The global observing system remains spatially uneven, with the density and continuity of in-situ observation markedly lower across much of the Global South, and this unevenness propagates into every downstream product, so that decision-relevant climate information is systematically weaker precisely where vulnerability is often highest (*high confidence*).** Reanalyses depend on assimilated observations and are less well constrained where those observations are sparse; satellite products, though globally uniform in coverage, still require in-situ data for calibration and validation; and derived services inherit the resulting geography of confidence. The result is a pattern in which the regions with the greatest exposure to climate hazards and the least institutional capacity to respond are also the regions where the underlying information is weakest.

This "information poverty" is not merely a scientific inconvenience; it is a determinant of adaptive capacity, and it compounds the equity concerns raised in Sections 10.2 and 10.7. The assessment is that closing observing-system gaps in under-observed regions would yield disproportionate improvements in decision-relevant information there, and that the value of such investment is high relative to its cost, although it is realized only if accompanied by the institutional capacity to convert observations into services (*high confidence*). Machine-learning methods, assessed in Section 10.4, offer partial mitigation by extracting more information from existing observations and by fusing heterogeneous data sources, but they cannot manufacture information where none has been observed (*high confidence*).

### 10.3.6 Water, an integrating variable for monitoring

**Water is the variable through which climate change is most directly experienced in many decisions, and the monitoring of water resources — surface water, groundwater, and water quality — is an integrating challenge that draws on the full observational enterprise (*high confidence*).** Assessments of global water resources emphasize the central and often under-appreciated role of groundwater as a buffer against climate variability and the importance of monitoring it if water systems are to remain resilient (Scanlon et al., 2023). Because groundwater is difficult to observe directly at scale, its monitoring exemplifies the fusion of in-situ measurement, satellite gravimetry, and modelling that characterizes modern decision-relevant water information.

Water quality adds a further monitoring dimension that is frequently neglected in climate assessment but is decisive for many decisions. Degradation of water quality arises from an interacting set of natural and anthropogenic drivers, and climate change modulates several of them (Akhtar et al., 2021). Decision-relevant water information therefore increasingly integrates quantity and quality, surface and subsurface, and climate and land drivers — an integration that stresses the observing system but that is necessary if water services are to support the decisions that depend on them (*high confidence*).

## 10.4 Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning in Climate Information

**Artificial intelligence and machine learning have become established, load-bearing components of the climate-information value chain, contributing skilful short-range prediction, computationally cheap emulation of expensive models, extraction of information from large Earth-observation archives, and new approaches to downscaling and bias correction, while introducing distinctive risks that must be managed if the resulting information is to be trustworthy for decisions (*high confidence*).** The assessment in this section is that machine learning is neither a panacea nor a passing fashion in climate information; it is a set of methods with well-characterized strengths and equally well-characterized failure modes, and its responsible use for decisions depends on matching method to task and on rigorous, honest uncertainty quantification.

### 10.4.1 The landscape of machine-learning applications

**Machine-learning methods now span the climate-information value chain — from Earth-observation processing, through prediction and emulation, to the design and evaluation of mitigation and adaptation options — and structured surveys of the field document both the breadth of application and the uneven maturity across use cases (*high confidence*).** Comprehensive reviews map machine learning onto a wide range of climate-relevant problems, distinguishing applications where the methods are mature and reliable from those where they remain exploratory, and emphasizing that the appropriate role of machine learning varies sharply by task (Kaack et al., 2022). This mapping is itself decision-relevant, because it allows information producers and users to calibrate their confidence in machine-learning-derived products according to the demonstrated maturity of the underlying method.

The methodological substrate for many of these applications is the family of deep neural network architectures whose capabilities in perception and pattern recognition have advanced rapidly. Convolutional architectures for image and gridded-field analysis, reviewed comprehensively in the computer-vision literature (Zhao et al., 2024), transfer naturally to gridded climate and Earth-observation data, where spatial structure is central. The same architectures that classify natural images can classify land cover, detect features in satellite imagery, and learn spatial patterns in climate fields, and this transfer of methods from computer vision into Earth-system science accounts for much of the recent expansion of machine learning in climate information (*high confidence*).

### 10.4.2 Skilful prediction and nowcasting

**Deep generative and other machine-learning models now produce short-range forecasts — notably precipitation nowcasts — that expert users judge to be more useful than established numerical alternatives, representing one of the clearest demonstrations of decision-relevant machine-learning skill in climate information (*high confidence*).** Deep generative models of radar can produce precipitation nowcasts that are both spatially realistic and probabilistically calibrated, and in structured evaluation with professional forecasters these products were preferred to competing methods for their operational usefulness (Ravuri et al., 2021). This result is significant not only as a technical achievement but as an instance of evaluation done correctly: skill was assessed against the decisions the forecasts inform and against the judgement of the users who make those decisions, rather than against a purely statistical score.

The nowcasting case also illustrates the appropriate scope of current machine-learning prediction. The demonstrated skill is at short lead times, where the relationship between recent observations and near-future states is strong and where large training archives exist. As lead time extends and the relevant dynamics become more chaotic and less directly constrained by recent observation, the advantage of purely data-driven methods diminishes and the role of physically based models grows. The assessment is that machine-learning prediction delivers clear, decision-relevant value at short ranges and for well-observed quantities, and that its extension to longer ranges and rarer events must be evaluated case by case rather than assumed (*high confidence*).

### 10.4.3 Emulation, downscaling, and bias correction

**Machine-learning emulators can reproduce the input-output behaviour of expensive physical models at a small fraction of the computational cost, enabling large ensembles, rapid scenario exploration, and finer effective resolution, provided their behaviour outside the training distribution is treated with appropriate caution (*high confidence*).** Emulation addresses a persistent constraint on decision-relevant climate information: the computational expense of physical models limits the number of scenarios, ensemble members, and resolutions that can be explored, and thereby limits the characterization of uncertainty. By learning a fast surrogate for a slow model, machine learning can relax this constraint and support the large ensembles that robust uncertainty characterization requires.

Downscaling and bias correction are closely related applications in which machine learning maps coarse-resolution model output to the finer scales and corrected statistics that decisions require. These methods are attractive because they can capture complex, non-linear relationships between coarse predictors and fine-scale predictands that simpler statistical methods miss. They also carry a characteristic risk: a downscaling model trained on the historical relationship between scales may not preserve that relationship under a changed climate, so that a method that performs well in validation on the past can mislead when applied to a future that lies outside its training experience. The assessment is that machine-learning emulation and downscaling are valuable and increasingly standard tools, but that their out-of-distribution behaviour is their principal limitation for decision use and must be explicitly evaluated (*high confidence*).

### 10.4.4 Uncertainty quantification in machine-learning products

**Rigorous uncertainty quantification is the single most important determinant of whether machine-learning-derived climate information is fit for decisions, and the field has developed a substantial toolkit for it, but the reliable estimation of predictive uncertainty in deep networks remains technically demanding and is frequently done poorly in practice (*high confidence*).** A deep network that produces a confident point prediction with no honest estimate of its own uncertainty is dangerous for decisions, because it invites users to act on predictions that the model itself has no basis to support. Surveys of uncertainty in deep neural networks catalogue the sources of uncertainty — arising from the data, the model, and the distribution shift between training and deployment — and the methods available to estimate each, while emphasizing that no single method is adequate across all settings (Gawlikowski et al., 2023).

For decision use, the critical distinction is between uncertainty that the model can represent because it resembles something seen in training, and uncertainty that arises precisely because the deployment situation is unlike anything in training. Climate applications are especially exposed to the second kind, because the future climate is, by definition, partly outside the historical training distribution. The assessment is that machine-learning climate information should be accompanied by uncertainty estimates that explicitly account for distribution shift, that point predictions without such estimates should be treated with suspicion for consequential decisions, and that the honest communication of machine-learning uncertainty is continuous with the broader problem of uncertainty communication assessed in Section 10.5 (*high confidence*).

### 10.4.5 The environmental and equity costs of the computation

**The computation underlying machine-learning climate information carries its own environmental and equity costs, and a full accounting of the technology's contribution to climate information must weigh these costs against its benefits (*medium confidence*).** The training and deployment of large models consume energy and resources, and the concentration of the required computational capacity in a small number of well-resourced institutions and regions raises equity concerns that parallel those of the observing system. The concept of sustainable artificial intelligence distinguishes between using artificial intelligence for sustainability goals and ensuring the sustainability of the artificial intelligence itself, and argues that both must be considered together (Wynsberghe, 2021).

For climate information specifically, this framing has two implications. First, the environmental cost of a machine-learning product should be proportionate to its decision value; deploying large models for tasks that simpler methods handle adequately is difficult to justify. Second, the concentration of machine-learning capacity risks reproducing, in the domain of climate information, the same geography of information poverty documented for observations in Section 10.3, so that the benefits of machine learning accrue disproportionately to already-advantaged users. The assessment is that the responsible use of machine learning in climate information requires attention to these costs, and that the technology's net contribution to equitable decision-relevant information is not guaranteed by its technical capabilities alone (*medium confidence*).

### 10.4.6 Machine learning in impact and adaptation decisions

**Beyond prediction and emulation, machine learning increasingly informs impact assessment and adaptation decisions directly, particularly in data-rich sectors such as agriculture and the built environment, though its reliability there depends on the quality and representativeness of the training data (*high confidence*).** In agriculture, machine-learning methods are applied across yield prediction, disease and pest detection, and resource-use optimization, and comprehensive reviews document both the breadth of these applications and the persistent challenges of data quality, transferability, and interpretability (Benos et al., 2021). In the built environment, machine-vision and deep-learning methods support monitoring, inspection, and management of infrastructure exposed to climate hazards (Baduge et al., 2022).

These applications share a dependence on training data that is representative of the conditions under which decisions will be made. Where training data are abundant and representative, machine learning can deliver decision-relevant accuracy; where they are scarce, biased, or drawn from conditions unlike the decision context, the resulting products can be confidently wrong. This dependence reproduces, at the level of individual applications, the equity concern that runs through this chapter: machine learning performs best where data are richest, which is often not where vulnerability is highest. The assessment is that machine learning is a valuable and expanding contributor to impact and adaptation decisions, and that its value in any specific application is bounded by the representativeness of the data on which it was trained (*high confidence*).

## 10.5 Communicating Uncertainty and Calibrated Confidence

**The honest, well-calibrated communication of uncertainty is a necessary condition for climate information to support good decisions, and the calibrated-language framework used throughout this Report is a deliberate response to the recognized failure modes of uncertainty communication, though it is one instrument among several and is not sufficient on its own (*high confidence*).** Uncertainty is not a defect of climate information to be minimized or hidden; it is a property to be characterized and conveyed, because a decision-maker's optimal action frequently depends more on the shape and framing of the uncertainty than on the central estimate. This section assesses how uncertainty in climate information is, and should be, communicated to those who act on it.

### 10.5.1 Why uncertainty communication is a decision problem

**How uncertainty is framed and presented systematically changes the decisions that users make, so that the communication of uncertainty is itself a design problem with decision consequences, not a neutral act of reporting (*high confidence*).** The same underlying uncertainty can be expressed as a probability, a range, a set of scenarios, a qualitative confidence statement, or a visual distribution, and these expressions are not interchangeable in their effect on decisions. Framing an outcome in terms of the probability of exceeding a threshold, rather than the expected value, can change whether a protective action is taken; presenting a range without indicating where within it the estimate is most likely can lead users to treat the extremes as equally probable.

The decision-relevance of framing is heightened for the quantities that most matter in climate risk: thresholds, tails, and rates of change. Many consequential decisions are governed by the probability that a critical value is exceeded — a flood level, a temperature threshold for human health or crop viability, a design standard for infrastructure. For these decisions, information about the tail of the distribution is more valuable than information about its centre, and uncertainty communication that focuses on central tendencies can systematically under-serve the decision. The assessment is that effective uncertainty communication must be designed around the structure of the decision, and in particular around the thresholds and tails that govern it (*high confidence*).

### 10.5.2 The calibrated-language framework and its limits

**The calibrated-language framework — expressing findings with standardized confidence and likelihood terms — provides a consistent, transparent vocabulary for conveying the state of knowledge, and its consistent use across this Report is a strength, but it also has limits that must be understood by those who rely on it (*high confidence*).** The framework's principal virtue is discipline: it forces authors to distinguish their confidence in a finding from the probability of an outcome, to be explicit about both, and to apply a common standard across a heterogeneous body of evidence. For decision-makers, this consistency is valuable because it allows confidence statements to be compared across findings and chapters.

The framework also has recognized limits. Calibrated terms compress a rich underlying assessment into a small number of categories, and users who do not understand the categories can misinterpret them — treating "likely" as a precise probability, or reading "medium confidence" as a weak or dismissible finding. The terms convey the assessors' judgement but not always the full basis for it, and they can obscure the difference between uncertainty that could be reduced with more research and uncertainty that is irreducible. The assessment is that calibrated language is a necessary and effective instrument for honest communication, but that it must be accompanied by accessible explanation of what the terms mean and do not mean, and complemented by other forms of uncertainty representation where decisions require them (*high confidence*).

### 10.5.3 Deep uncertainty and decision-making under it

**For many consequential climate decisions the relevant uncertainty is deep — the probabilities themselves are contested or unknown — and in these cases the appropriate response is to shift from optimizing against a predicted future to seeking decisions that are robust across many plausible futures (*high confidence*).** Where uncertainty can be well characterized as a probability distribution, decisions can in principle be optimized against it. But for many climate decisions — those involving unprecedented conditions, contested model structures, or futures that depend on human choices — the probabilities are not reliably known, and treating them as if they were can produce decisions that are fragile to the specific assumptions made.

Robust decision-making responds to deep uncertainty by asking which choices perform acceptably across the full range of plausible futures, rather than which choice is optimal for a single predicted future. This reframing changes the information that is decision-relevant: it privileges the identification of the conditions under which a decision would fail over the estimation of a most-likely outcome, and it favours flexible, adaptive strategies that can be revised as uncertainty resolves. The assessment is that deep uncertainty is the normal condition for many long-horizon climate decisions, and that climate information which supports robustness analysis — by mapping the range of plausible futures and the vulnerabilities of candidate decisions — is often more valuable than information which offers a single sharpened prediction (*high confidence*).

### 10.5.4 Visual and narrative communication of uncertainty

**Visual and narrative representations of uncertainty can convey decision-relevant information that numerical or categorical statements cannot, but poorly designed representations can also mislead, so that the design of uncertainty visualizations is a technical discipline with decision consequences (*medium confidence*).** Visualizations that show a full distribution, an ensemble of trajectories, or the spatial pattern of confidence can communicate the shape of uncertainty directly, allowing users to see tails, multi-modality, and spatial structure that a summary statistic would hide. Narrative approaches, including the use of contrasting scenarios or storylines, can make abstract uncertainty concrete by tracing plausible pathways through it.

Both approaches carry risks. Visualizations can imply false precision, can be read selectively, and can be dominated by their most salient feature regardless of its decision-relevance; narratives can lend undue vividness to particular pathways and be mistaken for predictions. The assessment is that visual and narrative uncertainty communication are powerful and increasingly necessary complements to numerical and calibrated-language approaches, and that their design should be tested against how users actually interpret them rather than assumed to be self-evidently clear (*medium confidence*).

## 10.6 Regional Climate Risk Assessment and the Interface to Working Group II

**Regional climate risk assessment is the principal interface through which the physical-science findings of this Working Group enter the impact, adaptation, and vulnerability assessments of Working Group II, and the integrity of that interface depends on shared definitions of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability, on consistent regional framing, and on the traceable propagation of uncertainty across the boundary (*high confidence*).** The physical-science community characterizes the climate hazard; the impacts community combines that hazard with exposure and vulnerability to assess risk. Where the two communities use compatible definitions, regions, baselines, and uncertainty conventions, information flows across the interface without distortion; where they do not, the hazard information can be misapplied, and the resulting risk assessment can be neither traced back to its physical basis nor defended.

### 10.6.1 The risk framing and the division of labour

**The now-standard framing of climate risk as the interaction of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability provides a shared structure that allows the physical-science and impacts communities to divide labour without losing coherence, and its consistent application is a precondition for a well-functioning interface (*high confidence*).** Under this framing, Working Group I information principally characterizes the hazard — the occurrence and intensity of physically defined climatic events and conditions — while exposure and vulnerability are assessed primarily by Working Group II. The value of the framing is that it makes explicit which community is responsible for which component and how the components combine, so that a risk statement can be decomposed and each part traced to its source.

The framing also clarifies where the difficulties lie. Hazard characterization, though demanding, is the component for which the physical-science basis is strongest; exposure and vulnerability are more contingent, more dynamic, and more dependent on social and economic factors that lie outside this Working Group's remit. As Section 10.3 documented, exposure is itself changing rapidly as land use changes (Winkler et al., 2021; Potapov et al., 2021), so that even the components conventionally assigned to Working Group II depend on physical-science monitoring. The assessment is that the hazard-exposure-vulnerability framing is an effective organizing structure for the interface, and that its effectiveness depends on both communities maintaining compatible definitions and on explicit attention to the components, such as dynamic exposure, that straddle the boundary (*high confidence*).

### 10.6.2 Regionalization and the problem of scale

**The regionalization of climate information — its expression at the spatial scales at which impacts occur and decisions are made — is essential for the interface to Working Group II but is also where physical-science confidence is often lowest, creating a structural tension between the scale decisions require and the scale at which the science is most reliable (*high confidence*).** Impacts and decisions are local; the physical-science basis is most robust at large scales. Bridging this gap requires downscaling, regional modelling, and the careful communication of the accompanying loss of confidence, and it is precisely at the regional and local scales most relevant to Working Group II that the uncertainty in physical-science information is largest.

Regional signals of change illustrate both the maturity and the limits of regionalized information. Some regional patterns are robust and strongly constrained — the Arctic, for example, has warmed markedly faster than the global average, a regionally specific finding with high physical-science confidence and direct implications for regional risk assessment (Rantanen et al., 2022). Other regional changes, particularly in precipitation and in the frequency of specific compound events, remain less well constrained. The assessment is that the regional interface to Working Group II is best served by explicit differentiation between robust regional signals and uncertain ones, and by resisting the pressure to provide sharp local information where the physical-science basis does not support it (*high confidence*).

### 10.6.3 Sectoral hazard information and its translation to risk

**Across the sectors most important for impact assessment — water, agriculture and food security, human health, and land — hazard characterization has matured to the point where physically robust hazard information is available, but the translation of that hazard information into locally actionable risk information remains the binding constraint (*high confidence*).** In water, the assessment of global water resources and the buffering role of groundwater provides a strong hazard and resource basis (Scanlon et al., 2023), while the drivers of water-quality degradation add a hazard dimension that risk assessment must incorporate (Akhtar et al., 2021). In agriculture and food security, coupled climate-crop modelling characterizes the hazard to production (Jägermeyr et al., 2021), shifting pest and pathogen pressure adds a biological hazard dimension (Singh et al., 2023), and the interaction of climate with soil degradation and salinization compounds the hazard on the land base itself (Hassani et al., 2021; Malhi et al., 2021).

In human health, the hazard posed by non-optimal ambient temperatures is now quantified with substantial confidence, including the burden of mortality attributable to heat and cold (Zhao et al., 2021), and successive assessments of health and climate change document the widening reach of climate hazards into human well-being (Romanello et al., 2021; Romanello et al., 2022; Romanello et al., 2023). Yet in each of these sectors the same pattern holds: the hazard is characterized more confidently than the risk, because converting hazard into risk requires exposure and vulnerability information that is local, dynamic, and often unavailable. The assessment is that the sectoral hazard base for the Working Group II interface is strong and improving, and that the persistent difficulty lies in the exposure and vulnerability information needed to complete the risk picture at decision-relevant scales (*high confidence*).

### 10.6.4 Compound and cascading risks across the interface

**Compound and cascading risks — arising from the co-occurrence or sequential interaction of multiple hazards, or the propagation of impacts through interconnected systems — are among the most decision-relevant and least tractable objects at the Working Group interface, and their assessment requires physical-science and impacts information to be integrated rather than exchanged sequentially (*medium confidence*).** Many of the most consequential risks are not the product of a single hazard acting on a single exposed system but of combinations: heat and drought together stressing agriculture and water simultaneously; a sequence of events that exhausts adaptive capacity; or an impact in one system that propagates into others through economic or infrastructural linkages.

These risks strain the conventional division of labour, because the interactions that generate them cross the hazard-exposure-vulnerability boundary. Assessing them requires the physical-science characterization of joint and sequential hazards to be developed together with the impacts community's understanding of how systems respond and interconnect. The assessment is that compound and cascading risks are increasingly recognized as central to climate risk, that the information needed to assess them is at the frontier of both Working Groups' capabilities, and that a sequential, one-directional handoff of hazard information is inadequate for them — they require the same co-productive integration between scientific communities that Section 10.2 identified as necessary between scientists and decision-makers (*medium confidence*).

### 10.6.5 Transition risk and the decision environment

**The interface to decisions extends beyond physical risk to transition risk — the consequences of the societal response to climate change itself — and climate information increasingly must inform decisions in a policy and market environment that is being reshaped by mitigation, so that the decision-relevant information set includes the trajectory of the response as well as the physical hazard (*medium confidence*).** Financial and economic decisions in particular are exposed not only to physical climate hazards but to the risk arising from policy, technological, and market shifts associated with decarbonization, and the pricing of this transition risk is an active and consequential domain (Bolton et al., 2023).

Information about the response trajectory is therefore decision-relevant in its own right. The interpretation of net-zero goals shapes the policy environment against which transition risk is assessed (Fankhauser et al., 2021); the pace and cost of the energy transition and of renewable deployment under a changing climate shape the opportunity set (Osman et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2022); and the deployment of nature-based approaches, where well-founded, offers response options with co-benefits that decisions must weigh (Seddon et al., 2021). The assessment is that decision-relevant climate information now spans both physical risk and transition risk, and that the two are increasingly assessed together because many decisions are jointly exposed to them (*medium confidence*).

## 10.7 Equity, Capacity, and Knowledge Systems in the Production of Climate Information

**Equity, institutional capacity, and the inclusion of diverse knowledge systems are not adjuncts to the production of decision-relevant climate information but central determinants of whether that information is trusted, used, and fairly distributed, and they recur as cross-cutting constraints throughout this chapter (*medium confidence*).** The preceding sections identified, at successive points in the value chain, a consistent pattern: the observing system is weakest where vulnerability is highest (Section 10.3); co-production is least available where capacity is lowest (Section 10.2); and machine learning performs best where data are richest, which is often not where need is greatest (Section 10.4). This section assesses these cross-cutting equity and knowledge-system dimensions directly.

### 10.7.1 The geography of climate-information capacity

**The capacity to produce, tailor, and deliver decision-relevant climate information is distributed very unevenly, and this uneven distribution tends to reinforce rather than offset the underlying distribution of vulnerability, so that improving equitable access to climate information is itself an adaptation priority (*high confidence*).** Producing usable climate information requires not only data but institutions: national services, trained personnel, boundary organizations, and stable relationships with users. These institutional assets are concentrated in wealthier countries and regions, and their absence elsewhere means that even freely available global data cannot be converted into decision-relevant local services.

The consequence is a compounding disadvantage. Regions with high exposure and high vulnerability frequently also have the weakest information-production capacity, so that the populations most in need of decision-relevant climate information are the least well served by it. The assessment is that investment in climate-information capacity in under-served regions yields high returns for adaptation, that such investment must address institutions and personnel and not only data and technology, and that without it the benefits of advances in observation, computation, and machine learning will accrue disproportionately to those already best served (*high confidence*).

### 10.7.2 Indigenous knowledge and local communities as knowledge partners

**Indigenous peoples and local communities hold knowledge that is decision-relevant for climate adaptation and environmental management, and information systems that recognize them as knowledge-partners rather than as passive recipients tend to produce outcomes that are both more effective and more equitable (*medium confidence*).** Evidence from conservation and land governance indicates that recognizing the role, rights, and knowledge of Indigenous peoples and local communities is associated with more effective and more equitable outcomes (Dawson et al., 2021). This finding has direct implications for the design of climate information: legitimate, usable climate services in many settings depend on the inclusion of local and Indigenous knowledge about environmental conditions, seasonal patterns, and locally appropriate responses.

The inclusion of these knowledge systems is not merely instrumental. As Section 10.2 argued, legitimacy is a property of process, and information produced through processes that exclude affected communities or their knowledge is less likely to be trusted or used by them. The assessment is that the integration of Indigenous and local knowledge into climate-information systems, on terms that respect the rights and agency of the knowledge-holders, improves both the effectiveness and the legitimacy of those systems, and that its neglect is a recurrent cause of the failure of externally produced information to be adopted locally (*medium confidence*).

### 10.7.3 The distributional consequences of climate information

**Climate information is not distributionally neutral: who receives it, in what form, and through which institutions shapes who is able to act, so that the design and delivery of climate information have equity consequences that must be assessed alongside its scientific quality (*medium confidence*).** The same information delivered through channels that reach some users and not others, or in forms accessible to some and not others, will advantage the connected and the well-resourced. Because the capacity to act on climate information is itself unevenly distributed, even equitably delivered information can produce unequal outcomes if the ability to respond is unequal.

This distributional dimension connects climate information to the broader equity concerns of climate response. The health burden of climate hazards falls unequally (Romanello et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2021), and information systems that fail to reach the most exposed populations compound that inequality. The assessment is that the equity consequences of climate information are a legitimate and necessary object of assessment, that they cannot be inferred from scientific quality alone, and that the design of climate services should be evaluated in part by whether it narrows or widens the gap between those who can and cannot act on the changing climate (*medium confidence*).

### 10.7.4 Synthesis: toward an integrated science of climate information

**The evidence assessed in this chapter supports treating climate information for decisions as an integrated socio-technical system spanning observation, computation, communication, and institutions, whose performance is determined jointly by scientific quality and by the human and institutional processes that convert science into action (*high confidence*).** No single component of this system is sufficient. Excellent observations without the capacity to convert them into services, powerful machine-learning methods without honest uncertainty quantification, or calibrated science without legitimate delivery, each fails to produce decision-relevant information on its own.

The recurring lesson across the chapter is that the binding constraints on decision-relevant climate information have shifted from the availability of data toward the fidelity of translation and the equity of access. Advances in reanalysis, Earth observation, carbon-cycle monitoring, and machine learning have greatly enriched the information base — carbon-cycle monitoring (Friedlingstein et al., 2022), globally consistent land-surface products (Zhang et al., 2021; Poggio et al., 2021), skilful data-driven prediction (Ravuri et al., 2021), and machine learning across the value chain (Kaack et al., 2022) — while the assessment of adaptation, mitigation, and their interactions has matured (Abbass et al., 2022). What most limits the value of this base for decisions is now the quality of co-production, the honesty of uncertainty communication, the integrity of the interface to impact assessment, and the equity of access. The assessment of this chapter is that the science of climate information is maturing into an integrated discipline in its own right, and that its further advance depends as much on the human and institutional dimensions of information as on the physical science it conveys (*high confidence*).


## 10.8 Operationalizing Climate Information: Standards, Evaluation, and Governance

**Turning climate science into a durable service requires more than good products; it requires standards, sustained institutions, evaluation practices, and governance arrangements that make the information reliable, comparable, and accountable over time, and the maturation of these operational foundations is uneven across the components of the climate-information enterprise (*high confidence*).** The preceding sections assessed the ingredients of decision-relevant climate information — co-production, observation, computation, communication, and the risk interface. This section assesses the operational scaffolding that allows those ingredients to be combined into services that persist, that can be trusted across users and settings, and that can be held to account.

### 10.8.1 Standards and interoperability

**Common standards for data formats, metadata, provenance, and uncertainty description are what allow climate information produced by one institution to be used reliably by another, and the value of the growing information base is substantially limited where such standards are absent or inconsistently applied (*high confidence*).** A gridded product is only as usable as its documentation: without clear metadata describing the variable, the reference period, the grid, the processing chain, and the uncertainty, even an excellent dataset cannot be safely combined with others or applied to a decision. Interoperability — the capacity of independently produced datasets to be combined without hidden inconsistency — is therefore a precondition for the assembly of decision-relevant information from heterogeneous sources.

The importance of standards is visible in the products this chapter has assessed. Globally consistent land-surface products succeed as decision inputs precisely because they apply a uniform classification and processing chain across space and time (Zhang et al., 2021), and soil-property products become decision-usable because they attach quantified, per-pixel uncertainty to every estimate (Poggio et al., 2021). The reconciliation that makes carbon accounting authoritative is itself a standardization exercise, imposing a common framework on heterogeneous emissions and sink estimates (Friedlingstein et al., 2022). The assessment is that standards and interoperability are not administrative overhead but a determinant of whether the information base can be converted into services, and that investment in them yields returns across every downstream use (*high confidence*).

### 10.8.2 Evaluation of climate information as a service

**The evaluation of climate information must extend beyond the scientific verification of accuracy to the assessment of usability, uptake, and decision outcomes, because a product can be accurate and still fail to inform decisions, and current evaluation practice remains weighted toward the former (*high confidence*).** Scientific evaluation asks whether a product is correct; service evaluation asks whether it is used, whether it is understood, and whether it improves the decisions it is meant to inform. These are different questions with different methods, and a climate service can score well on the first while failing entirely on the second.

The nowcasting evaluation assessed in Section 10.4 illustrates good practice: skill was assessed not only by statistical scores but by the judgement of the professional forecasters who would use the product, so that usefulness was measured against the decision it informs (Ravuri et al., 2021). This model — evaluating information against the decision and the user rather than against a purely physical benchmark — is applicable well beyond nowcasting, and its wider adoption is one of the clearer routes to improving the value of climate information. Insights from implementation science reinforce the point: sustained use depends on characteristics of the users, the settings, and the implementation process, none of which is captured by accuracy metrics alone (Damschroder et al., 2022). The assessment is that service-oriented evaluation, incorporating usability and decision outcomes, is essential and currently under-developed, and that its strengthening is a priority for the field (*high confidence*).

### 10.8.3 Sustained institutions and the problem of continuity

**Climate services depend on the continuity of the institutions that produce them, and the intermittent funding, staffing, and mandate of many climate-information providers is a structural threat to the reliability on which decisions depend (*high confidence*).** A defining feature of a service, as distinct from a study, is persistence: users build the service into their decision processes on the expectation that it will continue. When a service is discontinued, or when its methods change without warning, the decisions that depended on it are disrupted, and the trust that took years to build is lost quickly.

Continuity is threatened at several points. Observing systems require sustained funding to maintain the records whose value grows with their length; the geography of information poverty documented in Section 10.3 is in part a geography of discontinuous observation. Boundary organizations that specialize in translation between science and decisions are frequently funded on short cycles that are mismatched to the long relationships co-production requires. The assessment is that the value of climate information is inseparable from the continuity of its production, that continuity is an institutional and financial achievement rather than a scientific one, and that its neglect undermines even scientifically excellent services (*high confidence*).

### 10.8.4 Governance, accountability, and the integrity of climate information

**As climate information increasingly informs consequential public and private decisions — including the allocation of capital, the design of infrastructure, and the setting of policy — the governance of that information, including its provenance, its independence, and its accountability, becomes a matter of public interest (*medium confidence*).** When climate information carries financial and regulatory weight, it acquires stakes that create incentives to shade it. The pricing of transition risk depends on climate information (Bolton et al., 2023), and the interpretation of net-zero commitments depends on agreed accounting of a monitored quantity (Fankhauser et al., 2021); in both cases the integrity of the underlying information is exposed to the interests it affects.

Governance arrangements that protect the independence and traceability of climate information — clear provenance, transparent methods, and separation between the production of information and the interests that stand to gain or lose from it — are therefore increasingly important as the stakes rise. The assessment is that the governance of climate information is an emerging and under-developed dimension of the field, that it will grow in importance as climate information is more deeply embedded in financial and regulatory systems, and that the credibility of climate services will depend in part on demonstrable independence and accountability (*medium confidence*).

## 10.9 Cross-Cutting Themes and Deepening of the Decision Interface

**Several themes cut across the components assessed in this chapter — thresholds and non-linearity, the treatment of the baseline, the integration of mitigation and adaptation information, and the role of early warning — and treating them explicitly clarifies why decision-relevant climate information differs in structure from climate science as conventionally assessed (*high confidence*).** This section consolidates these cross-cutting themes and extends the assessment of the decision interface with attention to the operational services in which they are most consequential.

### 10.9.1 Thresholds, non-linearity, and the tails of the distribution

**Many of the most consequential climate decisions are governed by the crossing of thresholds and by non-linear responses, so that decision-relevant climate information must characterize the tails and the rates of change of climate variables rather than their central tendencies alone (*high confidence*).** A crop may tolerate a range of temperatures and then fail sharply beyond a critical value; a health outcome may respond mildly to moderate heat and then rise steeply at extremes; an infrastructure design may be safe up to a return-period event and vulnerable beyond it. In each case the decision-relevant information concerns the probability and frequency of exceeding a critical value, and information about the mean is at best indirectly useful.

The evidence assessed in this chapter repeatedly displays this threshold structure. The mortality burden of ambient temperature is concentrated at the extremes of the temperature distribution rather than distributed across its centre (Zhao et al., 2021), and agricultural failure is governed by the crossing of physiological limits that coupled climate-crop models are designed to capture (Jägermeyr et al., 2021). The implication for climate information is methodological: products optimized to represent central tendencies may misrepresent precisely the tail behaviour that decisions depend on, and decision-relevant products must be designed and evaluated with the tails in view. The assessment is that threshold and tail information is systematically more decision-relevant than central-tendency information for a large class of climate decisions, and that this reorientation is one of the principal ways in which the decision-first framing changes practice (*high confidence*).

### 10.9.2 The baseline problem

**Because most decisions are framed against expectations calibrated on the past, the choice of baseline or reference period against which change is expressed is a consequential and sometimes contested element of climate information, and an inappropriate baseline can distort a decision even when the underlying science is sound (*high confidence*).** A decision-maker asking whether conditions have changed is implicitly comparing the present or projected climate to a remembered or recorded normal, and the choice of that normal — its period, its length, its representativeness — shapes the answer. A baseline drawn from an unusually benign or unusually severe period will bias the perceived change; a baseline too short to average out variability will confound noise with trend.

The baseline problem interacts with the rapid change documented elsewhere in this Report. When the climate is changing quickly, any fixed historical baseline becomes progressively less representative of the conditions a decision will actually face, so that decision-relevant information may need to be expressed against a moving or forward-looking reference rather than a fixed historical one. This is particularly acute for exposure, which is itself changing as land use changes (Winkler et al., 2021; Potapov et al., 2021), so that both the climate baseline and the exposure baseline are moving simultaneously. The assessment is that the explicit, decision-appropriate treatment of the baseline is a necessary element of decision-relevant climate information, and that unexamined reliance on conventional historical baselines is a common and correctable source of distortion (*high confidence*).

### 10.9.3 Integrating mitigation and adaptation information

**Decisions increasingly require climate information that spans mitigation and adaptation together, because many choices affect both, and information systems that treat the two separately can obscure the trade-offs and co-benefits that decisions must weigh (*medium confidence*).** A land-use decision may simultaneously affect carbon storage and local adaptive capacity; an energy decision may affect both emissions and resilience to climate hazards. Where climate information is organized to address mitigation and adaptation in isolation, the joint consequences of such decisions are hard to see, and decisions may optimize one dimension at the expense of the other.

The assessed literature increasingly addresses this integration. Nature-based approaches, where soundly designed, can deliver adaptation benefits alongside mitigation and other co-benefits, though the evidence cautions against treating them as universally beneficial (Seddon et al., 2021). The monitoring of land-based carbon storage, including the large and policy-relevant stocks of mineral-associated soil organic carbon (Georgiou et al., 2022), provides information that is simultaneously relevant to mitigation accounting and to the management of the land systems on which adaptation depends. The pace and cost of the energy transition under a changing climate couple mitigation choices to the physical hazards that adaptation must address (Osman et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2022). The assessment is that integrated mitigation-adaptation information is increasingly necessary for decisions that affect both, and that its provision requires information systems designed to represent the couplings rather than to separate the two agendas (*medium confidence*).

### 10.9.4 Early warning as the integrative climate service

**Early warning systems are among the most mature and demonstrably valuable climate services, integrating observation, prediction, communication, and institutional response into a single decision-oriented chain, and they exemplify the socio-technical integration that this chapter has argued is necessary for climate information to save lives and reduce harm (*high confidence*).** An effective early warning system is not a forecast but a chain: it observes a developing hazard, predicts its evolution, communicates the risk to those exposed in a form they can act on, and connects to institutions capable of responding. A failure at any link — an unobserved onset, a forecast not delivered in time, a warning not understood, an absent response capacity — renders the whole ineffective, regardless of the quality of the others.

Early warning therefore concentrates, in a single service, every theme of this chapter. It depends on the observing foundation of Section 10.3, on the predictive methods of Section 10.4, on the uncertainty communication of Section 10.5, and on the institutional and equity considerations of Sections 10.2 and 10.7. Its effectiveness is greatest where the last link — reaching and enabling the most exposed populations — is strongest, and weakest where the geography of information poverty leaves those populations unobserved and unreached. The health evidence underscores the stakes, as the burden of climate hazards falls most heavily on the exposed and vulnerable (Romanello et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2021). The assessment is that early warning systems are a proven, high-value integrative climate service, that their value is realized only when every link in the chain is maintained, and that extending their reach to currently under-served populations is among the highest-return investments available in the domain of climate information (*high confidence*).

### 10.9.5 The health framing as a route to actionable information

**Framing climate information in terms of human health has proven a powerful route to actionability, because health outcomes are proximate, salient, and shared across constituencies, and successive health-and-climate assessments demonstrate the decision-relevance of translating physical hazards into health terms (*high confidence*).** Abstract statements about temperature or emissions are difficult for many decision-makers to act on; statements about heat-related mortality, the spread of climate-sensitive disease, or the health co-benefits of mitigation connect directly to widely shared priorities. The health framing therefore functions as a translation device, converting physical-climate information into a currency that motivates and directs decisions.

The assessed health-and-climate literature exemplifies this translation, tracking a broad set of indicators that link climate change to health outcomes and to the response measures that affect them (Romanello et al., 2021; Romanello et al., 2022; Romanello et al., 2023). The quantification of the mortality burden of non-optimal temperatures gives the framing a rigorous empirical anchor (Zhao et al., 2021). Crucially, the health framing also foregrounds equity, because the health burden of climate hazards falls most heavily on the exposed and the vulnerable, so that information organized around health naturally surfaces distributional questions that other framings can obscure. The assessment is that the health framing is an effective and evidence-rich route to actionable climate information, and that its capacity to connect physical hazards to shared human priorities makes it a valuable organizing device for decision-relevant information (*high confidence*).

### 10.9.6 Data ethics and the governance of machine-learning services

**As machine-learning methods become embedded in operational climate services, the ethical governance of those methods — their transparency, their treatment of uncertainty, their environmental cost, and their distributional consequences — becomes an operational requirement rather than an abstract concern (*medium confidence*).** A machine-learning climate service that produces confident predictions without honest uncertainty, that consumes disproportionate resources for marginal decision value, or that performs well only where data are abundant, embeds ethical choices in its ordinary operation. Governing these choices is part of operating the service responsibly.

The assessed literature provides the conceptual tools. The characterization of uncertainty in deep networks establishes what honest uncertainty quantification requires and where it is difficult (Gawlikowski et al., 2023); the concept of sustainable artificial intelligence frames the obligation to weigh the environmental and equity costs of the computation against its benefits (Wynsberghe, 2021); and reviews of machine learning across climate applications map where the methods are mature enough for operational trust and where they are not (Kaack et al., 2022). The assessment is that the responsible operation of machine-learning climate services requires explicit governance of uncertainty, resource use, and distributional impact, and that this governance is continuous with the broader governance of climate information assessed in Section 10.8 rather than separate from it (*medium confidence*).

### 10.9.7 Concluding assessment

**Taken together, the evidence assessed in this chapter establishes that decision-relevant climate information is a distinct object of study and practice, governed by the joint properties of information and decision context, and that its advancement now depends as much on co-production, communication, institutions, and equity as on the physical science it conveys (*high confidence*).** The information base has been transformed by advances in observation, reanalysis, Earth observation, carbon-cycle monitoring, and machine learning, and the assessment of climate change impacts, adaptation, and mitigation has matured alongside it (Abbass et al., 2022; Friedlingstein et al., 2022; Kaack et al., 2022). The binding constraints on the value of this base for decisions have shifted decisively toward the human and institutional processes that translate it into action, and toward the equity of access that determines who benefits.

The chapter's overarching assessment is therefore twofold. First, the scientific and technical foundations of climate information are strong and improving, and continued advances in observation and computation will further enrich the base (*high confidence*). Second, the realization of that base as decision-relevant information depends on co-production, honest uncertainty communication, a well-governed interface to impact assessment, sustained institutions, and equitable access, and it is in these dimensions that the greatest current gaps and the greatest opportunities lie (*high confidence*). A mature science of climate information for decisions and services is emerging at the intersection of the physical and the institutional, and its further progress will be measured not by the volume of information produced but by the quality of the decisions it enables. The transition from a data-limited to a translation-limited and equity-limited regime is, in the assessment of this chapter, the defining shift of the period covered by this Report, and it reframes the central task of the field: not to produce more climate information, but to ensure that the information already within reach is co-produced with those who will use it, communicated with calibrated honesty about what remains uncertain, connected through a traceable interface to the assessment of impacts and adaptation, sustained by durable and accountable institutions, and made accessible to the exposed and vulnerable populations whose decisions it must ultimately serve (*high confidence*).
