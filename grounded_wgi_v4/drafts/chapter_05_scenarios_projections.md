# Chapter 5: Scenarios and Near-Term Projections

## Executive Summary

**The scenario framework inherited from the Sixth Assessment Report remains fit for purpose in the near term, but the plausibility of its highest-emission storylines has continued to erode as observed and pledged mitigation trajectories diverge from unmitigated-growth assumptions (*high confidence*).** The Shared Socioeconomic Pathway architecture, coupled to the Scenario Model Intercomparison Project experimental design, continues to anchor multi-model projections; its near-term temperature spread is dominated less by scenario choice than by model response and internal variability (Tebaldi et al., 2021) (*high confidence*).

**Near-term global surface temperature is projected to continue rising across all plausible emissions pathways, and the difference between pathways is small before about 2040 because near-term warming is largely committed by past and present emissions (*very likely*).** Divergence between low- and high-emission pathways becomes assessable only toward mid-century; consequently the near-term is a regime in which mitigation choices are being made but their temperature signal has not yet emerged from the noise of internal variability (*high confidence*).

**Global fossil carbon dioxide emissions have remained near record levels through the early 2020s, and the remaining carbon budget consistent with limiting warming to well below 2 degrees C continues to shrink (*high confidence*).** The annual carbon budget accounting shows emissions plateauing rather than falling at the rate implied by the lowest scenarios (Friedlingstein et al., 2023), so the gap between current-policy trajectories and deep-mitigation pathways is widening in absolute terms (*high confidence*).

**Sectoral decarbonization pathways for the hardest-to-abate sectors — cement, iron and steel, and coal power — show that near-term emissions are strongly path-dependent on capital-stock turnover, and committed emissions from existing infrastructure constrain the feasible speed of the near-term transition (*high confidence*).** Plant-level analyses of coal (Cui et al., 2021), steel (Lei et al., 2023), and cement (Cheng et al., 2023) indicate that near-term abatement is dominated by decisions about existing and under-construction assets rather than by long-run technology availability (*high confidence*).

**Internal variability is the largest source of uncertainty in near-term regional projections and can locally mask or amplify the forced trend for a decade or more (*high confidence*).** In rapidly warming regions such as the Arctic, the forced signal is large enough that it dominates even at near-term horizons (Rantanen et al., 2022), whereas in mid-latitude land regions the near-term trend can be obscured by variability (*high confidence*).

**Initialized decadal prediction adds skill over uninitialized projections for some large-scale quantities in the first few years, but skill for regional precipitation and extremes remains limited (*medium confidence*).** The near-term prediction enterprise is maturing, yet its principal value is presently in ocean-heat and large-scale circulation indices rather than in actionable regional extremes forecasts (*medium confidence*).

**The probability of exceeding key near-term thresholds — including individual years above 1.5 degrees C relative to pre-industrial — has become substantial, though a single threshold-exceeding year does not constitute crossing the long-term warming level (*high confidence*).** Near-term extremes, particularly heatwaves and compound drought-heat events, are projected to intensify faster than the mean, so risk-relevant change outpaces the change in global mean temperature (Barriopedro et al., 2023; Tripathy et al., 2023) (*high confidence*).

**Low-probability, high-impact outcomes — including the possibility of engaging tipping elements and cascading risks — are not excluded on near-term horizons and warrant explicit assessment alongside central projections (*medium confidence*).** Storyline and risk-framing approaches complement probabilistic projection by making the tails of the distribution and their societal entanglements assessable (Simpson et al., 2021), complemented by the assessment of catastrophic and extreme storylines (Kemp et al., 2022) (*medium confidence*).

## 5.1 Scenario frameworks and their evolution since the Sixth Assessment

**The community scenario framework used for near-term projection is a layered construction — socioeconomic storylines, emissions and land-use pathways, and a coordinated set of climate-model experiments — and its near-term behavior is best understood by treating those layers separately (*high confidence*).** In the assessment lineage inherited from the Sixth Assessment Report, the socioeconomic layer is provided by the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways, the forcing layer by harmonized emissions pathways spanning a range of end-of-century radiative forcing levels, and the climate-response layer by the multi-model experiments coordinated under the Scenario Model Intercomparison Project (Tebaldi et al., 2021). This separation matters for the near term because, before about 2040, the projected climate is far more sensitive to the climate-response layer — model spread and internal variability — than to the choice among socioeconomic storylines. The scenarios diverge in their emissions almost immediately, but the climate system integrates that forcing slowly, so the temperature consequences of divergent choices are still emerging over the assessment's near-term window.

The core experimental design that underpins near-term multi-model projection was documented as part of the Scenario Model Intercomparison Project, which specified a small number of priority pathways spanning very low to very high forcing and a larger set of pathways filling in the response surface (Tebaldi et al., 2021). The near-term envelope in that design is constructed so that all pathways share, to a good approximation, the same recent historical forcing and differ chiefly in the rate at which they depart from it. As a result, the multi-model near-term spread is dominated by the range of transient climate responses across models and by the phasing of simulated internal variability, rather than by the labels attached to the pathways. This is a central interpretive point for readers of near-term projections: a plot of global mean temperature to 2040 that fans out by scenario is, over that window, mostly displaying model and initial-condition differences, not the policy content of the scenarios.

**The plausibility of the highest-forcing pathways as descriptions of the real world's near-term trajectory has continued to weaken since the Sixth Assessment, principally because their assumed fossil-energy expansion is increasingly at odds with observed technology deployment and announced policy (*high confidence*).** The highest pathways were constructed as high-reference cases in which fossil-fuel use — especially coal — expands strongly through the century. Analyses of realized and announced decarbonization indicate that such coal-intensive futures have become progressively less consistent with the trajectory of the global energy system (Cui et al., 2021; Victoria et al., 2021). This does not remove the highest pathways from the assessment: they retain value as sensitivity cases and as bounding scenarios for the carbon cycle and for physical-science process studies. But for near-term projection of the most likely climate outcomes, the assessment gives greater weight to the intermediate and lower pathways, while continuing to report the full range so that low-probability, high-impact outcomes remain visible.

The socioeconomic layer of the framework has been extended since the Sixth Assessment by explicit sustainable-development pathways that couple climate mitigation to broader development goals, illustrating that a given near-term forcing level can be reached by socioeconomic routes with markedly different implications for equity, land use, and co-benefits (Soergel et al., 2021). These development-oriented pathways are useful in the near term precisely because near-term emissions are determined by concrete, near-at-hand choices — infrastructure, land use, and energy investment — rather than by distant technological assumptions. A sustainable-development framing makes explicit that the near-term emissions layer is where policy actually operates, even though its temperature signal is delayed. The assessment treats such pathways as members of the scenario family rather than as replacements for the priority set, so that near-term projections can be interpreted against a spread of plausible socioeconomic contexts.

**The translation from emissions pathways to global temperature outcomes is now performed through well-documented reduced-complexity and assessment models whose near-term behavior has been calibrated against the fuller model ensemble, improving the traceability of near-term projections (*high confidence*).** A key methodological advance carried into this assessment is the explicit, reproducible chain that converts an emissions pathway into a probabilistic temperature outcome. The methods used to produce the mitigation-pathway temperature assessment in the Sixth Assessment cycle were documented in detail, including the emulator calibration and the propagation of carbon-cycle and forcing uncertainty (Kikstra et al., 2022). For near-term projection this traceability is valuable because it separates three distinct contributions to the projected temperature: the emissions themselves, the conversion of emissions to radiative forcing, and the climate response to that forcing. In the near term the last of these — the fast component of the climate response — dominates, which is why near-term temperature is relatively insensitive to scenario choice and relatively sensitive to the assumed transient response.

The forcing layer itself has been refined by improved treatment of non-carbon-dioxide agents, whose near-term influence is disproportionate. Short-lived climate forcers — including aerosols and methane — exert a strong influence on the near-term forcing trajectory even though their long-term temperature contribution is smaller than that of carbon dioxide. Because several low-emission pathways achieve rapid reductions in co-emitted aerosols alongside carbon dioxide, they can exhibit a transient near-term warming from the unmasking of aerosol cooling, before the reduced greenhouse-gas forcing dominates at longer horizons. This aerosol-unmasking effect is a distinctive near-term feature of the low pathways and is one reason the near-term temperature difference between low and high pathways is small or can even reverse in sign during the first decade or two. The assessment reports this explicitly so that the near-term behavior of the lowest pathways is not misread as evidence that mitigation is ineffective; rather, it reflects the different time constants of aerosol and greenhouse-gas forcing.

**The scenario framework is increasingly complemented, not replaced, by storyline and risk-based approaches that make the tails of the near-term distribution and their societal entanglements assessable (*high confidence*).** Probabilistic multi-model projection is well suited to characterizing the central part of the near-term distribution, but decision-relevant risk often lies in the tails and in the interaction of climate hazards with vulnerable systems. A framework for complex climate-change risk assessment has been articulated that decomposes risk into interacting hazard, exposure, vulnerability, and response components, and that is explicitly designed to handle the compounding and cascading of risks that pure hazard projection does not capture (Simpson et al., 2021). In the near term, where the hazard change is modest in the global mean but potentially large in specific compound events, this risk framing is essential to avoid understating the consequences of near-term change. The assessment therefore treats the scenario framework and the risk framework as complementary: the former supplies the physical hazard trajectory, the latter situates it within exposed and vulnerable systems.

The explicit assessment of high-impact, low-likelihood outcomes has been strengthened relative to earlier cycles. Catastrophic and extreme scenarios — those in the upper tail of the response distribution or those engaging strongly nonlinear Earth-system feedbacks — have been argued to be under-examined relative to their importance for risk management (Kemp et al., 2022). For near-term projection, the practical consequence is that the assessment reports not only the central estimate and likely range but also explicitly flags the physically plausible upper tail, including the possibility that the transient response lies at the high end of the assessed range. This is not a prediction that such outcomes will occur in the near term; it is a statement that they cannot be excluded and that their potential severity justifies their inclusion in the assessed range. The near-term window is precisely the horizon over which early-warning indicators of a high-sensitivity world would begin to be detectable, which gives the upper tail particular salience for the coming two decades.

**Since the Sixth Assessment, the coupling of climate risk to interacting global stresses — a "polycrisis" framing — has been advanced as a way to represent how near-term climate change interacts with concurrent economic, food, and geopolitical stresses (*medium confidence*).** The recognition that climate hazards do not act in isolation but interact with other global stresses has been formalized in the analysis of entangled crises and their causal mechanisms (Lawrence et al., 2024). While this framing sits at the boundary of Working Group I's physical-science remit, it is relevant to near-term projection because the societal consequences of a given near-term hazard trajectory depend strongly on the state of the systems it impinges upon. The physical-science assessment supplies the hazard side of this interaction; the polycrisis and coupled-crisis literature (Pörtner et al., 2023) situates the hazard within concurrent biodiversity, food, and development stresses. The assessment notes this coupling without extending its own quantitative projections beyond the physical climate system.

Taken together, the evolution of the scenario framework since the Sixth Assessment is best described as consolidation and extension rather than replacement. The Shared Socioeconomic Pathway architecture and the Scenario Model Intercomparison Project design remain the backbone of near-term multi-model projection (Tebaldi et al., 2021). The main developments are: a downweighting of the highest-forcing pathways as descriptions of the most likely near-term future while retaining them as bounding cases; a more traceable emissions-to-temperature chain (Kikstra et al., 2022); an extension of the socioeconomic layer toward sustainable-development framings (Soergel et al., 2021); and a stronger integration of risk, storyline, and high-impact-tail approaches (Simpson et al., 2021), complemented by the assessment of catastrophic and extreme storylines (Kemp et al., 2022). None of these changes the central near-term conclusion that scenario choice has a small effect on global mean temperature before about 2040, but all of them sharpen the assessment's ability to represent the near-term risks that matter most for decision-making.

## 5.2 Emissions pathways and near-term forcing

**Global fossil carbon dioxide emissions have remained near their highest recorded levels through the early part of the present decade, so the near-term forcing trajectory is currently tracking the intermediate rather than the low pathways (*high confidence*).** The annual global carbon accounting shows total anthropogenic carbon dioxide emissions plateauing at a high level, with fossil emissions still rising slowly and land-use-change emissions declining only modestly (Friedlingstein et al., 2023). This observed trajectory is the single most important constraint on near-term forcing, because it fixes the starting point from which all pathways depart. A pathway consistent with limiting warming to well below 2 degrees C requires emissions to peak and then decline steeply within the near-term window; the observed plateau means that the near-term is currently following a course closer to the intermediate pathways than to the steep-decline low pathways. The gap between the observed trajectory and the low pathways is not merely a matter of degree: because the low pathways require near-immediate declines, every year of continued high emissions makes the subsequent required decline steeper and narrows the remaining budget.

The remaining carbon budget — the cumulative future carbon dioxide emissions consistent with a given warming level at a given likelihood — is the most policy-relevant integral of the near-term emissions pathway. The carbon-budget framing follows from the near-linear relationship between cumulative carbon dioxide emissions and peak warming, and it makes the near-term decisive: because the budget is a stock, near-term emissions consume a disproportionate share of the space remaining for the most stringent targets. The annual accounting indicates that, at current emission rates, the budget consistent with the most stringent targets is being consumed within a small number of years to a decade or so (Friedlingstein et al., 2023). This gives the near term an outsized role: the pathways diverge slowly in temperature but rapidly in cumulative emissions, so the near-term choice among pathways largely determines whether the most stringent long-term targets remain physically attainable.

**Near-term forcing is shaped strongly by non-carbon-dioxide agents, and the co-evolution of aerosols and greenhouse gases produces a near-term forcing trajectory that differs qualitatively from the long-term one (*high confidence*).** Carbon dioxide dominates the cumulative forcing and hence the long-term warming, but on near-term horizons the forcing is materially influenced by methane, other short-lived greenhouse gases, and aerosols. Aerosols exert a net cooling that partially masks greenhouse warming; because aerosols are co-emitted with fossil combustion, pathways that decarbonize rapidly also reduce aerosol cooling rapidly. The near-term forcing trajectory of the low pathways therefore reflects a competition between falling greenhouse-gas forcing and falling aerosol masking. This is a defining feature of near-term projection and explains why the low pathways can show little near-term temperature benefit — or even a transient relative warming — before their greenhouse-gas reductions dominate at longer horizons. The assessment treats this as a physical consequence of the different atmospheric lifetimes involved rather than as an argument against near-term mitigation, since the long-term benefit of the low pathways is unambiguous.

Methane occupies a distinctive place in the near-term forcing balance because its atmospheric lifetime is short enough that reductions translate into a falling forcing contribution within the near-term window, unlike carbon dioxide whose forcing persists. This makes near-term methane mitigation an unusually effective lever on the near-term forcing trajectory: a rapid reduction in methane emissions can bend the near-term forcing curve downward on a timescale over which carbon dioxide reductions have little temperature effect. The near-term forcing pathways therefore depend not only on the carbon dioxide trajectory but on the assumed trajectory of methane and other short-lived agents, and pathways that combine deep carbon dioxide reductions with rapid methane reductions achieve the most favorable near-term forcing outcome. The assessment reports the methane contribution separately from the carbon dioxide contribution precisely because their near-term behavior differs so markedly, and because near-term methane action is one of the few levers capable of producing a detectable near-term forcing benefit.

The partitioning of the global carbon budget between fossil and land-use sources also shapes the near-term forcing pathway and its uncertainty. The annual carbon accounting resolves total anthropogenic emissions into fossil combustion and land-use-change components, and it also quantifies the fraction absorbed by the ocean and land sinks (Friedlingstein et al., 2023). For near-term forcing, the behavior of these sinks is important because it determines how much of each year's emissions remains in the atmosphere to exert forcing. The sinks have historically absorbed roughly half of anthropogenic emissions, but their near-term efficiency is subject to variability and to the possibility of weakening under continued warming, which would raise the airborne fraction and hence the near-term forcing per unit of emissions. The assessment treats the near-term evolution of the sinks as a source of uncertainty in the mapping from emissions to forcing, distinct from the uncertainty in the emissions themselves, and it emphasizes that continued monitoring of the sinks is essential to constraining the near-term forcing pathway.

Land-use change and the terrestrial carbon cycle interact with the near-term pathway in ways that couple the emissions layer to the physical climate. Because the land sink responds to climate — to temperature, moisture, and the concentration of carbon dioxide itself — the near-term land-use and land-carbon trajectory is not independent of the climate it helps to determine. Disturbances such as fire, drought, and the potential destabilization of large carbon stores can convert parts of the land from sink to source, and while such conversions are not the central near-term expectation, they represent a near-term risk to the emissions balance that the assessment flags explicitly. The near-term forcing pathway is therefore best understood as the outcome of an interaction between prescribed anthropogenic emissions and a partly responsive natural carbon cycle, rather than as a purely prescribed input, and this coupling is one reason the near-term forcing carries uncertainty even for a specified emissions pathway.

The composition of near-term emissions is determined by the sectoral structure of the energy and materials economy, and sector-resolved pathway analysis has become central to assessing what near-term forcing trajectories are feasible. The energy-transition modeling literature has matured to the point where the tools, their assumptions, and their limitations can themselves be reviewed and compared, clarifying which near-term pathways are represented robustly and which depend on contested assumptions (Chang et al., 2021). This methodological maturation matters for the physical-science assessment because it determines how much confidence can be placed in the feasibility of the steep near-term declines that the low pathways require. Where multiple independent modeling approaches agree that a near-term decline is achievable given deployed technology, the corresponding forcing pathway can be assessed as feasible; where the decline depends on assumptions that only some models make, the corresponding pathway is treated with more caution.

**Sector-level analyses of the hardest-to-abate emitting sectors indicate that near-term emissions are strongly constrained by existing capital stock, so committed emissions from operating and under-construction infrastructure limit the feasible speed of near-term decarbonization (*high confidence*).** Coal-fired power is the largest single source of energy-system carbon dioxide, and plant-by-plant analysis shows that the near-term coal emissions trajectory is governed by the age structure and utilization of the existing fleet rather than by the long-run availability of alternatives (Cui et al., 2021). A high-ambition near-term coal phaseout is physically possible but requires early retirement or curtailment of assets that would otherwise operate for decades, which is why coal is a decisive lever for near-term forcing. The existence of a large, young coal fleet in some regions is one of the principal reasons the highest-forcing pathways were originally constructed as coal-intensive; the same fleet analysis shows that a rapid phaseout, while demanding, is not precluded by the physical stock.

Iron and steel production is a second hard-to-abate sector whose near-term emissions are set by the installed base of blast-furnace capacity. Plant-level accounting of global steel emissions and of the routes to carbon neutrality shows that near-term abatement is dominated by decisions about existing integrated works and by the timing of investment in lower-emission production routes (Lei et al., 2023). Because steelmaking assets are long-lived and capital-intensive, the near-term steel emissions pathway is largely locked in by the current stock, and material near-term reductions require either curtailment of existing capacity or accelerated switching to lower-emission routes. The assessment treats such sectoral committed emissions as a physical constraint on the near-term forcing pathway: they set a floor below which near-term emissions cannot easily fall without early asset retirement.

Cement is a third sector with distinctive near-term dynamics, because a large fraction of its emissions is process-related — released by the chemistry of calcination rather than by fuel combustion — and therefore not addressed by decarbonizing the energy supply alone. Projections of cement-sector emissions, particularly in developing economies where construction demand is growing, indicate that near-term cement emissions are likely to remain substantial and are among the more difficult to reduce quickly (Cheng et al., 2023). The process-emission fraction means that even a fully decarbonized energy supply would leave a residual cement emission that requires carbon capture or material substitution to address. For near-term forcing, cement is therefore an example of a sector whose emissions are relatively insensitive to the energy-transition levers that dominate the power sector, and whose near-term trajectory depends on construction demand and on the deployment of capture technology.

Building materials more broadly contribute a large and often under-counted share of near-term emissions, and life-cycle analysis of residential and commercial construction indicates that material-related emissions could remain a significant component of the global total through mid-century absent targeted mitigation (Zhong et al., 2021). The near-term relevance is that a substantial fraction of the emissions associated with buildings is embodied in their materials and is emitted at the time of construction, so near-term construction activity translates promptly into near-term emissions. This contrasts with the operational emissions of buildings, which are spread over their lifetimes and are more responsive to grid decarbonization. The assessment notes that the near-term emissions pathway is thus sensitive not only to how energy is produced but to how much material-intensive construction occurs, a dependence that the sectoral pathway literature makes explicit.

**The near-term feasibility of the low pathways depends on the deployment rate of low-carbon energy and on the scaling of carbon-removal and capture technologies, both of which are advancing but from a small base relative to the required scale (*high confidence*).** Solar photovoltaics has reached a cost and deployment level at which it is capable of supplying a large and growing share of electricity, and its trajectory is one of the principal reasons the highest-forcing pathways have become less plausible (Victoria et al., 2021). The near-term forcing benefit of photovoltaic deployment operates through displacement of fossil generation, so its effect on the emissions pathway depends on what it displaces and how quickly. Analyses of very high renewable-energy systems indicate that near-total renewable electricity supply is technically representable in energy-system models, though the near-term realization depends on grid integration, storage, and the retirement schedule of existing fossil capacity (Breyer et al., 2022). These deployment dynamics are the concrete, near-at-hand determinants of whether the near-term emissions pathway bends downward at the rate the low scenarios require.

Climate change itself modifies the near-term supply of renewable energy, introducing a feedback between the projected climate and the feasibility of the pathways designed to limit it. Assessment of climate-change impacts on renewable-energy supply indicates that changes in wind, solar resource, and hydrological conditions can alter generation potential, generally by modest amounts in the near term but with regional variation (Gernaat et al., 2021). For near-term projection this feedback is second-order relative to deployment rates, but it is not negligible, and it illustrates that the emissions pathway and the climate response are not fully independent. The assessment treats this coupling as a refinement to the feasibility of the low pathways rather than as a first-order control on near-term forcing.

Carbon dioxide removal and capture technologies enter the near-term pathway assessment because most low pathways rely on some deployment of removal to offset residual emissions from hard-to-abate sectors. A survey of carbon capture, utilization, and storage technologies indicates that these are technically demonstrated but deployed far below the scale that the low pathways assume for later decades, so their near-term contribution to the emissions pathway is small (Dziejarski et al., 2023). Direct air capture, in particular, is assessed to be energetically and economically demanding at scale, and its near-term contribution to the emissions balance is minor relative to the reductions achievable through energy-system decarbonization (Sabatino et al., 2021). The near-term implication is that the emissions pathway over the coming two decades is determined overwhelmingly by how fast gross emissions fall, not by how fast removals scale; removals become quantitatively important to the balance only later. The assessment therefore cautions against near-term pathways that rely on early large-scale removal, since the deployment evidence does not currently support it.

Hydrogen and related energy carriers are a further element of the low pathways whose near-term contribution is assessed to be limited. A perspective on the hydrogen economy as a route to net-zero emissions indicates that low-carbon hydrogen can address specific hard-to-abate uses but that its near-term deployment is constrained by production cost, infrastructure, and the emissions intensity of current hydrogen supply (Spek et al., 2022). For the near-term emissions pathway, hydrogen is therefore best regarded as an enabling technology whose principal effect lies beyond the near-term window, with only a modest near-term contribution to the forcing trajectory.

**The economic valuation of near-term emissions has been revised upward, reinforcing that the near-term choice among pathways carries large and quantifiable consequences even though its temperature signal is delayed (*high confidence*).** Comprehensive re-estimation of the social cost of carbon dioxide, incorporating updated damage functions and discounting, indicates a substantially higher marginal cost of emissions than earlier assessments used (Rennert et al., 2022). While the social cost of carbon is an economic rather than a physical-science quantity, its revision is relevant to the interpretation of near-term pathways because it changes the weight attached to near-term emissions: a higher marginal cost implies that the near-term divergence in cumulative emissions between pathways — which is large even where the temperature divergence is small — carries correspondingly larger consequences. The physical-science assessment reports the emissions and temperature trajectories; the economic literature attaches value to them, and the direction of recent revision is toward greater weight on near-term action.

In summary, the near-term forcing trajectory is currently tracking the intermediate pathways, the remaining budget for the most stringent targets is being consumed within the near-term window, and the feasibility of bending the pathway downward depends on concrete near-term deployment and retirement decisions in a small number of high-emitting sectors, as the sectoral pathway analyses make clear (Friedlingstein et al., 2023). The near-term is the horizon over which the emissions pathway is most malleable and least reflected in temperature, which is the defining tension of near-term climate assessment.

## 5.3 Near-term projections to about 2040

**Global surface temperature is projected to continue rising through the near-term period under all assessed pathways, with the multi-pathway spread in the global mean remaining small until close to 2040 (*very likely*).** Because the near-term climate response is dominated by forcing already committed and by the fast component of the system's adjustment, the projected global mean temperature to 2040 is largely insensitive to which pathway is followed. The multi-model experiments coordinated under the Scenario Model Intercomparison Project produce, over this window, an envelope whose width is set principally by the range of transient climate responses across models and by the phasing of internal variability, not by scenario divergence (Tebaldi et al., 2021). This is the single most robust conclusion about near-term global temperature: it will keep rising, and the amount of rise before 2040 is bounded within a relatively narrow range that mitigation choices made now cannot substantially alter, precisely because those choices act through cumulative emissions whose temperature effect is delayed.

The narrowness of the near-term global-mean spread should not be mistaken for certainty about the near-term climate as experienced. The global mean is an average over a system whose regional and seasonal responses are far more variable, and whose extremes change faster than the mean. The assessment therefore distinguishes sharply between the near-term global-mean trajectory — well constrained and pathway-insensitive — and the near-term regional and extreme-event trajectory, which is more uncertain and, for the quantities that matter most to impacts, changing faster than the global mean would suggest.

**The probability of individual near-term years exceeding 1.5 degrees C above pre-industrial levels has become substantial, but a single exceeding year does not constitute crossing the long-term warming level, which is defined as a multi-decadal average (*high confidence*).** As the forced warming approaches 1.5 degrees C, the combination of the trend and interannual variability makes it increasingly likely that individual years will exceed that level even before the long-term average reaches it. The assessment maintains a strict distinction between a threshold-exceeding year — a fluctuation of the annual mean above a level — and the crossing of the corresponding long-term warming level, which is defined over a multi-decadal window and is the quantity to which the carbon-budget and pathway analyses refer. Conflating the two would misrepresent the near-term: the first threshold-exceeding years are expected before the long-term level is reached, and their occurrence is a signature of the approaching level rather than evidence that it has been crossed. This distinction is essential for interpreting near-term observations against pathway projections, since the observed record will show exceeding years while the assessed long-term level is still below the threshold.

**Near-term warming is strongly amplified in the Arctic, where the forced signal is large enough to dominate internal variability even at near-term horizons, so Arctic near-term change is among the most confidently projected regional signals (*high confidence*).** The Arctic has warmed at a rate several times the global average over recent decades, a robustly observed and physically understood amplification (Rantanen et al., 2022). Because the amplified signal is large, the near-term Arctic trend emerges clearly from variability, and near-term projections of continued rapid Arctic warming are among the most confident regional statements the assessment can make. The mechanisms — sea-ice loss and the associated surface-albedo and heat-flux feedbacks, changes in atmospheric and oceanic heat transport, and cloud and water-vapor feedbacks — are sufficiently well established that the near-term direction and approximate magnitude of Arctic warming are robust across models and pathways. This makes the Arctic a distinctive near-term case: a region where the forced signal is strong enough that the near-term projection is dominated by the trend rather than by the noise.

Arctic near-term change extends beyond temperature to the hydrological cycle. Improved-model analyses indicate that Arctic precipitation is projected to increase faster and by more than earlier generations of models suggested, with a transition toward a rain-dominated regime occurring earlier than previously projected in parts of the Arctic (McCrystall et al., 2021). For near-term projection, this means that the Arctic hydrological response, like its temperature response, is a leading indicator: the near-term signal is large enough to be assessed with relatively high confidence, in contrast to mid-latitude precipitation, where the near-term signal is small relative to variability. The rapidity of the projected Arctic precipitation change also has implications for near-term feedbacks, since changes in snow and rain partitioning affect surface albedo and the surface energy balance.

**Near-term changes in temperature extremes, particularly heatwaves, are projected to outpace changes in the global mean, so the near-term rise in the intensity, frequency, and duration of hot extremes is larger in relative terms than the rise in mean temperature (*high confidence*).** Heatwaves are among the extremes whose change is most directly and robustly tied to warming, and the physical understanding of how they respond to a warming climate has advanced to the point where their near-term intensification can be assessed with confidence (Barriopedro et al., 2023). The relationship between mean warming and the tail of the temperature distribution is such that a modest shift in the mean produces a disproportionate increase in the probability of extreme heat, and additional processes — land-atmosphere feedbacks, soil-moisture depletion, and circulation persistence — can amplify the change in the tail beyond what a simple mean shift would imply. For the near term this means that heat-related hazard is projected to increase noticeably even over the window in which the global mean rises only modestly, which is a central reason the near term is not a period of negligible change from an impacts perspective.

Compound events — the co-occurrence of multiple hazards or of a hazard with an aggravating condition — are a particular focus of near-term extremes assessment because their impacts can exceed the sum of the individual hazards. Concurrent drought and heat are a leading example: soil-moisture deficits and high temperatures reinforce one another through land-atmosphere coupling, and the joint probability of severe compound drought-heat events is projected to rise faster than the probability of either hazard alone (Tripathy et al., 2023). Crucially, the high-end risk of such compound events is projected to accelerate, so the upper tail of the compound-event distribution grows faster than the central estimate. For near-term projection this is significant because it means that the most severe compound events — those with the largest impacts — are projected to become more likely at a rate that outpaces the change in mean conditions. The assessment therefore treats compound events as a domain in which near-term change is both more uncertain and potentially more consequential than the global-mean trajectory would indicate.

Drought more generally is a near-term hazard whose projection is complicated by the interplay of physical and human drivers. The concept of anthropogenic drought recognizes that drought hazard in the near term reflects not only precipitation and temperature change but also water demand, land use, and management, so the realized near-term drought risk depends on human as well as physical factors (AghaKouchak et al., 2021). This framing is important for near-term projection because it clarifies that the near-term drought signal cannot be read from the physical climate projection alone; it emerges from the interaction of a modestly changing physical hazard with rapidly changing human water systems. The physical-science assessment supplies the hazard trend — generally toward increased evaporative demand and, in many regions, toward more frequent or intense dry conditions — while recognizing that the near-term realized risk is co-determined by human factors.

The economic dimension of near-term water stress reinforces the salience of the drought projections. Analysis of the economic impact of water scarcity indicates that even modest near-term changes in water availability can have substantial and unevenly distributed economic consequences (Dolan et al., 2021). While this is an impacts-and-economics result rather than a physical projection, it bears on the interpretation of the near-term hazard trajectory: the near-term change in the physical drivers of water scarcity, though modest in the global mean, intersects with economic systems in ways that amplify its consequences. The assessment cites this to underline that the near-term physical signal, small as it is in aggregate, maps onto disproportionate societal effects through the systems it impinges upon.

**Wildfire hazard is projected to expand in both space and time under near-term warming, with the fire-weather season lengthening and fire-prone conditions extending into regions and seasons previously less affected (*high confidence*).** The spatial and temporal expansion of wildland-fire activity in response to climate change has been assessed to continue under near-term warming, driven by the compounding of higher temperatures, altered precipitation, and vegetation changes (Senande-Rivera et al., 2022). Fire weather integrates several near-term climate changes — temperature, humidity, wind, and antecedent moisture — so its projected expansion is a useful summary indicator of near-term compound change. Because fire also interacts with the carbon cycle, near-term increases in fire activity carry a feedback dimension, releasing stored carbon and potentially altering the emissions pathway at the margin. The assessment treats near-term fire-weather expansion as a robust regional-to-continental signal, while noting that the realized fire activity depends additionally on ignition, fuel management, and land use.

**Near-term regional projections outside the strongly amplified regions are dominated by internal variability, so for many mid-latitude land regions the near-term forced signal is small relative to the range of possible outcomes (*high confidence*).** In contrast to the Arctic, where the forced signal dominates, many populated mid-latitude land regions have a near-term forced temperature and, especially, precipitation signal that is comparable to or smaller than the amplitude of internal variability over the same window. This means that near-term regional projections for such regions must be expressed as distributions whose width reflects variability as much as forced change, and that a single realization — including the observed evolution — can depart substantially from the forced trend for a decade or more. The assessment addresses this by emphasizing initial-condition large ensembles, which sample the range of internal-variability trajectories consistent with a given forcing, and by reporting near-term regional change as a probability distribution rather than a single trajectory. The practical consequence is that near-term regional planning must contend with a genuinely wide range of physically possible outcomes, and that observed near-term regional trends should not be over-interpreted as evidence for or against the forced projection.

The individual Earth-system models that populate the near-term projection ensemble differ in their representation of the processes that govern regional response, and understanding these differences is part of interpreting the near-term spread. Documentation of individual model configurations — for example the detailed description of a participating Earth-system model's simulations under the shared experimental protocol (Lovato et al., 2022) — allows the assessment to attribute parts of the near-term spread to specific structural choices in ocean, land, carbon-cycle, and aerosol representation. This traceability is valuable because it distinguishes near-term spread that reflects genuine uncertainty in the climate response from spread that reflects known biases in particular models. The assessment uses such model documentation to weight and interpret the ensemble rather than treating all members as equally reliable for every quantity.

**Sea-level and cryospheric changes have near-term trajectories that are highly committed and relatively pathway-insensitive over the near term, so near-term sea-level rise is projected with high confidence to continue at rates comparable to or exceeding the recent past (*high confidence*).** The slow-response components of the climate system — the ocean's thermal expansion and the large ice masses — respond to accumulated rather than instantaneous forcing, so their near-term change is largely set by past emissions and is insensitive to near-term pathway choice. This makes near-term sea-level rise one of the most confidently projected quantities: it will continue, at a rate that near-term mitigation cannot materially slow, because the near-term rise reflects heat and mass changes already in train. Pathway choice affects sea level strongly at long horizons but weakly in the near term, a temporal structure that mirrors the temperature case but is even more pronounced because of the longer response times involved. The assessment emphasizes that the near-term commitment in sea level is a reason to treat adaptation to continued near-term rise as unavoidable regardless of mitigation ambition, even as mitigation remains decisive for the long-term trajectory.

**Near-term projections of the large-scale atmospheric and oceanic circulation are more uncertain than projections of thermodynamic quantities, so circulation-dependent regional changes carry lower near-term confidence than temperature and its direct consequences (*medium confidence*).** Many of the most societally important regional changes — shifts in storm tracks, monsoon timing and intensity, and the position of the mid-latitude jets — depend on the response of the large-scale circulation, which is governed by a competition between different warming patterns and is less robustly simulated than the thermodynamic response. In the near term, the forced circulation signal is generally small relative to internal variability, and models disagree more about circulation than about temperature. As a consequence, near-term projections of circulation-dependent regional precipitation are typically expressed as wide distributions, and the assessment attaches lower confidence to them than to the near-term thermodynamic signals such as the intensification of hot extremes (Barriopedro et al., 2023). This asymmetry — high confidence in thermodynamically controlled near-term change, lower confidence in dynamically controlled near-term change — is a general organizing principle for interpreting the near-term regional projections, and it explains why quantities such as heat extremes are projected with more confidence than quantities such as regional storminess.

The near-term behavior of the large-scale ocean circulation is a particular focus because of its role in regional climate and in the slow variability that underpins decadal predictability. The near-term evolution of the major overturning circulations affects regional ocean heat content, sea level, and the heat exchange with the atmosphere, and it is one of the slowly evolving components that initialized prediction attempts to forecast. Because these circulations respond to accumulated forcing and to internal variability on decadal timescales, their near-term change is partly committed and partly predictable, and the quality of the near-term forecast depends on how well the initial ocean state is observed and represented (Jean-Michel et al., 2021). The assessment treats the near-term ocean-circulation projection as a domain in which the slow, partly predictable component coexists with genuine structural uncertainty in the models, so that confidence is higher for the direction of change than for its precise near-term magnitude.

**Near-term regional projection is increasingly supported by the systematic use of single-model initial-condition large ensembles, which separate the forced response from internal variability and thereby make the near-term uncertainty attributable to its physical sources (*high confidence*).** A single climate model run many times from slightly different initial states produces an ensemble whose spread, for a fixed forcing, is a direct estimate of the internal-variability contribution to the near-term projection. Comparing such single-model ensembles across models then separates the internal-variability spread from the structural, model-to-model spread. This methodology has become central to near-term projection because it allows the assessment to state, for a given region and quantity, how much of the near-term uncertainty arises from internal variability — which is irreducible on near-term horizons but bounded — and how much arises from genuine disagreement about the forced response. The documentation of individual model configurations supports this decomposition by making the forced-response characteristics of each model traceable (Lovato et al., 2022). The practical result is that near-term regional projections can be accompanied by an explicit account of why their range is what it is, rather than presenting an undifferentiated spread.

The near-term projection of extremes benefits especially from the large-ensemble methodology, because extremes are, by definition, tail events whose statistics require many realizations to estimate reliably. For hot extremes and for compound drought-heat events, the large-ensemble approach provides the sample size needed to characterize the changing tail of the distribution and to distinguish a genuine change in extreme statistics from the sampling noise of a small ensemble (Tripathy et al., 2023). This is why the assessment's confident statements about near-term intensification of extremes rest on large-ensemble evidence: the change in the tail is estimated from enough realizations to separate signal from noise. For circulation-dependent extremes, by contrast, even large ensembles leave substantial near-term uncertainty because the underlying dynamical response is itself uncertain, reinforcing the thermodynamic-versus-dynamic confidence asymmetry described above.

In aggregate, the near-term projection to 2040 is characterized by a well-constrained, pathway-insensitive global-mean warming; strongly amplified and confidently projected change in the Arctic and in the slow-response cryospheric and sea-level components; extremes — especially heat and compound drought-heat — changing faster than the mean; and a wide, variability-dominated range for many mid-latitude regional quantities (Tebaldi et al., 2021). The near-term is thus not a period of small change uniformly, but one in which certain quantities change confidently and consequentially while others remain dominated by the noise of internal variability — the subject to which the assessment turns next.

## 5.4 Internal variability, predictability, and decadal prediction

**Internal variability is the dominant source of uncertainty in near-term projections of regional climate and of many extremes, and it can locally reinforce or offset the forced trend for a decade or more (*high confidence*).** The climate system generates variability internally, without any change in external forcing, through the coupled dynamics of the ocean and atmosphere and through slower modes of the ocean and cryosphere. On near-term horizons this internally generated variability is comparable in amplitude to the forced change for many quantities, so the realized near-term climate is the sum of a forced trend and a variability trajectory whose phase is, in an uninitialized projection, unknown. This is why the near-term multi-model spread is dominated by variability and model response rather than by scenario: the scenarios have barely diverged in forcing, while the variability spread is already large. The assessment addresses this structurally by using large ensembles of simulations that differ only in their initial conditions, which sample the distribution of variability trajectories consistent with a given forcing and thereby separate the forced signal from the variability spread.

The separation of forced signal from internal variability is not merely a technical convenience; it is essential to the correct interpretation of near-term observations. Because a single realization of the climate — including the one we observe — can depart from the forced trend for a decade or more, an observed near-term trend that is faster or slower than the forced projection is not, by itself, evidence that the projection is wrong. Only when the observed departure exceeds what the variability spread can plausibly produce does it constitute a genuine tension with the projection. The assessment therefore evaluates near-term consistency between observations and projections against the full initial-condition spread, not against the ensemble mean alone, and it cautions against interpreting individual near-term episodes — accelerations, slowdowns, or pauses in the observed trend — as changes in the forced response.

**The largest modes of internal variability, particularly those of the ocean, provide the principal source of near-term predictability beyond that supplied by the forced trend, because their slow evolution can in principle be forecast once the ocean state is known (*medium confidence*).** Where internal variability is generated by slowly evolving components — most importantly the ocean — it carries memory, and that memory is the basis for near-term predictability that goes beyond simply extrapolating the forced trend. If the current state of the slowly evolving ocean can be observed and initialized in a model, the subsequent evolution of the associated variability can be predicted for some period into the future, adding skill over an uninitialized projection that knows only the forcing. This is the physical rationale for initialized near-term, or decadal, prediction: it attempts to forecast not only the forced response but also the phase of the slow internal variability. The degree to which this is achievable in practice is the central question of the decadal-prediction enterprise, and the answer differs strongly by quantity and region.

**Initialized decadal prediction demonstrably improves skill over uninitialized projections for some large-scale quantities in the first few years, most robustly for upper-ocean heat content and some large-scale surface-temperature indices, but skill for regional precipitation and for extremes remains limited (*medium confidence*).** The clearest gains from initialization are found where the predictable signal resides in the slowly evolving ocean: upper-ocean heat content and basin-scale surface-temperature averages retain skill for several years because they are governed by ocean states that persist and evolve predictably. For these quantities, initialized prediction adds value over an uninitialized projection, and the near-term forecast can be sharpened relative to the pathway-based projection. For quantities dominated by fast atmospheric variability — regional precipitation, individual extreme events — the predictability from initialization is much weaker, because the atmospheric memory is short and the predictable oceanic influence on these quantities is modest and regionally variable. The assessment therefore characterizes decadal prediction as presently most useful for large-scale, ocean-influenced quantities and as still developing for the regional and extreme quantities that are most directly relevant to impacts.

The near-term predictability of the ocean state itself depends on the quality of the observed initial state, which has improved with the maturation of ocean observing and reanalysis systems. High-resolution ocean and sea-ice reanalyses that assimilate the available observations provide the initial conditions from which decadal predictions are launched, and their fidelity sets an upper bound on the achievable near-term prediction skill (Jean-Michel et al., 2021). Improvements in these reanalysis products — in resolution, in the treatment of the sea-ice component, and in the assimilation of subsurface observations — propagate directly into improved initialization and hence into the potential skill of near-term prediction. The assessment notes that the observing system underpinning these reanalyses is therefore a critical, and in places fragile, foundation for near-term prediction, and that gaps in subsurface and polar observations translate into gaps in near-term predictive skill.

**The predictable component of near-term variability interacts with the forced trend, so that the near-term realized trajectory can be understood as a forced signal modulated by a partly predictable variability phase, and skillful near-term prediction requires representing both correctly (*medium confidence*).** Because the near-term climate is the sum of a forced trend and an internal-variability trajectory, and because part of the variability trajectory is in principle predictable from the initialized ocean state, the most skillful near-term forecasts combine a well-characterized forced response with a well-initialized variability phase. Errors in either component degrade the forecast: a model with a biased forced response will drift away from the observations even if its variability phase is correct, while a model with a poor initial state will misrepresent the variability phase even if its forced response is accurate. This is why the assessment treats the fidelity of the forced response — the subject of the model-evaluation and projection chapters — and the fidelity of initialization as jointly necessary for near-term prediction skill. The documentation of individual model configurations and their biases (Lovato et al., 2022) supports this joint evaluation by making the forced-response characteristics of each prediction system traceable.

The relative importance of the forced signal and internal variability shifts across the near-term window and across regions, and this shifting balance structures where and when near-term prediction can add value. Early in the near-term window and in regions of strong variability, the variability component is large and, where it is predictable, initialization adds the most value. Later in the window and in regions of strong forced response — the Arctic being the clearest example (Rantanen et al., 2022) — the forced signal grows to dominate, and the marginal value of initialization declines because the uninitialized projection already captures most of the signal. The assessment uses this structure to guide the interpretation of near-term prediction: initialization is most valuable in the first several years and in variability-dominated regions, while for strongly forced quantities the uninitialized projection is already close to the best available near-term estimate.

The predictable modes of internal variability that give near-term prediction its skill reside chiefly in the ocean, and their near-term forecasting depends on both the observed initial state and the model's ability to propagate that state forward. Slow ocean modes carry memory over years, and where a model can be initialized with an accurate ocean state and can propagate it with fidelity, the associated near-term variability can be predicted with useful skill. The reanalysis systems that supply the initial state have improved in resolution and in their treatment of the ocean and sea-ice components (Jean-Michel et al., 2021), which has improved the potential skill of near-term prediction, but limitations remain where the observing system is sparse — particularly in the deep ocean and in polar regions. The assessment therefore treats near-term predictive skill as jointly limited by the observing system, by the fidelity of the initialization, and by the model's representation of the slow modes, and it identifies the observing system as the component most amenable to near-term improvement through sustained investment.

A recurring interpretive challenge is that the skill of near-term prediction must be assessed against an appropriate baseline, since a forecast that merely reproduces the forced trend is not demonstrating skill from initialization. The assessment distinguishes the skill that arises from knowing the forcing — which an uninitialized projection also possesses — from the additional skill that arises from initializing the internal-variability state. Only the latter is unique to decadal prediction, and it is this incremental skill, rather than total skill, that measures the value added by initialization. Evaluated on this basis, initialized prediction adds clear incremental skill for large-scale, ocean-influenced quantities in the first several years and little incremental skill for regional precipitation and extremes, a pattern that is stable across prediction systems and that defines the current frontier of the near-term prediction enterprise.

**Near-term prediction of extremes and of high-impact events remains a frontier in which the forced change in the hazard distribution is better constrained than the year-to-year predictability of individual events (*medium confidence*).** For extremes such as heatwaves and compound drought-heat events, the assessment can state with confidence that the underlying distribution is shifting toward more severe values under near-term warming (Barriopedro et al., 2023; Tripathy et al., 2023), but predicting the occurrence of a specific extreme in a specific near-term year is a much harder problem that depends on the predictability of the fast variability that triggers the event. The current state of the science supports skillful projection of the changing statistics of extremes but only limited skill in predicting individual extreme years in advance. This asymmetry is important for near-term decision-making: the assessment can inform planning for a changed distribution of extremes with more confidence than it can provide year-ahead forecasts of specific events. The wildfire, drought, and compound-event projections discussed above should therefore be read as statements about the evolving hazard distribution rather than as predictions of specific near-term events (Senande-Rivera et al., 2022).

**The low-likelihood, high-impact tail of the near-term distribution — including the possibility of engaging tipping elements or of cascading Earth-system responses — is not excluded on near-term horizons and is best assessed through storyline and risk-based methods that complement probabilistic prediction (*medium confidence*).** Some components of the Earth system exhibit the potential for abrupt or self-reinforcing change once thresholds are crossed, and the interaction among such tipping elements can raise the risk of cascading responses (Wunderling et al., 2021). While the central near-term projection does not have most such elements crossing their thresholds within the near-term window, the possibility cannot be excluded, and the near-term is the horizon over which early indicators of approach to a threshold would begin to be detectable. Assessment of specific large systems — for example the potential for critical transitions in the Amazon forest under the combined pressure of climate change and land use (Flores et al., 2024) — indicates that the near-term evolution of such systems warrants monitoring even where a near-term transition is not the central expectation. The assessment treats these outcomes through storyline approaches that trace physically self-consistent high-impact trajectories, and through the risk-framing methods discussed in Section 5.1 (Simpson et al., 2021), complemented by the assessment of catastrophic and extreme storylines (Kemp et al., 2022), rather than attempting to attach precise probabilities to inherently poorly constrained tail events.

The entanglement of near-term climate risk with concurrent global stresses further complicates the assessment of the tail. Because near-term climate hazards act on systems that are simultaneously stressed by other global pressures, the societal consequences of a near-term hazard can be amplified through interactions that the physical projection alone does not capture (Lawrence et al., 2024), and that the coupled climate-biodiversity assessment also emphasizes (Pörtner et al., 2023). This coupling is most consequential in the tail, where a severe near-term hazard coinciding with stress in food, water, or economic systems could produce impacts disproportionate to the physical signal. The assessment notes this coupling as a reason to weight the near-term tail more heavily than its probability alone would suggest, while keeping the physical-science projection and the impacts assessment methodologically distinct.

**In synthesis, near-term projection and prediction form a spectrum: pathway-based projection supplies the well-constrained forced trend, initialized prediction adds partly recoverable skill for large-scale ocean-influenced quantities in the first several years, and the low-likelihood tail requires dedicated risk and storyline assessment (*high confidence*).** The near-term is neither fully predictable nor merely a scaled-down version of the long term. Its forced global-mean signal is robust and pathway-insensitive; its regional and extreme behavior is dominated by internal variability, part of which is predictable from the ocean state and part of which is not; and its tail contains outcomes whose potential severity justifies explicit assessment even where their probability is low. The methodological developments since the Sixth Assessment — traceable emissions-to-temperature chains (Kikstra et al., 2022), initial-condition large ensembles, maturing decadal-prediction systems built on improved ocean reanalyses (Jean-Michel et al., 2021), and integrated risk frameworks (Simpson et al., 2021) — collectively improve the assessment's ability to characterize each part of this spectrum. The overarching near-term message is unchanged and robust: warming will continue across all pathways through the coming two decades, the choices that determine the longer-term trajectory are being made now even though their temperature signal is delayed, and the risks that matter most in the near term are concentrated in extremes and in the tail rather than in the slowly diverging global mean.

## 5.5 Cross-cutting considerations for near-term projection

**The feasibility of the near-term emissions pathways that low scenarios require is increasingly assessed with probabilistic rather than deterministic methods, which better represent the genuine uncertainty in how fast the energy system can transform (*high confidence*).** Deterministic single-trajectory descriptions of an energy transition understate the uncertainty in deployment rates, retirement schedules, and demand growth. Probabilistic assessment of a large economy's transition toward carbon neutrality demonstrates that the near-term emissions trajectory is best represented as a distribution whose width reflects uncertainty in technology deployment and policy implementation rather than as a single line (Zhang et al., 2022). For near-term projection this matters because it clarifies that the near-term emissions layer — the input to the physical projection — is itself uncertain, and that the uncertainty in near-term forcing therefore has a socioeconomic as well as a physical component. The assessment treats the near-term forcing as conditional on this distribution of feasible emissions trajectories, which is one reason the near-term projection is expressed as a range conditioned on scenario rather than as a set of deterministic predictions. The probabilistic framing also makes explicit that the lower tail of the emissions distribution — the most rapid feasible transitions — remains physically available even where central projections track the intermediate pathways.

The maturation of energy-transition modeling has a direct bearing on how the physical-science assessment interprets the scenario inputs. Because the tools, assumptions, and limitations of transition models can now be systematically reviewed (Chang et al., 2021), the assessment can distinguish near-term emissions trajectories that are robust across modeling approaches from those that depend on contested assumptions about behavior, cost, or policy. This traceability from socioeconomic assumption to emissions input to physical forcing is the near-term counterpart of the emissions-to-temperature traceability established for the temperature response (Kikstra et al., 2022). Together these two traceable chains — assumptions to emissions, and emissions to temperature — allow the near-term projection to be decomposed end to end, so that a given near-term temperature range can be attributed to identifiable choices and uncertainties at each stage rather than emerging from an opaque pipeline.

**Observations continue to narrow the near-term projection by constraining the recent forced trend and the transient response, so the observational record is not merely a validation target but an active constraint on the near-term forecast (*high confidence*).** The near-term projection depends on the transient climate response — the warming realized per unit of forcing on decadal timescales — and this quantity is increasingly constrained by the observed warming of recent decades combined with estimates of the forcing that produced it. As the observed record lengthens and the forcing history is better characterized, the plausible range of the transient response narrows, and with it the near-term temperature spread. The near-term is the horizon on which this observational constraint is strongest, because it is close to the observed period and therefore least affected by the accumulating uncertainties that dominate long-term projection. The assessment uses the observational constraint on the transient response as one of the principal means of narrowing the near-term envelope beyond the raw multi-model spread, and it treats the ongoing observing system as essential infrastructure for maintaining and improving this constraint (Jean-Michel et al., 2021).

The value of observations for near-term projection extends to the individual processes that govern regional response. Where a regional feedback is both strongly observed and well understood — as in the case of Arctic amplification (Rantanen et al., 2022) — the observational constraint on the near-term regional projection is strong, and the projection is correspondingly confident. Where a regional process is poorly observed or weakly constrained, the near-term regional projection retains a wide range. This heterogeneity in observational constraint is one reason near-term confidence varies so strongly by region and quantity: it is not that the models disagree uniformly, but that some near-term signals are anchored by strong observational constraints while others are not. The assessment makes this heterogeneity explicit by attaching calibrated confidence to each near-term regional statement rather than reporting a single global level of confidence.

**Near-term sea-level rise is among the most policy-relevant committed changes, and its near-term trajectory is robust across pathways because it is dominated by the slow-response components of the system (*high confidence*).** The near-term rise in global mean sea level reflects ocean thermal expansion and mass loss from glaciers and ice sheets that respond to accumulated forcing over long timescales. Because these components integrate past forcing, the near-term sea-level trajectory is largely insensitive to near-term pathway choice, and continued rise at rates comparable to or exceeding the recent past is projected with high confidence regardless of mitigation ambition. This committed character makes near-term sea-level rise a clear example of a change to which adaptation is unavoidable, even as the long-term trajectory remains strongly dependent on the emissions pathway. The near-term regional sea-level pattern, which differs from the global mean because of ocean dynamics, gravitational effects, and vertical land motion, adds spatial structure that is important for local planning but does not alter the robust global near-term conclusion of continued rise.

The near-term evolution of the cryosphere reinforces the sea-level picture and interacts with it. Continued near-term loss of land ice contributes directly to sea level, while the loss of sea ice — particularly in the Arctic, where the forced signal is large (Rantanen et al., 2022) — contributes to regional amplification and to changes in the surface energy balance rather than to sea level directly. The distinction between land-ice and sea-ice contributions is important for near-term interpretation: sea-ice loss is a leading, confidently projected near-term signal with strong feedback consequences, whereas the near-term sea-level contribution from the large ice sheets is more uncertain in magnitude even though its direction is robust. The assessment reports these components separately so that the confident near-term signals are not diluted by the more uncertain ones.

**Near-term changes in the hydrological cycle are spatially heterogeneous, with confident intensification in some regions and variability-dominated ranges in others, so the near-term hydrological projection resists a single global summary (*high confidence*).** The global hydrological cycle intensifies with warming in a broad thermodynamic sense, but the near-term regional expression of this intensification is highly variable. In the strongly forced high latitudes, near-term precipitation increases are projected with relatively high confidence, and the transition toward rain-dominated regimes in parts of the Arctic is a leading near-term signal (McCrystall et al., 2021). In many populated mid-latitude and subtropical regions, by contrast, the near-term precipitation signal is small relative to internal variability, and the projection must be expressed as a wide distribution. Compound hydrological hazards — concurrent drought and heat — are a domain in which the near-term change in the joint distribution is more confident and more consequential than the change in either variable alone, with the upper tail of severe events projected to grow especially fast (Tripathy et al., 2023). The assessment therefore treats near-term hydrological change not as a single trajectory but as a mosaic of confidently projected signals in some regions and variability-dominated ranges in others, with compound events warranting particular attention because their impacts are disproportionate.

The realized near-term hydrological risk depends additionally on human water systems, which is why the anthropogenic-drought framing is essential for translating the physical projection into risk (AghaKouchak et al., 2021). A given near-term change in evaporative demand or precipitation interacts with water infrastructure, allocation, and demand to produce a realized drought risk that can differ substantially from the physical signal alone. The economic consequences of this realized risk can be large even for modest physical changes, and they are unevenly distributed across regions and sectors (Dolan et al., 2021). The assessment cites these results to underline that the near-term hydrological hazard, though spatially heterogeneous and often modest in the physical mean, maps onto societal consequences through the human systems it impinges upon, and that the near-term risk therefore cannot be read from the physical projection in isolation.

**The near-term is the horizon over which early indicators of a high-sensitivity or tipping-prone Earth system would first become detectable, which gives near-term monitoring a distinctive role in risk management (*medium confidence*).** If the true transient climate response lies at the high end of the assessed range, or if strongly nonlinear feedbacks are closer to engagement than central estimates suggest, the first detectable signatures would appear within the near-term window as departures of the observed trend or of specific process indicators from the central projection. Interacting tipping elements raise the possibility that such departures, once begun, could reinforce one another (Wunderling et al., 2021), and the near-term monitoring of specific vulnerable systems — such as the Amazon forest, where critical transitions are physically possible under combined climatic and land-use pressure (Flores et al., 2024) — is therefore a component of near-term risk assessment even where a near-term transition is not the central expectation. The assessment frames this not as a prediction but as a rationale for sustained observation: the near-term is when the system would first reveal whether it is tracking the central projection or a higher-impact trajectory, and the value of detecting that early is high because the response options narrow as the system evolves.

The coupling of near-term climate hazards to concurrent global stresses further elevates the importance of near-term monitoring and risk assessment. Because near-term hazards act on systems already stressed by other pressures, their societal consequences can be amplified through interactions that the physical projection does not capture (Lawrence et al., 2024). The coupled character of the climate and biodiversity crises is a specific example in which near-term climate change and near-term ecological change reinforce one another with societal consequences that exceed the sum of the parts (Pörtner et al., 2023). The assessment treats these couplings as reasons to weight the near-term tail more heavily than its physical probability alone would suggest, while maintaining a clear methodological separation between the physical-science projection, which is Working Group I's remit, and the impacts and risk assessment that builds upon it.

**The economic weight attached to near-term emissions has risen, reinforcing that the near-term divergence in cumulative emissions between pathways carries large consequences even where the near-term temperature divergence is small (*high confidence*).** The re-estimation of the marginal cost of carbon dioxide emissions toward substantially higher values (Rennert et al., 2022) changes the interpretation of the near-term pathway divergence. Because the pathways diverge rapidly in cumulative emissions even while diverging slowly in temperature, and because each tonne of near-term emissions now carries a higher assessed marginal cost, the economic stakes of the near-term choice among pathways are large and front-loaded. This economic framing complements the physical finding that the near-term is where the pathway is most malleable: the physical science shows that near-term action determines the long-term trajectory with little near-term temperature feedback, and the economic literature shows that the value of that near-term action is high. The assessment reports both, keeping the physical projection and the economic valuation methodologically distinct while noting their consistent implication that near-term action is disproportionately consequential.

**The near-term also frames the interpretation of individual threshold-exceeding years against the long-term warming levels to which policy targets refer, and clear communication of this distinction is a cross-cutting responsibility of the assessment (*high confidence*).** As the forced warming approaches widely referenced levels, the combination of the trend and interannual variability makes individual exceeding years increasingly likely before the corresponding long-term level is reached. The near-term will therefore contain years whose global mean exceeds a target level while the assessed long-term warming remains below it, and misinterpreting such years as the crossing of the level would misrepresent the state of the climate system. The assessment addresses this by consistently defining warming levels as multi-decadal averages and by explaining that the appearance of exceeding years is an expected signature of approaching a level rather than evidence of having crossed it. This communication task is cross-cutting because it bears on how the near-term observational record — which will show exceeding years — is read against the pathway projections and the carbon-budget analyses, all of which refer to long-term averages. Getting this distinction right is essential to avoid both premature declarations that a level has been crossed and complacent dismissal of exceeding years as meaningless noise; the correct reading is that they are early, expected indicators that the long-term level is being approached. The near-term is thus not only a period of committed physical change but a period in which the interpretation of the emerging record requires particular care, so that the physical-science assessment supports rather than confuses the public understanding of where the climate system stands relative to the levels that anchor mitigation goals.

**Taken as a whole, near-term projection integrates a well-constrained physical trajectory, a socioeconomically conditioned emissions input, a strong but heterogeneous observational constraint, and an explicit treatment of the high-impact tail, and its principal message for decision-making is that the near-term is decisive despite its muted temperature signal (*high confidence*).** The near-term global mean warming is robust and pathway-insensitive; the near-term regional and extreme behavior is a mosaic of confidently projected signals and variability-dominated ranges; the near-term emissions pathway is malleable and economically consequential; and the near-term tail contains outcomes whose potential severity justifies explicit assessment and sustained monitoring. The methodological developments carried into this assessment — probabilistic transition analysis (Zhang et al., 2022), traceable emissions-to-temperature chains (Kikstra et al., 2022), initial-condition large ensembles, maturing decadal prediction on improved ocean reanalyses (Jean-Michel et al., 2021), and integrated risk frameworks (Simpson et al., 2021) — collectively sharpen each of these elements. The near-term is not a period of small consequence; it is the period in which the determinants of the longer-term trajectory are set, in which the risks that most affect societies are already intensifying, and in which the Earth system would first signal whether it is following the central projection or a higher-impact path.
