# Chapter 1: Framing, Context, and Methods

## Executive Summary

**The Variant Earths assessment framework treats the observed climate system as one realization drawn from a structured space of physically admissible Earth states, and this chapter establishes the framing, evidentiary base, and methodological conventions that govern every subsequent chapter of this Working Group I volume (*high confidence*).** The framing is designed to make the counterfactual and projected members of that space commensurable with the observed member, so that attribution, projection, and scenario analysis share a single calibrated vocabulary.

**Multiple independent lines of evidence establish that the contemporary Earth system has moved outside the envelope of Holocene variability across several coupled domains, and this displacement is the physical context that the assessment must explain (*virtually certain*).** Assessments of planetary boundaries indicate that six of nine have been transgressed (Richardson, 2023), the global carbon budget shows sustained growth in anthropogenic emissions and atmospheric accumulation (Friedlingstein, 2022) (Friedlingstein, 2023), and the biosphere itself is now a primary driver of Earth-system trajectory rather than a passive backdrop (Folke, 2021).

**Regional amplification of the forced signal is a defining feature of the observed record and a stringent test of models, with Arctic near-surface warming having proceeded nearly four times faster than the global mean since 1979 (*high confidence*).** The magnitude of this amplification (Rantanen, 2022), together with cryospheric instabilities such as the anomalous collapse of Arctic sea-ice arches (Moore, 2021), constrains the dynamical realism that any credible member of the Variant Earths ensemble must reproduce.

**The methodological core of this assessment rests on the integration of physical models, observational reanalyses, and machine-learning methods into a single evidentiary pipeline whose uncertainties are explicitly quantified and propagated (*high confidence*).** Coordinated multi-model projections under shared scenarios (Tebaldi, 2021), physics-informed and data-driven emulators (Karniadakis, 2021) (Bi, 2023), and formal treatments of predictive uncertainty (Gawlikowski, 2023) are combined under governance rules that prevent any single method from being reported without its associated confidence.

**Calibrated uncertainty language is applied uniformly, with likelihood terms reserved for quantified probabilistic statements and confidence terms reserved for the assessed robustness of evidence and agreement (*high confidence*).** This chapter specifies how those terms map onto the underlying quantitative and expert-elicited evidence, and how methodological biases — including those introduced by newer machine-learning and generative tools (Gawlikowski, 2023; Díaz-Rodríguez, 2023) — are surfaced rather than concealed.

## 1.1 Framing the Assessment

### 1.1.1 The Variant Earths premise

**This volume frames the climate system as one admissible realization within a structured space of Earth states, and adopting this framing changes how observations, counterfactuals, and projections are made commensurable rather than changing the underlying physics (*high confidence*).** The observed Earth is treated as a single draw from a distribution of physically consistent system configurations that differ in their forcing histories, internal variability phasing, and boundary conditions. The purpose of the framing is neither speculative nor rhetorical: it provides an explicit, shared object — the ensemble of variant Earths — against which attribution statements ("how does the observed member differ from a member without anthropogenic forcing?") and projection statements ("which members are consistent with a given emissions pathway?") can be posed with a common vocabulary and a common uncertainty calculus.

The framing has three practical consequences that propagate through every subsequent chapter. First, it forces every quantitative claim to be located within the ensemble rather than asserted about a single trajectory; a statement about, for example, the frequency of a compound extreme is a statement about the fraction of admissible members exhibiting that extreme under specified conditions. Second, it requires that observational products and model output be brought onto a common footing, because the observed member and the simulated members must be compared like with like. Third, it makes uncertainty quantification a first-class requirement rather than an afterthought, because the width and structure of the ensemble is itself the primary scientific output.

None of these consequences is novel to climate science in substance; coordinated model intercomparisons already treat projections as distributions over models and scenarios (Tebaldi, 2021), and the global assessment of anthropogenic carbon perturbation is already reported as a budget with quantified terms and residuals (Friedlingstein, 2022). What the Variant Earths framing adds is the insistence that the observed system, the counterfactual system, and the projected systems be represented within one explicitly enumerated space, so that no comparison is made across incommensurable representations. This is a methodological discipline, and the remainder of this chapter is devoted to making it operational.

### 1.1.2 The Earth system has departed the Holocene envelope

**Assessment of the aggregate state of the Earth system indicates that several coupled domains have moved outside the range characteristic of the Holocene, and this displacement is the physical premise the assessment must explain rather than a conclusion it seeks to prove (*virtually certain*).** The planetary-boundaries synthesis finds that six of nine boundaries — including climate change, biosphere integrity, land-system change, freshwater change, biogeochemical flows, and novel entities — have been transgressed (Richardson, 2023). Each boundary is defined by a control variable with a proposed safe operating range, and transgression indicates that the control variable has moved into a regime for which Holocene analogues are absent or weak. The assessment does not treat the boundaries framework as a predictive model; it treats it as an organizing diagnosis of how far, and in how many dimensions, the system has moved.

The land surface is central to this diagnosis. Reconstructions of global land-use change indicate that the area affected by human land-use activity is roughly four times greater than earlier estimates when the full history of transitions is accounted for (Winkler, 2021), and cropland extent has expanded at an accelerating pace in the twenty-first century (Potapov, 2021). These changes matter for the climate assessment not only through their direct effect on albedo and evapotranspiration but because they reorganize the terrestrial carbon and water cycles that the assessment must close. Soil is a large and dynamic reservoir in this reorganization: the stabilization of soil organic matter is governed by interactions at the mineral–organic interface (Kleber, 2021), the molecular composition of stabilized carbon reflects both plant and microbial sources (Angst, 2021), and the global stock of mineral-associated soil organic carbon represents a substantial and spatially structured capacity (Georgiou, 2022). High-resolution global soil information products now quantify these properties with explicit spatial uncertainty (Poggio, 2021), which is precisely the form in which the assessment requires them.

The freshwater domain is similarly displaced. Global assessment of water resources emphasizes the increasingly central role of groundwater in buffering a system under stress (Scanlon, 2023), while projections indicate widespread primary soil salinization under a changing climate over the twenty-first century (Hassani, 2021). Inland waters are changing in ways that couple physical and biogeochemical processes: temperate lakes are undergoing widespread deoxygenation as they warm and stratify (Jane, 2021). These are not isolated observations; taken together they describe a terrestrial and hydrological system whose state variables have moved coherently, which is why the assessment adopts a coupled, Earth-system framing rather than treating the atmosphere in isolation.

### 1.1.3 The biosphere as an active driver

**The living biosphere is assessed as an active participant in the Earth system's trajectory, mediating carbon, water, and energy fluxes in ways that feed back on the physical climate rather than merely responding to it (*high confidence*).** The framing of the current epoch as an Anthropocene biosphere emphasizes that human societies and ecosystems are now co-determining the system's path, with implications for how resilience and abrupt change are assessed (Folke, 2021). This is not a purely conceptual point. Biological processes set the composition and persistence of soil carbon (Angst, 2021; Kleber, 2021), vegetation and land management determine the partitioning of the anthropogenic carbon perturbation into land and ocean sinks (Friedlingstein, 2023), and ecological responses to warming — from shifting pest and pathogen ranges to fire regimes — alter the boundary conditions of the physical system.

The assessment therefore treats biological and ecological evidence as part of its core evidentiary base, not as impact reporting downstream of the physical conclusions. The consequences of warming for plant pathogens and food security (Singh, 2023), the emergence of climate impacts on agriculture in a new generation of coupled climate and crop models (Jägermeyr, 2021), and the global-scale risk of pesticide pollution to soils and waters (Tang, 2021) are all relevant to how the terrestrial system will evolve. Machine-learning methods for monitoring wildlife and ecosystems (Tuia, 2022) are, in turn, part of the observational apparatus that makes the biosphere legible at the scales the assessment requires. The Variant Earths framing accommodates these couplings naturally: a variant Earth is specified not only by its physical forcing but by the state of its biosphere, and the admissible ensemble spans plausible biospheric configurations consistent with the observations.

### 1.1.4 Scope, boundaries, and what this volume does not assess

**The scope of this Working Group I volume is the physical science basis of the variant-Earths ensemble — its observed state, the attributable drivers of that state, and its projected evolution under specified pathways — while adjacent questions of impact, adaptation, and response are framed here only insofar as they constrain or are constrained by the physical assessment (*high confidence*).** This boundary is drawn deliberately. Evidence on the health burden of climate change (Romanello, 2022) (Romanello, 2023), on heat-related mortality under non-optimal temperatures (Zhao, 2021), and on the respiratory consequences of wildfire smoke (Aguilera, 2021) is cited in this volume where it bears on the physical characterization of hazards — for example, where mortality data help validate the spatial and temporal structure of modeled temperature extremes — but the assessment of impacts and their management is the responsibility of other working groups.

Similarly, the economic valuation of the carbon perturbation, including comprehensive estimates of the social cost of CO2 (Rennert, 2022), is treated here as context that motivates the precision of the physical assessment rather than as a physical-science conclusion. The Variant Earths framing is helpful in maintaining this discipline: because the physical ensemble is defined independently of any valuation or response, the physical conclusions can be reported without contamination by normative assumptions, and the downstream working groups can draw on a clean, uncertainty-quantified physical basis.

Two exclusions deserve explicit statement. First, this volume does not assess the desirability of any emissions pathway; it assesses which members of the ensemble are consistent with each pathway and with what likelihood. Second, this volume does not adjudicate response options; where response-relevant technologies appear in the evidentiary base — for example, work on fuel cells for heavy-duty transport (Cullen, 2021), ammonia as a fuel (Valera-Medina, 2021), or the electrochemical reduction of CO2 (Monteiro, 2021) (Yang, 2023) — they are cited only where they inform the physical forcing assumptions that define pathway members, never as recommendations.

### 1.1.5 The role of framing in preventing incommensurable comparison

**A central purpose of explicit framing is to prevent the silent comparison of quantities computed under incompatible assumptions, a failure mode that the Variant Earths ensemble is specifically constructed to expose (*high confidence*).** In practice, incommensurability enters through three doors: differing baselines, differing spatial and temporal support, and differing treatments of uncertainty. The framing closes each door by requiring that every reported quantity carry its baseline, its support, and its uncertainty representation, and by requiring that comparisons be made only among ensemble members that share these attributes.

This discipline is not cost-free, and the chapter is candid about the tension it creates. Requiring common support often means degrading a high-resolution product to match a coarser one, which discards information; requiring common baselines sometimes means recomputing anomalies that were originally reported against a different reference period. The assessment accepts these costs because the alternative — reporting apparently precise comparisons that are in fact incommensurable — is the more serious error. The methodological sections below (1.3 and 1.4) specify the procedures by which support, baseline, and uncertainty are harmonized, and the conventions by which any residual incommensurability is flagged in the assessed statements themselves.

## 1.2 Context: The Observed State and Its Drivers

### 1.2.1 The anthropogenic carbon perturbation and its budget

**The atmospheric accumulation of CO2 is assessed as the dominant driver of the contemporary radiative imbalance, and its magnitude, growth, and partitioning are quantified with sufficient precision to serve as the backbone of the physical assessment (*virtually certain*).** The global carbon budget synthesis reconciles emissions from fossil sources and land-use change against the observed atmospheric growth rate and the estimated land and ocean sinks, and reports each term with an explicit uncertainty and a budget residual (Friedlingstein, 2022). Successive annual updates confirm the persistence of emissions growth and refine the partitioning among sinks (Friedlingstein, 2023). For the Variant Earths framing, the carbon budget is more than a bookkeeping exercise: it defines the axis along which pathway members of the ensemble are separated, because a variant Earth's forcing trajectory is, to first order, determined by its cumulative and time-resolved carbon perturbation.

The sectoral structure of emissions is part of this context. Assessment of the trends and drivers of greenhouse-gas emissions by sector over recent decades shows both the scale of the perturbation and the heterogeneity of its sources, with energy supply, industry, transport, and land use contributing in changing proportions (Lamb, 2021). This structure matters to the physical assessment in two ways. First, different sectors emit different mixtures of long-lived and short-lived agents, so the temporal shape of the radiative response depends on the sectoral composition, not only on the aggregate. Second, the plausibility of pathway members depends on the technical and economic feasibility of sectoral transitions, which is why the evidentiary base includes work on candidate low-carbon energy carriers and conversion technologies — heavy-duty fuel cells (Cullen, 2021), ammonia as a fuel (Valera-Medina, 2021), and CO2 electroreduction chemistry (Monteiro, 2021) (Yang, 2023). These are cited here strictly as constraints on which forcing trajectories are physically and technologically admissible, consistent with the scope boundary drawn in Section 1.1.4.

The budget also anchors the assessment's treatment of the terrestrial sink, which is neither passive nor stationary. The persistence of soil organic carbon, and hence the strength of the land sink, depends on mineral–organic interactions (Kleber, 2021) and on the balance of plant- and microbial-derived inputs (Angst, 2021), while the total mineral-associated stock sets an upper bound on a large fraction of the sink's capacity (Georgiou, 2022). The assessment treats these process-level findings as the mechanistic basis for the land-sink term in the budget, so that the budget's residual can be interpreted physically rather than as an unexplained closure error.

### 1.2.2 Regional amplification and the structure of the forced signal

**The forced warming signal is spatially and seasonally structured, with pronounced high-latitude amplification, and reproducing this structure is a necessary condition for any ensemble member to be admitted as physically credible (*high confidence*).** The most stringent single constraint is the rate of Arctic warming: near-surface temperatures over much of the Arctic have increased nearly four times faster than the global mean since 1979 (Rantanen, 2022). This amplification arises from coupled feedbacks — surface-albedo loss, changes in vertical mixing and cloud properties, and altered meridional energy transport — whose correct representation is a demanding test of the coupled models that generate the ensemble.

Cryospheric dynamics sharpen the test. The anomalous collapse of the sea-ice arches that normally regulate flow through Nares Strait leads to enhanced export of thick, old Arctic sea ice, an example of a threshold-like reorganization that linear diagnostics would miss (Moore, 2021). The assessment treats such events as diagnostic of a member's dynamical realism: an ensemble member that cannot support the formation and episodic failure of these arches is not merely biased in a mean sense but is missing a mechanism, and the framing requires that such structural deficiencies be flagged rather than absorbed into a tuned mean state.

Elevation adds a further axis of structure. The mountains of the world exhibit distinctive and elevation-dependent patterns of climate change, with warming rates that can differ systematically with altitude and that couple to snow, ice, and hydrology in ways poorly resolved by coarse models (Pepin, 2022). Because mountain regions are the source of the water resources on which large populations depend (Scanlon, 2023), the assessment gives elevation-dependent signals particular attention, and treats a member's ability to reproduce them as part of its admissibility criteria. The recurring theme is that the forced signal is not a spatially uniform offset; it is a structured field, and the credibility of the ensemble rests on reproducing that structure, not merely the global mean.

### 1.2.3 Extremes, compound events, and abrupt transitions

**The observed record includes extremes and compound events whose frequency and intensity have shifted in ways consistent with the forced signal, and characterizing these shifts is a central task of the assessment because extremes dominate the impact-relevant tail of the ensemble (*high confidence*).** Flash droughts — rapid-onset droughts driven by evaporative demand — are projected to become more common under continued warming, representing a shift in the character and not merely the frequency of drought (Yuan, 2023). Fire regimes have responded to climate variability and change, with large and extreme forest fires in regions such as southeastern Australia showing connections to warming and to modes of variability (Abram, 2021), and the atmospheric consequences of the resulting smoke are themselves a physically distinct hazard (Aguilera, 2021).

Compound and cascading behavior is a defining feature of these extremes. A single warm-season sequence can couple heat, evaporative demand, vegetation stress, and fire, so that the joint distribution of hazards is not the product of their marginals. The assessment treats compound extremes as intrinsically multivariate objects and represents them within the ensemble as joint exceedances across the relevant fields, rather than as independent univariate events. This is one place where the Variant Earths framing is directly operational: the fraction of members exhibiting a specified compound event, under specified conditions, is a well-defined and reportable quantity, whereas the same statement made about a single trajectory would be ambiguous.

Abrupt transitions — the sea-ice arch collapse discussed above (Moore, 2021), the deoxygenation of stratifying lakes (Jane, 2021), the acceleration of cropland expansion into new regions (Potapov, 2021) — share the property that a smooth change in a driver produces a threshold-like change in a state variable. The assessment does not claim to predict the timing of such transitions with high confidence; it claims to identify where the ensemble supports them and to report the associated likelihoods honestly, including the cases where the evidence supports only a statement of possibility rather than probability.

### 1.2.4 The terrestrial and hydrological system as coupled context

**The land surface and hydrological cycle are assessed as a tightly coupled system whose reorganization is both a consequence of and a contributor to the forced climate signal, and treating them as coupled is essential to closing the assessment's energy, water, and carbon budgets (*high confidence*).** Land-use change on the scale now documented (Winkler, 2021; Potapov, 2021) alters surface energy partitioning, moisture recycling, and the terrestrial carbon sink simultaneously, so that changes in one budget cannot be assessed in isolation from the others. High-resolution, uncertainty-quantified soil information (Poggio, 2021) and process-level understanding of soil carbon stabilization (Kleber, 2021) (Angst, 2021) (Georgiou, 2022) provide the terrestrial state variables that the coupled assessment requires.

The hydrological context is one of increasing stress and increasing reliance on buffering reservoirs. Groundwater is assessed as central to a resilient water future precisely because surface water is becoming more variable (Scanlon, 2023), while soil salinization threatens the productive capacity of irrigated and rainfed land over the coming century (Hassani, 2021). Inland-water biogeochemistry is changing in step: lake deoxygenation (Jane, 2021) illustrates how a physical change (warming and stratification) propagates into a biogeochemical state change with its own feedbacks. The assessment treats these findings as a coherent picture of a hydrological system moving out of its Holocene envelope, consistent with the planetary-boundaries diagnosis for freshwater change (Richardson, 2023).

### 1.2.5 Why the context demands an ensemble representation

**The heterogeneity, coupling, and threshold behavior documented in the observed record make a single-trajectory representation inadequate, and the ensemble representation adopted by this volume is a response to the structure of the evidence rather than a stylistic choice (*high confidence*).** The context assembled in this section — a structured forced signal (Rantanen, 2022; Pepin, 2022), coupled terrestrial and hydrological change (Winkler, 2021) (Scanlon, 2023) (Hassani, 2021), compound and abrupt extremes (Yuan, 2023) (Abram, 2021) (Moore, 2021), and a biosphere that is itself a driver (Folke, 2021) — cannot be summarized by a single number or a single trajectory without discarding exactly the information that matters for assessment.

The Variant Earths ensemble is constructed to retain that information. Each member carries a full, physically consistent field for every assessed variable, so that couplings and joint behavior are represented internally rather than reconstructed after the fact. The width and internal structure of the ensemble encode the uncertainty, and the fraction of members exhibiting any specified behavior is the assessment's fundamental probabilistic output. The methodological sections that follow specify how the ensemble is built, how observational and model evidence are combined to constrain it, and how its uncertainties are quantified and reported. The context, in short, determines the method: because the system is coupled, structured, and threshold-prone, the assessment must be ensemble-based, uncertainty-explicit, and calibrated.

## 1.3 Methods: Building and Constraining the Ensemble

### 1.3.1 Observational products, reanalyses, and the common footing

**Observational and reanalysis products provide the anchor member of the ensemble — the observed Earth — and their assessed uncertainty is propagated into every comparison rather than treated as negligible (*high confidence*).** The assessment draws on gridded reanalysis and remote-sensing products that combine sparse in-situ observations with physical models to produce spatially complete fields, and it treats the reanalysis not as ground truth but as a member whose own uncertainty must be characterized. Land-surface reanalyses that assimilate observations into a physically consistent framework provide the terrestrial state fields the assessment requires, and their documented performance and known biases set limits on the precision of any comparison against them. The Copernicus land reanalysis programme is one such product used here as institutional context, and the assessment follows its published uncertainty characterization rather than assuming a perfect reference.

Soil and land-cover products are treated analogously. Global soil information with quantified spatial uncertainty (Poggio, 2021) and global maps of cropland extent and change (Potapov, 2021) enter the assessment as fields with per-pixel uncertainty, and the framing requires that this uncertainty be carried forward when these fields constrain ensemble members. The discipline here is the same as in Section 1.1.5: a product's uncertainty is an attribute of the product, and comparisons are made only among quantities that share a compatible uncertainty representation. Where a product reports uncertainty only implicitly, the assessment reconstructs an explicit representation before use, and where it cannot, it downgrades the confidence of any statement depending on that product.

Bringing products onto a common footing is a technical operation with scientific consequences. Regridding a high-resolution soil product (Poggio, 2021) to the resolution of a coarse coupled model discards fine-scale structure that may be physically important, for example in mountainous terrain where climate change is elevation-dependent (Pepin, 2022). The assessment records these operations explicitly and reports the information loss they entail, because a comparison that appears precise after aggressive regridding may be less informative than a coarser comparison that preserves the relevant structure. This is the observational face of the incommensurability discipline established in the framing.

### 1.3.2 Physical models and coordinated scenario projections

**Coupled physical models, run under coordinated and shared scenarios, generate the dynamical backbone of the ensemble, and their coordination is what makes cross-model uncertainty assessable rather than anecdotal (*high confidence*).** The assessment relies on projections produced under a common experimental design in which participating models are driven by identical scenario forcings, so that the spread among models can be interpreted as a structured estimate of model uncertainty (Tebaldi, 2021). The scenario design spans a range of forcing pathways, and each pathway defines a subspace of the Variant Earths ensemble: the members consistent with that pathway. This is the point at which the framing and the modeling infrastructure meet, because the coordinated projections are, in effect, a sampling of the pathway-conditioned ensemble.

The coordinated design has three properties that the assessment exploits. First, because the forcings are shared, differences among members reflect model structure and internal variability rather than differing assumptions, which is a precondition for interpreting the spread. Second, because multiple initial conditions are available for many models, internal variability can be partially separated from forced response, allowing the assessment to distinguish the width of the ensemble due to chaotic variability from the width due to structural uncertainty. Third, because the design is documented and reproducible, the assessment can state precisely which members it is drawing on, satisfying the requirement that comparisons be made only among enumerated members.

The assessment is candid about the limits of the coordinated design. Model spread is not a calibrated probability distribution; it is a sample from an ensemble of opportunity whose members are neither independent nor equally credible. Some models share components and biases, so the effective number of independent members is smaller than the nominal count. The assessment therefore does not treat the raw model spread as a likelihood; it treats it as one input to a constrained estimate, weighted where possible by each member's ability to reproduce the structured observational constraints of Section 1.2 — the Arctic amplification rate (Rantanen, 2022), elevation-dependent warming (Pepin, 2022), the carbon-budget partitioning (Friedlingstein, 2022), and the terrestrial-hydrological state (Scanlon, 2023) (Hassani, 2021). Members that fail these constraints are down-weighted or excluded, and the basis for each exclusion is recorded.

### 1.3.3 Machine-learning emulators and data-driven forecasting

**Machine-learning methods are assessed as a mature and increasingly central component of the ensemble-generation pipeline — as fast emulators, as data-driven forecasters, and as tools for constraining physical models — and their integration is governed by explicit rules on physical consistency and uncertainty (*high confidence*).** Data-driven global weather forecasting has reached skill competitive with or exceeding traditional numerical prediction for medium-range forecasts, with neural architectures producing accurate global forecasts at substantially lower computational cost (Bi, 2023). This capability is directly relevant to ensemble generation, because the principal obstacle to large ensembles is computational: a fast, skillful emulator makes it feasible to sample the ensemble densely enough to characterize its tails, which is exactly where the impact-relevant extremes of Section 1.2.3 reside.

The assessment distinguishes three roles for machine learning and applies different governance to each. In the first role, machine learning acts as an emulator of a physical model, reproducing its input–output behavior at lower cost; here the governing requirement is fidelity to the emulated model and honest reporting of emulation error. In the second role, machine learning acts as a data-driven forecaster or downscaler trained directly on observations (Bi, 2023); here the governing requirement is physical consistency — the outputs must respect conservation laws and boundary conditions to the extent claimed — and the assessment draws on physics-informed methods that embed physical constraints into the learning process (Karniadakis, 2021) (Cuomo, 2022). In the third role, machine learning acts as a discovery or classification tool, for example in ecological monitoring (Tuia, 2022) or in the broader programme of applying learning methods to climate problems (Kaack, 2022); here the governing requirement is that the tool's outputs be validated against independent evidence before entering the assessment.

Physics-informed machine learning is treated with particular attention because it directly addresses the central risk of data-driven methods: physically inconsistent output that is nonetheless statistically plausible. By embedding governing equations into the loss function or architecture, physics-informed neural networks constrain the learned solution to the physically admissible manifold (Karniadakis, 2021), and the survey of where the field stands and what remains open (Cuomo, 2022) informs the assessment's judgment about which applications are mature enough to rely on. The broader programme of integrating scientific knowledge with machine learning for environmental systems provides the methodological frame the assessment adopts (Willard, 2022): machine learning is not a replacement for physical understanding but a means of combining physical constraints with data, and its outputs are admitted to the ensemble only when the physical constraints are demonstrably respected.

The assessment also draws on the rapid progress in general machine-learning methods that underpins these climate applications. Reviews of deep-learning modelling techniques (Ahmed, 2023) and of convolutional architectures for spatial data (Zhao, 2024) document the methods on which climate emulators are built, while advances in atomistic and materials modelling with universal neural-network potentials (Deng, 2023) and in foundation models for tabular data (Hollmann, 2025) illustrate the transferability of the underlying techniques. The relevance to the assessment is methodological rather than substantive: these advances determine what emulation and downscaling capabilities are available, and hence how densely the ensemble can be sampled and how finely it can be resolved. Machine learning in agriculture (Benos, 2021) and in the built environment (Baduge, 2022) similarly indicate the breadth of the toolkit the assessment can draw on for sectoral downscaling, always subject to the physical-consistency and validation requirements above.

### 1.3.4 Ensembles, weighting, and the aggregation of evidence

**The assessment aggregates physical-model members, emulator members, and observationally constrained members into a single weighted ensemble, and the weighting scheme is designed to reflect each member's demonstrated skill against independent constraints rather than to impose an arbitrary prior (*high confidence*).** Ensemble methods are a mature area of machine learning in their own right, and the assessment draws on the understanding of how ensembles reduce variance and improve calibration when their members are diverse and their errors are partially independent (Ganaie, 2022). The same principles govern the multi-method ensemble assembled here: diversity among members is an asset only to the extent that their errors are independent, and the assessment estimates and reports the effective diversity rather than assuming it.

Weighting is the operation by which the assessment converts a heterogeneous collection of members into a constrained estimate. Each member is weighted according to its performance against the structured observational constraints identified in Section 1.2 — a member that reproduces Arctic amplification (Rantanen, 2022), elevation-dependent mountain warming (Pepin, 2022), the observed carbon-budget partitioning (Friedlingstein, 2022) (Friedlingstein, 2023), and the terrestrial-hydrological state (Scanlon, 2023) (Hassani, 2021) (Jane, 2021) is weighted more heavily than one that does not. The assessment is careful to avoid double-counting: where a constraint has been used to train or tune a member, that constraint cannot also be used to weight it, and the assessment tracks which constraints have been used where to prevent circular confirmation. This bookkeeping is a direct implementation of the falsification discipline the volume adopts: a member is credible only insofar as it succeeds against constraints it was not fitted to.

The aggregation also confronts the problem of shared structure among members. Physical models that share components, and emulators trained on the outputs of those models, are not independent, and treating them as independent would produce a falsely narrow ensemble. The assessment estimates the correlation structure among members and inflates the effective uncertainty accordingly, so that the reported ensemble width reflects the true, smaller number of independent lines of evidence. This is the ensemble analogue of the common-method-bias problem well documented in the methodological literature (Podsakoff, 2023): when apparent independent confirmations in fact share a method or a source, the naive combination overstates confidence, and the assessment corrects for this explicitly.

### 1.3.5 Uncertainty quantification and its propagation

**Every assessed quantity carries an uncertainty that is quantified using methods appropriate to its source and propagated through every subsequent operation, and this propagation — not the point estimate — is the assessment's principal deliverable (*high confidence*).** Uncertainty in the ensemble arises from several distinguishable sources: observational error in the anchor member, internal variability sampled across initial conditions, structural uncertainty across models and methods, and the additional uncertainty introduced by machine-learning emulation. The assessment adopts the formal decomposition of uncertainty in deep neural networks — separating aleatoric uncertainty, which is irreducible given the data, from epistemic uncertainty, which reflects limited knowledge and can in principle be reduced — as the organizing scheme for the machine-learning contributions (Gawlikowski, 2023). This decomposition matters because the two kinds of uncertainty behave differently under additional data and must not be conflated when the assessment reports what could be learned by further observation.

Propagation is handled by carrying full distributions, or adequate approximations to them, through each stage of the pipeline rather than propagating point estimates with error bars attached at the end. When a data-driven emulator (Bi, 2023) is used to expand a physical ensemble, the emulator's own predictive uncertainty (Gawlikowski, 2023) is added to the physical spread rather than ignored, and when a physics-informed method (Karniadakis, 2021) (Cuomo, 2022) is used to enforce a constraint, the residual violation of that constraint is quantified and propagated as an additional uncertainty term. The assessment's rule is that no operation may reduce the reported uncertainty unless it adds genuine, independent information; operations that merely reprocess existing information must preserve or increase the uncertainty, never spuriously shrink it.

The assessment is explicit that some uncertainties resist quantification and must be handled as such. Structural uncertainty about processes that no member represents — a missing mechanism rather than a spread in a represented one — cannot be captured by the ensemble width and is reported separately as a limitation, following the treatment of threshold behavior in Section 1.2.3. The anomalous sea-ice arch collapse (Moore, 2021) is an example: if few or no members support the mechanism, the ensemble width understates the true uncertainty, and the assessment says so rather than reporting a falsely narrow range. This candor about the limits of quantified uncertainty is a governance requirement, specified in Section 1.4, and it is the mechanism by which the assessment avoids the overconfidence that a purely ensemble-internal uncertainty estimate would produce.

### 1.3.6 Reproducibility, provenance, and the source-first discipline

**The assessment is built under a source-first discipline in which every quantitative claim is traceable to an identified, verifiable source, and this provenance requirement is enforced procedurally rather than left to individual diligence (*high confidence*).** In practice this means that each assessed statement is linked to the specific products, model outputs, and published analyses that support it, and that the chain from raw evidence to assessed conclusion is recorded so that it can be independently re-examined. The requirement is deliberately strict: a claim that cannot be traced to an identified source is not admitted to the assessment, even if it is widely believed, because an untraceable claim cannot be verified and therefore cannot be assigned calibrated confidence.

Reproducibility extends to the methods themselves. The coordinated scenario design (Tebaldi, 2021) is reproducible because its experimental protocol is documented; the machine-learning components are reproducible to the extent that their architectures, training data, and validation procedures are documented (Bi, 2023) (Karniadakis, 2021) (Ahmed, 2023); and the observational products carry documented provenance and uncertainty (Poggio, 2021; Potapov, 2021). Where a component's provenance is incomplete — for example, a proprietary training set or an undocumented preprocessing step — the assessment flags the resulting statements as having reduced reproducibility, and this flag propagates into the confidence language of Section 1.4. The source-first discipline is therefore not a bureaucratic overlay; it is the mechanism that makes the calibrated confidence statements meaningful, because a confidence statement is only as trustworthy as the traceability of the evidence beneath it.

## 1.4 Calibrated Language, Evidence Handling, and Governance

### 1.4.1 The two-axis calibrated vocabulary

**The assessment uses a two-axis calibrated vocabulary in which confidence terms express the assessed robustness of evidence and agreement, and likelihood terms express quantified probability, and the two axes are kept distinct because conflating them is a primary source of miscommunicated uncertainty (*high confidence*).** A confidence statement — for example, that a finding holds with *high confidence* — summarizes the type, amount, quality, and consistency of the underlying evidence together with the degree of agreement among independent lines. A likelihood statement — for example, that an outcome is *likely* or *virtually certain* — is reserved for cases where the evidence supports a quantified probability, whether derived from the ensemble fraction of Section 1.1.5 or from an explicit probabilistic analysis. The assessment does not attach likelihood language to statements that lack a quantified probabilistic basis, and it does not attach confidence language as a substitute for a probability when a probability is warranted.

The mapping from evidence to language is specified rather than left to intuition. When a finding is supported by multiple independent lines of high-quality evidence that agree, it earns high confidence; when the lines are fewer, of lower quality, or in tension, the confidence is correspondingly reduced. When a probability is derived from the weighted ensemble, the likelihood term reflects the ensemble fraction after the weighting and uncertainty-inflation procedures of Section 1.3.4 and 1.3.5, so that the term carries the corrections for shared structure and emulation uncertainty rather than the raw fraction. This specification is what allows the calibrated language to be reproducible: two assessors following the same procedure on the same evidence should arrive at the same term.

The assessment is explicit about the interaction between the two axes. A quantified likelihood can itself be held with varying confidence: an outcome may be assessed as *likely* with only medium confidence if the ensemble supporting the probability is narrow or poorly constrained. The volume reports both where they differ, rather than collapsing them into a single term, because the reader's needs differ — a decision sensitive to the tails needs the confidence in the probability, not only the probability. This dual reporting is a deliberate departure from any convention that would report a single number, and it follows directly from the ensemble framing, in which the probability and the confidence in the probability are distinct, separately estimable quantities.

### 1.4.2 Evidence handling and the falsification discipline

**Every finding is subjected to an explicit falsification discipline before it is assigned calibrated language, and this discipline requires that credible alternative explanations and the possibility of artifact be actively tested rather than passively acknowledged (*high confidence*).** The discipline has a fixed structure. First, a finding must be checked against a null or baseline expectation: a claimed signal must be shown to exceed what internal variability or observational error alone would produce. Second, the finding must be checked for method-induced artifact, because apparent signals can arise from shared preprocessing, from the reuse of a constraint in both training and evaluation, or from correlated errors among nominally independent sources. Third, the finding must survive an audit of its underlying data for silent-failure signatures — default or fallback values, zero-variance records, or missing data coded as a value — before it is compared against any historical baseline.

The methodological literature the assessment draws on makes clear why each step is necessary. Common-method bias — the inflation of apparent agreement when independent-seeming measures in fact share a method — is widespread, consequential, and not easily remedied after the fact (Podsakoff, 2023), which is why the assessment tests for shared method at the design stage rather than correcting for it afterward. The formal decomposition of predictive uncertainty (Gawlikowski, 2023) provides the tools to distinguish reducible epistemic uncertainty, which further evidence could resolve, from irreducible aleatoric uncertainty, which it cannot, so that a finding is not credited with more robustness than the reducible component of its uncertainty warrants. The falsification discipline is, in effect, the procedural embodiment of the principle that a mechanism that cannot beat its baseline is not a mechanism, whatever else it beats.

The discipline is applied with particular stringency to findings that depend on machine-learning components, because these components can produce plausible output that is physically inconsistent or that reflects training-set idiosyncrasies rather than the target system. A data-driven forecast (Bi, 2023) that agrees with observations over its training period must be shown to generalize before its agreement is credited, and a physics-informed method (Karniadakis, 2021) (Cuomo, 2022) that enforces a constraint must be shown to do so within a quantified tolerance before the constraint is treated as satisfied. The assessment records, for each machine-learning-dependent finding, which falsification tests it passed and which it did not, and downgrades the calibrated language accordingly. This is the mechanism by which the assessment prevents the appearance of confirmation from substituting for genuine, independent verification.

### 1.4.3 Ground-truth integrity and contamination audits

**Before any assessed quantity is compared against historical scores, labels, or reference products, those references are audited for integrity, and the contamination rate is reported and used to define the clean subset on which key comparisons are rerun (*high confidence*).** The audit targets the specific failure modes that silently corrupt reference data: constant or fallback values written by a failed process, records with zero variance where variance is expected, and missing values encoded as legitimate data. These signatures are detectable, and the assessment treats their detection as a precondition for comparison rather than as an optional check. Where contamination is found, the assessment reports the fraction of the reference affected and reruns the affected comparisons on the uncontaminated subset, reporting both results so that the effect of the contamination is visible.

This requirement is not abstract caution; it reflects a documented failure mode in which a large fraction of a reference dataset consisted of fallback values that masqueraded as genuine scores, producing findings that were entirely artifacts of the contamination. The assessment's rule is therefore categorical: no comparison against a historical reference is reported until the reference has passed an integrity audit, and the audit result travels with the finding. The observational products the assessment relies on — soil information (Poggio, 2021), cropland maps (Potapov, 2021), and the reanalysis fields used as the anchor member — are subjected to this audit, and any regions or periods flagged as contaminated are excluded from the constraint calculations of Section 1.3.4 rather than allowed to influence the weighting.

The integrity audit interacts with the uncertainty propagation of Section 1.3.5. A contaminated reference does not merely bias a comparison; it can produce a falsely narrow uncertainty by injecting spurious agreement, exactly as shared method does (Podsakoff, 2023). The assessment therefore treats contamination as a source of uncertainty inflation as well as a source of bias: when contamination is detected and the clean subset is smaller, the reduced effective sample size widens the reported uncertainty, and this widening is propagated forward. The combined effect is that a finding resting on a partly contaminated reference is reported with both a corrected point estimate and an appropriately widened uncertainty, and its calibrated language reflects both corrections.

### 1.4.4 Governance of machine-learning and generative tools in the assessment

**The use of machine-learning and generative tools within the assessment process is governed by explicit rules requiring physical consistency, validation against independent evidence, and disclosure of the tool's role, because these tools introduce distinctive failure modes that ungoverned use would conceal (*high confidence*).** The governance distinguishes between tools that generate scientific content — emulators and forecasters (Bi, 2023) (Karniadakis, 2021) — and tools that assist the assessment process itself, such as classification, retrieval, or drafting aids. For the former, the governing requirements are those of Section 1.3.3: physical consistency, honest emulation-error reporting, and independent validation. For the latter, the governance requires that the tool's output be treated as a hypothesis to be verified, never as evidence in itself, and that its use be disclosed so that the provenance chain of Section 1.3.6 remains intact.

The assessment draws on the broader analysis of trustworthy artificial intelligence to structure this governance, adopting the movement from high-level principles and ethics through concrete requirements to responsible, auditable systems (Díaz-Rodríguez, 2023). The relevant requirements for the assessment are traceability, robustness, and the disclosure of limitations, each of which maps onto a specific assessment practice: traceability onto the source-first provenance discipline, robustness onto the falsification and integrity audits, and disclosure of limitations onto the calibrated-language mapping. The assessment also takes seriously the documented risks that generative tools introduce, including the generation of fluent but unfounded content and the security and integrity concerns that accompany their deployment (Gupta, 2023), and it applies the corresponding controls: no generated claim enters the assessment without independent verification, and the possibility of tool-induced error is included in the falsification discipline of Section 1.4.2.

The governance is deliberately conservative about the epistemic status of generative tools. Surveys of large language models document rapid capability growth alongside persistent failure modes (Zhao, 2026), and analyses of their organizational adoption emphasize both their utility and the need for human oversight (Budhwar, 2023). The assessment's position is that such tools can accelerate the mechanical aspects of assessment — retrieval, formatting, consistency checking — but cannot be delegated the judgments that the calibrated language encodes, because those judgments require the falsification and integrity procedures that only an accountable human assessor, following the specified discipline, can certify. Every tool-assisted step is therefore subject to human verification, and the assessment records where tools were used so that the reader can weigh the disclosure.

### 1.4.5 Variance, determinism, and honest reporting of trials

**The assessment reports the variance structure of its findings honestly, distinguishing genuinely stochastic estimates with quantified trial-to-trial variance from deterministic procedures whose apparent stability reflects the absence of independent trials rather than robustness (*high confidence*).** A deterministic method run many times produces identical output every time, and reporting that stability as though it were the low variance of many independent trials would badly overstate confidence. The assessment therefore flags deterministic procedures explicitly and does not present their reproducibility as evidence of robustness. Where the ensemble supplies genuine trial-to-trial variance — across initial conditions, across models, across resampled training sets — the assessment reports that variance and the number of effectively independent trials behind it, corrected for the shared-structure effects of Section 1.3.4.

This requirement bears directly on the machine-learning components. A trained emulator applied repeatedly to the same input is deterministic, and its repeated agreement with itself is not evidence of anything; only its performance across genuinely independent inputs, or across independently trained instances, carries information about its reliability. Ensemble learning provides the appropriate framework, because a properly constructed ensemble of models exposes the variance due to model choice and training stochasticity (Ganaie, 2022), and the assessment uses such ensembles precisely to convert an otherwise deterministic prediction into an estimate with quantified variance. The predictive-uncertainty decomposition (Gawlikowski, 2023) then attributes that variance to its aleatoric and epistemic components, so that the reader can see how much of it further evidence could reduce.

The honest reporting of variance closes the loop with the calibrated language of Section 1.4.1. A finding's likelihood term derives from the ensemble fraction; its confidence term derives, in part, from the variance structure behind that fraction and from the number of independent trials supporting it. A probability estimated from a single deterministic run, however stable, cannot earn high confidence, because it has no trial variance to characterize; a probability estimated from a diverse, well-sampled ensemble with corrected effective size can. By making the variance structure explicit and by refusing to present determinism as robustness, the assessment ensures that its confidence language reflects the true independence of the evidence rather than the superficial stability of repeated identical computations. This is the final safeguard against the overconfidence that an uncritical reading of the ensemble would otherwise invite.

## 1.5 Assessment Workflow, Cross-Cutting Methods, and Roadmap

### 1.5.1 The end-to-end assessment workflow

**The assessment follows a fixed end-to-end workflow — from evidence intake through constraint, aggregation, uncertainty propagation, and calibrated reporting — and fixing the workflow is what makes the volume's conclusions reproducible and auditable rather than a curated selection (*high confidence*).** The workflow begins with evidence intake under the source-first discipline of Section 1.3.6: every product, model output, and published analysis is registered with its provenance and uncertainty before it can be used. Intake is followed by the integrity audit of Section 1.4.3, which screens references for silent-failure signatures and defines the clean subsets on which comparisons will be run. Only after intake and audit does the substantive assessment begin, so that no finding rests on unregistered or unaudited evidence.

The substantive stages proceed in a fixed order. Physical-model members and machine-learning members are generated or ingested (Section 1.3.2, 1.3.3); each member is scored against the structured observational constraints of Section 1.2 and weighted accordingly, with the anti-double-counting bookkeeping of Section 1.3.4 enforced; the weighted members are aggregated into a constrained ensemble whose effective size is corrected for shared structure; uncertainties are propagated through every operation (Section 1.3.5); and the resulting distributions are mapped onto calibrated language (Section 1.4.1) after passing the falsification discipline (Section 1.4.2) and the variance-honesty check (Section 1.4.5). The workflow is deliberately linear in its dependencies so that the provenance of any conclusion can be traced backward through it, and any conclusion whose chain is broken — by an unaudited reference, an unvalidated tool, or a failed falsification test — is either repaired or reported with reduced confidence.

The workflow is instrumented so that its execution can be verified independently. Each assessed statement carries a record of which members supported it, which constraints weighted them, which falsification tests it passed, and which uncertainty terms were propagated into it. This instrumentation is the operational meaning of the source-first build: a reader with access to the record can reconstruct the path from evidence to conclusion and check each step. The volume treats this reconstructability as a non-negotiable property, because a calibrated confidence statement whose derivation cannot be reconstructed is indistinguishable from an assertion, and the assessment's entire value rests on the difference between the two.

### 1.5.2 Cross-cutting methodological themes

**Several methodological themes recur across every domain of the assessment — the primacy of coupled representation, the discipline of common footing, the correction for shared structure, and the separation of reducible from irreducible uncertainty — and stating them once here allows later chapters to invoke them without re-deriving them (*high confidence*).** The primacy of coupled representation, established in Section 1.2.4, means that terrestrial, hydrological, cryospheric, and atmospheric evidence are assessed jointly rather than in isolation, because their state variables move coherently (Winkler, 2021) (Scanlon, 2023) (Hassani, 2021) (Jane, 2021) (Moore, 2021). Later chapters treat this as a default: a finding about one component that is inconsistent with the coupled behavior of the others is flagged, because the coupling is a constraint that individual-component analyses can violate only at the cost of physical consistency.

The discipline of common footing, established in Section 1.1.5 and operationalized in Section 1.3.1, recurs whenever products of differing resolution, baseline, or uncertainty representation are compared. The correction for shared structure, established in Section 1.3.4, recurs whenever apparently independent lines of evidence are combined, and it draws throughout on the recognition that shared method inflates apparent agreement (Podsakoff, 2023). The separation of reducible epistemic from irreducible aleatoric uncertainty, established in Section 1.3.5 using the formal decomposition for learned models (Gawlikowski, 2023), recurs whenever the assessment states what further observation could and could not resolve. These four themes are the methodological spine of the volume, and their consistent application is what gives the assessment its internal coherence.

A fifth theme is the treatment of machine learning as an integrated but governed component rather than a separate track. Across domains, machine-learning methods appear as emulators, downscalers, and discovery tools (Bi, 2023) (Karniadakis, 2021) (Cuomo, 2022) (Willard, 2022) (Tuia, 2022) (Kaack, 2022), and in every case they are subject to the same physical-consistency, validation, and disclosure requirements (Section 1.4.4), drawing on the trustworthy-AI framework (Díaz-Rodríguez, 2023) and the honest accounting of their failure modes (Gupta, 2023) (Zhao, 2026). The assessment's position is that machine learning has become indispensable for sampling the ensemble densely enough to characterize its tails, and precisely because it is indispensable it must be governed rigorously; the two propositions are complementary, not in tension.

### 1.5.3 The evidentiary base and its breadth

**The evidentiary base of this volume is deliberately broad, spanning physical, biological, methodological, and technological literatures, because the coupled Earth-system framing requires evidence from every domain that materially constrains the ensemble (*high confidence*).** The physical and Earth-system evidence anchors the assessment of state and drivers: planetary boundaries (Richardson, 2023), the carbon budget (Friedlingstein, 2022) (Friedlingstein, 2023), regional amplification (Rantanen, 2022; Pepin, 2022), and cryospheric and hydrological change (Moore, 2021) (Scanlon, 2023) (Hassani, 2021) (Jane, 2021). The biological and ecological evidence establishes the biosphere as an active driver (Folke, 2021) and constrains the terrestrial system through soil-carbon process understanding (Kleber, 2021) (Angst, 2021) (Georgiou, 2022), land-use dynamics (Winkler, 2021; Potapov, 2021), and the responses of pathogens, pests, and agriculture to warming (Singh, 2023) (Jägermeyr, 2021) (Tang, 2021).

The methodological evidence supplies the tools: coordinated scenario design (Tebaldi, 2021), physics-informed and data-driven learning (Karniadakis, 2021) (Cuomo, 2022) (Bi, 2023) (Willard, 2022), ensemble methods (Ganaie, 2022), uncertainty quantification (Gawlikowski, 2023), the general deep-learning toolkit (Ahmed, 2023) (Zhao, 2024) (Deng, 2023) (Hollmann, 2025), and the methodological literature on bias and trustworthiness (Podsakoff, 2023; Díaz-Rodríguez, 2023). The technological and applied evidence constrains the plausibility of forcing pathways and the breadth of the downscaling toolkit: energy-carrier and conversion chemistry (Cullen, 2021) (Valera-Medina, 2021) (Monteiro, 2021) (Yang, 2023), and applied machine learning in agriculture and the built environment (Benos, 2021) (Baduge, 2022). The assessment also situates its findings within the wider evidence on human consequences where that evidence validates the physical characterization of hazards (Romanello, 2022) (Romanello, 2023) (Zhao, 2021) (Aguilera, 2021) and within the economic context that motivates the assessment's precision (Rennert, 2022).

The breadth of this base is a methodological commitment, not an accident of collection. Because the Variant Earths framing defines a member by its full, coupled state — physical, biological, and, through forcing pathways, technological — the evidence that constrains a member must be correspondingly broad. A narrow evidentiary base would leave large parts of each member unconstrained and would produce an ensemble that is wide in the wrong places, permissive where evidence exists and falsely confident where it does not. The assessment's breadth is the means by which the ensemble is constrained everywhere the evidence allows, and the source-first discipline ensures that each strand of this broad base is traceable and auditable.

### 1.5.4 Key limitations and known failure modes

**The assessment states its principal limitations explicitly, because a methods chapter that concealed its own failure modes would violate the disclosure discipline it imposes on every finding (*high confidence*).** The first limitation is structural uncertainty that no member represents. Where a mechanism is absent from the entire ensemble — a threshold process such as the sea-ice arch collapse (Moore, 2021) that few or no members support — the ensemble width understates the true uncertainty, and the assessment can flag but not quantify the shortfall. This is an irreducible limitation of any ensemble-based method, and the volume handles it by reporting such cases as statements of possibility with explicit low confidence rather than as quantified likelihoods.

The second limitation is the non-independence of the evidentiary base. Physical models share components, emulators are trained on model output, and methodological choices propagate across studies, so the effective number of independent lines of evidence is smaller than the nominal count. The assessment corrects for this where it can estimate the correlation structure (Section 1.3.4), drawing on the recognition that shared method inflates apparent agreement (Podsakoff, 2023), but the correction is itself uncertain, and residual overconfidence from unrecognized shared structure cannot be excluded. The third limitation is the maturity of the machine-learning components. Data-driven methods are advancing rapidly (Bi, 2023) (Ahmed, 2023) (Zhao, 2024) (Hollmann, 2025), which means both that their capabilities are growing and that their failure modes are incompletely characterized; the assessment governs them conservatively (Section 1.4.4) precisely because their long-run reliability under distribution shift is not yet fully established, a caution reinforced by the documented failure modes of generative systems (Gupta, 2023) (Zhao, 2026).

The fourth limitation is the incompleteness and heterogeneity of the observational record. The anchor member is itself uncertain, observational products carry spatially varying uncertainty (Poggio, 2021; Potapov, 2021), and coverage is uneven in space and time, so the constraints that weight the ensemble are stronger in well-observed regions and periods than in poorly observed ones. The assessment reports this heterogeneity rather than smoothing over it, and it refrains from claiming uniform confidence across regions where the observational constraint is uneven. Taken together, these limitations define the honest boundary of what the volume can assert: the assessment is strongest where the evidence is broad, independent, well-observed, and mechanistically represented, and it says so, and it correspondingly reduces its confidence where any of these conditions fails.

### 1.5.5 Roadmap to the volume

**The remaining chapters apply the framing, evidentiary base, and methods established here to the successive components of the Variant Earths ensemble, and the reader should carry forward from this chapter the conventions that govern every subsequent assessment statement (*high confidence*).** Subsequent chapters assess the observed state of each component in detail, attribute the observed changes to their drivers by comparing the observed member against counterfactual members, and project the evolution of the ensemble under the scenario-defined pathways (Tebaldi, 2021). Each of these tasks uses the machinery of this chapter without re-deriving it: the coupled representation, the common-footing discipline, the weighted-ensemble aggregation with shared-structure correction, the propagated uncertainty, and the calibrated language.

The reader should carry forward four conventions in particular. First, every likelihood term denotes a quantified probability derived from the corrected ensemble fraction, and every confidence term denotes assessed evidential robustness, and the two are distinct (Section 1.4.1). Second, every finding has passed the falsification discipline and the integrity audit before receiving its calibrated language (Section 1.4.2, 1.4.3), and where it has not, its reduced confidence is stated. Third, machine-learning-derived findings are governed, validated, and disclosed (Section 1.4.4), and their contribution to uncertainty is propagated rather than hidden (Gawlikowski, 2023). Fourth, deterministic procedures are never presented as robust on the strength of their repeatability (Section 1.4.5). These conventions are the connective tissue of the volume, and their consistent application is what allows the individual chapters to combine into a single, coherent, uncertainty-explicit assessment of the variant-Earths ensemble.

The volume closes the loop opened in Section 1.1: the observed Earth is one member of a structured ensemble, the assessment's task is to characterize that ensemble — its observed member, its counterfactual members, and its projected members — under a common calibrated vocabulary, and the methods established here are the means by which that characterization is made traceable, reproducible, and honest about its own uncertainty. Everything that follows is an application of this framing to the evidence, and the reader is now equipped to read the subsequent chapters as such.

## 1.6 Counterfactual Construction, Attribution, and Scenario Design

### 1.6.1 Counterfactual members and the logic of attribution

**Attribution in this volume is defined as a comparison between the observed member and counterfactual members of the ensemble that differ only in a specified driver, and defining attribution this way makes it a statement about the ensemble rather than about an isolated trajectory (*high confidence*).** A counterfactual member is a physically consistent Earth state constructed under an altered forcing history — most importantly, a history without the anthropogenic carbon perturbation quantified in the global carbon budget (Friedlingstein, 2022) (Friedlingstein, 2023). The attributable effect of a driver on a given quantity is then the difference in the distribution of that quantity between the members with and without the driver, and this difference inherits the uncertainty structure of the ensemble, including the corrections for shared structure and emulation error established in Section 1.3.

This construction has the advantage that attribution and projection use the same machinery. Both are statements about how the ensemble's distribution shifts when a specified condition is changed: for attribution, the condition is the presence or absence of a historical driver; for projection, it is the choice of a future forcing pathway. Because the two share the ensemble representation and the calibrated vocabulary, the volume can report attribution and projection statements that are mutually consistent by construction, rather than reconciling them after the fact. The regional structure of the forced signal — Arctic amplification (Rantanen, 2022), elevation dependence (Pepin, 2022) — is reproduced in the counterfactual comparison, so that attribution statements are spatially resolved rather than global-mean only.

The counterfactual logic is applied with particular care to extremes, where the attributable change is often a change in probability rather than in mean. For a compound event such as the heat–drought–fire sequences discussed in Section 1.2.3 (Yuan, 2023) (Abram, 2021), the attributable effect is the change in the fraction of members exhibiting the joint exceedance, and this fraction-based statement is exactly the quantity the ensemble framing makes well-defined. The assessment reports such changes as changes in likelihood, with the confidence in that likelihood determined by the width and independence of the ensemble supporting it, following the dual-reporting convention of Section 1.4.1.

### 1.6.2 Constructing physically consistent counterfactuals

**Counterfactual members must be physically consistent Earth states, not merely observed fields with a driver subtracted, and enforcing physical consistency is where the methods of Section 1.3 are most heavily exercised (*high confidence*).** A naive counterfactual — the observed field minus an estimated forced response — is not a valid member, because it need not satisfy the conservation laws and dynamical balances that constrain a real Earth state. The assessment therefore constructs counterfactuals by running or emulating the coupled system under the altered forcing, using coordinated model configurations (Tebaldi, 2021) and, where computational cost is prohibitive, physics-informed or data-driven emulators that respect the governing constraints (Karniadakis, 2021) (Cuomo, 2022) (Bi, 2023). The emulator's own uncertainty is propagated into the counterfactual distribution (Gawlikowski, 2023), so that the attributable effect carries the full uncertainty of its construction.

Physical consistency is especially demanding for the terrestrial and hydrological components, whose counterfactual states must remain consistent with soil-carbon dynamics (Kleber, 2021) (Angst, 2021) (Georgiou, 2022), land-use configuration (Winkler, 2021; Potapov, 2021), and the coupled water cycle (Scanlon, 2023) (Hassani, 2021). A counterfactual that altered the atmospheric forcing without correspondingly adjusting the coupled terrestrial state would violate the coupling established in Section 1.2.4, and the assessment rejects such members. This is the counterfactual face of the coupled-representation theme: attribution is credible only if the counterfactual member is coupled as thoroughly as the observed member.

The assessment validates its counterfactuals against the same structured constraints used to weight the forward ensemble. A counterfactual member is admissible only if it reproduces the observed relationships among components in regimes where the driver's effect is small, because a member that cannot match the observed system where the perturbation is weak provides no basis for trusting its behavior where the perturbation is strong. This validation reuses the observational constraints of Section 1.2 under the anti-double-counting rule of Section 1.3.4, so that the constraints used to admit a counterfactual are not also credited as independent confirmation of the attribution it supports.

### 1.6.3 Scenario design and the pathway subspaces

**Future members of the ensemble are organized into subspaces defined by shared, coordinated emissions scenarios, and using coordinated scenarios is what allows projection uncertainty to be decomposed into scenario, model, and internal-variability contributions (*high confidence*).** The scenario framework provides a set of forcing pathways spanning a range of plausible futures, and each pathway defines the subspace of ensemble members consistent with it (Tebaldi, 2021). Projection uncertainty then decomposes naturally: variation across scenarios reflects the choice of pathway, variation across models within a scenario reflects structural uncertainty, and variation across initial conditions within a model reflects internal variability. The assessment reports this decomposition rather than a single lumped range, because the three components behave differently and are relevant to different questions — scenario uncertainty is a matter of societal choice, structural uncertainty is a matter of scientific knowledge, and internal variability is irreducible.

The plausibility of each pathway is constrained by evidence on the feasibility of the underlying transitions, consistent with the scope boundary of Section 1.1.4. The sectoral structure of emissions (Lamb, 2021) determines which pathways are reachable from the present state, and the technological evidence base — low-carbon energy carriers and conversion chemistry (Cullen, 2021) (Valera-Medina, 2021) (Monteiro, 2021) (Yang, 2023) — constrains the rate at which sectoral emissions can plausibly decline. The assessment does not assign probabilities to the pathways themselves, because their realization depends on societal choices outside the physical-science remit; it assesses, conditional on each pathway, the distribution of physical outcomes across the corresponding ensemble subspace.

Scenario design also governs how the assessment handles the deep-future and the deep-tail. Because the impact-relevant risk often lies in the tails (Section 1.2.3), the assessment samples each pathway subspace densely enough to characterize its tail behavior, which is feasible only because fast emulators (Bi, 2023) make large ensembles affordable. The tail members are subject to the same falsification and consistency requirements as the central members, so that a low-probability, high-impact member is admitted only if it is physically consistent and survives the falsification discipline, never merely because it is extreme. This prevents the tails from being populated by artifacts while ensuring that genuine tail risk is represented.

### 1.6.4 Bringing attribution and projection into a single frame

**The volume reports attribution and projection within a single ensemble frame so that the observed, counterfactual, and projected members are directly comparable, and this unification is the central methodological payoff of the Variant Earths framing (*high confidence*).** Because all three kinds of member — observed, counterfactual, projected — are represented in the same coupled, uncertainty-quantified ensemble, statements about them are commensurable without further translation. The attributable warming to date, the counterfactual warming that would have occurred without the anthropogenic perturbation, and the projected warming under each pathway are all differences of distributions within one ensemble, computed with one uncertainty calculus and reported in one calibrated vocabulary.

This unification pays off most clearly where attribution and projection meet: in statements about how an already-attributable change will evolve under a given pathway. The assessment can, for instance, express the projected change in a compound-extreme frequency (Yuan, 2023) (Abram, 2021) as a continuation of an attributable historical change, with the historical and future components computed consistently and their uncertainties propagated together. Such statements are difficult to make coherently when attribution and projection use separate frameworks, and they are natural in the ensemble frame. The regional structure carries through: because the ensemble reproduces amplification patterns (Rantanen, 2022; Pepin, 2022) in both the counterfactual and projected members, the joined attribution–projection statements are spatially resolved.

The unified frame also disciplines the reporting of confidence. A joined statement inherits the weaker of the confidence levels of its components: if the attribution is high-confidence but the projection under a given pathway is only medium-confidence, the joined statement is reported at medium confidence, and the reason is stated. This propagation of the weaker confidence prevents the unified frame from manufacturing false confidence by combining a strong component with a weak one, and it follows directly from the evidence-handling discipline of Section 1.4.2. The result is an assessment in which every statement — observational, attributive, or projective — is located in the same ensemble, quantified with the same calculus, and reported in the same honest, calibrated language, which is precisely the coherence the framing was introduced to achieve.

### 1.6.5 Observational networks, data stewardship, and continuity

**The credibility of every ensemble statement ultimately rests on the continuity and stewardship of the observational networks that define the anchor member, and the assessment treats observational continuity as a methodological prerequisite rather than an external given (*high confidence*).** The structured constraints that weight the ensemble — the Arctic amplification rate (Rantanen, 2022), the carbon-budget partitioning (Friedlingstein, 2022) (Friedlingstein, 2023), the terrestrial and hydrological state (Poggio, 2021) (Potapov, 2021) (Scanlon, 2023) — are only as strong as the underlying observing systems, and gaps or discontinuities in those systems propagate directly into weakened constraints and widened ensembles. The assessment therefore documents the provenance and continuity of each observational product it relies on, and it reports where a constraint is weakened by observational limitation rather than by genuine physical uncertainty.

Specialized regional and process-specific records are part of this stewardship. Long, homogenized event databases — for example, curated tropical-cyclone records maintained by national meteorological services (Lu, 2021) — provide the multi-decadal continuity needed to characterize the statistics of high-impact events, and their careful construction is what allows the assessment to distinguish a genuine change in event statistics from an artifact of changing observing practice. The integrity audit of Section 1.4.3 applies with full force to such records, because a discontinuity in observing practice can masquerade as a physical trend, and the falsification discipline of Section 1.4.2 requires that any claimed trend be shown to survive correction for such discontinuities before it is credited.

Data stewardship also shapes what the assessment can say about the future of its own evidence base. Because the machine-learning components depend on training data drawn from the observational and model archives (Bi, 2023) (Karniadakis, 2021) (Willard, 2022), the continuity of those archives determines whether the emulators can be retrained and revalidated as the system evolves; an emulator trained on a now-discontinued observing system may degrade silently under distribution shift, a risk the governance of Section 1.4.4 is designed to catch. The assessment thus treats observational continuity, data stewardship, and machine-learning reliability as a single connected concern: the ensemble is only as trustworthy as the observations that anchor it, the archives that train its emulators, and the stewardship that keeps both intact over the multi-decadal horizons the assessment addresses.

### 1.6.6 Communicating the ensemble to downstream users

**The assessment communicates the ensemble to downstream working groups and decision-relevant users as a distribution with quantified uncertainty and stated confidence, never as a single trajectory, because collapsing the ensemble to a point estimate discards exactly the tail and coupling information that downstream use requires (*high confidence*).** The primary deliverable to downstream users is therefore not a number but a characterized distribution: the fraction of members exhibiting each outcome of interest, the confidence in that fraction, and the couplings among outcomes that determine joint risk. This is a deliberate design choice, because downstream applications — the health, water, and food-system assessments that build on the physical basis (Romanello, 2022) (Romanello, 2023) (Scanlon, 2023) (Singh, 2023) — are frequently sensitive to the tails and to the joint behavior of hazards rather than to central estimates alone.

The communication follows the two-axis vocabulary of Section 1.4.1 without simplification. A downstream user receives both the likelihood of an outcome and the confidence in that likelihood, so that a user sensitive to low-probability, high-impact members can distinguish a well-constrained probability from a poorly constrained one. The assessment resists pressure to collapse these two axes into a single headline figure, because doing so would strip the confidence information that is often decisive for risk-sensitive users. Where a single summary is unavoidable for communication, the assessment provides it alongside, not in place of, the full distribution and its confidence, and it states which information the summary omits.

The assessment is also explicit about what the ensemble cannot tell downstream users. The structural-uncertainty limitation of Section 1.5.4 means that outcomes depending on mechanisms absent from the ensemble are under-represented, and the assessment communicates these as flagged possibilities rather than allowing their absence from the ensemble to be read as evidence of their improbability. Likewise, the heterogeneity of observational constraint means that the ensemble is better constrained in some regions and periods than others, and the assessment communicates this spatial and temporal variation in confidence rather than presenting a uniform reliability the evidence does not support. The final methodological commitment of this chapter is therefore a communicative one: the ensemble is delivered downstream with its uncertainty, its confidence, its couplings, and its limitations intact, because an assessment that concealed any of these in the interest of a cleaner message would betray the calibrated honesty that is its entire purpose. This commitment binds every chapter that follows: the observed member, its counterfactual siblings, and its projected descendants are reported as one uncertainty-quantified ensemble, in one calibrated vocabulary, with one auditable chain from evidence to conclusion. The framing, context, and methods set out in this chapter are, in the end, a single instrument for keeping that promise, and the remainder of the volume is their application to the physical science basis of the variant-Earths ensemble.
