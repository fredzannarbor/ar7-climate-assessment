# Technical Summary

This Technical Summary condenses the assessment of the physical science basis presented in the ten chapters of this Working Group I volume. It is organized as ten sections, TS.1 through TS.10, each corresponding to one chapter and each opening with a synthesis statement that carries the calibrated confidence of the underlying assessment, followed by technical elaboration of the quantitative ranges the chapters assess. The Variant Earths framing that governs the volume treats the observed climate as one realization drawn from a structured space of physically admissible Earth states; the counterfactual and projected members of that space are constructed to be commensurable with the observed member, so that attribution, projection, scenario analysis, and stabilization science share a single calibrated vocabulary {1.1, 1.6}. Cross-references to chapters and sections are given in curly braces. Calibrated uncertainty language follows the two-axis convention in which likelihood terms express quantified probability and confidence terms express the assessed robustness of evidence and agreement {1.4}.

## TS.1 Framing, Context, and Methods

> **The assessment rests on a single methodological framework in which physical models, observational reanalyses, and machine-learning methods are integrated into one evidentiary pipeline whose uncertainties are explicitly quantified and propagated, and in which the observed Earth is treated as one member of a structured ensemble of physically admissible states (*high confidence*).** Multiple independent lines of evidence establish that the contemporary Earth system has departed the envelope of Holocene variability across several coupled domains, and this displacement is the physical context the assessment must explain (*virtually certain*) {1.1, 1.2}.

The framing chapter establishes why an ensemble representation is necessary and how the members of that ensemble are made comparable. The premise is that attribution and projection are the same operation applied to different members of a common state space: a counterfactual world without the anthropogenic perturbation, the observed world, and a set of projected worlds are all constructed under shared physical constraints so that differences between them can be read as the signature of a forcing rather than as an artifact of incommensurable model configurations {1.1.1, 1.6.1}. The displacement of the Earth system from its Holocene envelope is documented across domains: assessments of planetary boundaries find that six of nine have been transgressed, the global carbon budget shows sustained growth in anthropogenic emissions and atmospheric accumulation, and the biosphere is now assessed as a primary driver of Earth-system trajectory rather than a passive backdrop {1.1.2, 1.1.3}.

Regional amplification of the forced signal is a defining feature of the observed record and a stringent test of any credible ensemble member. Arctic near-surface warming has proceeded nearly four times faster than the global mean since 1979, a magnitude that exceeds the value reproduced by many models and that, together with cryospheric instabilities, constrains the dynamical realism the assessment requires (*high confidence*) {1.1, 1.2.2}. The methodological core combines coordinated multi-model projections under shared scenarios, physics-informed and data-driven emulators, and formal treatments of predictive uncertainty under governance rules that prevent any single method from being reported without its associated confidence (*high confidence*) {1.3.2, 1.3.3, 1.3.4}.

The calibrated-language protocol is applied uniformly: likelihood terms are reserved for quantified probabilistic statements and confidence terms for the assessed robustness of evidence and agreement {1.4.1}. Three disciplines specific to this edition govern the handling of evidence. The first is a falsification discipline requiring that any mechanism, selector, or policy claim be tested against a baseline before it is reported {1.4.2}. The second is a ground-truth integrity audit: historical scores and labels used as reference are screened for silent-failure signatures — default or fallback values, zero-variance records, and missing data coded as a value — before any comparison is drawn against them, and the contamination rate is reported {1.4.3}. The third is honest reporting of variance, in which deterministic policies are flagged as having zero trial variance rather than implied to rest on independent trials {1.4.5}. Machine-learning and generative tools are admitted into the pipeline only under explicit governance that surfaces rather than conceals the biases they introduce, including brittle out-of-distribution behavior and opaque uncertainty {1.4.4}. Reproducibility, provenance, and a source-first discipline — in which every assessed quantity is traceable to its evidentiary origin — complete the methodological foundation {1.3.6}.

The chapter is explicit about scope and about what the volume does not assess: it is a physical-science assessment, and impacts, adaptation, and mitigation policy are addressed only insofar as they bear on the interpretation of physical change {1.1.4}. The end-to-end workflow — from observational products and reanalyses through model projection, emulation, ensemble weighting, uncertainty propagation, and calibrated reporting — is designed so that the counterfactual, observed, and projected members remain commensurable throughout {1.5.1, 1.6.4}. Known failure modes, including the systematic underestimation of polar amplification in parts of the model ensemble and the entanglement of aerosol and cloud uncertainties, are declared at the outset rather than discovered downstream {1.5.4}.

## TS.2 Large-Scale Changes in the Climate System and Their Causes

> **The climate system as a whole has continued to warm, and the imprint of human influence is now detectable across every major component — atmosphere, ocean, cryosphere, and biosphere — with multiple independent lines of evidence converging on the same conclusion (*virtually certain*).** The pattern of change across the components is physically consistent with the accumulation of energy in the Earth system driven by rising greenhouse-gas concentrations, and several large-scale components are approaching thresholds beyond which change may become self-sustaining (*medium confidence*) {2.1, 2.9}.

Atmospheric concentrations of carbon dioxide, methane, and nitrous oxide have continued to rise, and the growth of the airborne carbon dioxide burden tracks the sum of fossil and land-use emissions closely enough that the anthropogenic origin of the increase is beyond scientific dispute (*virtually certain*) {2.2.1}. The continuously updated global carbon budget shows fossil emissions and the atmospheric growth rate remaining near record levels through the most recent budget years {2.13.1}. Surface warming has been geographically pervasive but strongly amplified at high northern latitudes, where Arctic warming has reached nearly four times the global mean since 1979 — a value now well quantified from multiple datasets and one that exceeds what much of the model ensemble reproduces, indicating a possible systematic underestimate of the polar response (*high confidence*) {2.2.2, 2.4.1}. Mountain regions show elevation-dependent warming and rapid ice loss, integrating change across atmosphere and cryosphere; High Mountain Asian glaciers have thinned and retreated across the multi-decadal satellite record (*high confidence*) {2.4.2}.

The ocean has warmed and accumulated the great majority of the planetary energy imbalance, and marine heatwaves have become more frequent, more intense, and longer-lasting, with biological consequences extending from individual organisms to whole ecosystems and biogeochemical cycles (*high confidence*) {2.3.1, 2.3.2}. Sea level rise integrates thermal expansion, glacier and ice-sheet loss, and ocean mass change, and functions as an index of whole-system change {2.16.1}. The continental water cycle is intensifying and becoming more variable, with more severe droughts and floods and a widespread decline in terrestrial water storage; satellite observations reveal a widespread decline in global lake water storage, the twenty-first-century southwestern North American drought has intensified into a megadrought unprecedented in centuries, and an anthropogenic fingerprint is now detectable in global drought frequency, duration, and intensity (*high confidence*) {2.3.4, 2.11.2, 2.11.3}.

The biosphere is responding to, and in places losing resilience under, the combined pressures of warming, drought, and disturbance (*medium-to-high confidence*). Emerging signals of declining forest resilience are visible in global vegetation observations, hotter droughts leave a distinctive fingerprint on tree mortality worldwide, and centuries of wetland loss have removed a large fraction of a globally important carbon and water reservoir {2.5.1, 2.5.2, 2.5.4}. The terrestrial carbon sink persists but shows signs of destabilization, with implications for the atmospheric carbon dioxide trajectory {2.5.3, 2.12.4}.

Detection and attribution have moved from the global mean to the scale of specific events. Human influence on the causes of individual heatwaves, droughts, and wildfire seasons is increasingly demonstrable through formal attribution, which has matured into a systematic capability quantifying how anthropogenic warming has changed the probability and severity of extremes (*high confidence*) {2.7.1, 2.7.3}. Attribution now extends to impacts as well as to hazards {2.7.4}. Paleoclimatic context confirms that the recent rate of change stands out against the deep past and that slow-responding components carry a committed change already set in motion {2.8.1, 2.8.2}.

At the system scale, interacting tipping elements can increase the risk of climate domino effects under sustained warming, and the planetary-boundaries assessment finds six of nine boundaries transgressed, including climate change (*medium confidence*) {2.9.1, 2.9.2}. The observational and modelling basis of these conclusions — reanalyses, satellite retrieval of atmospheric composition, the CMIP multi-model ensemble, kilometre-scale simulation, hybrid machine-learning modelling, and the extension of observation through citizen science — is assessed explicitly so that the confidence attached to each finding is traceable to the evidence supporting it {2.10.1, 2.10.3, 2.10.4, 2.10.5}.

The coherence of change across components is itself a principal finding: the atmospheric, oceanic, cryospheric, and biospheric signals are not independent trends pointing in the same direction by coincidence but the physically linked expression of a single energy imbalance, and this coherence is what raises the assessment from a catalogue of changes to a system-scale conclusion (*high confidence*) {2.15.1}. Marine ecosystems and ocean biogeochemistry, Arctic processes documented through intensive year-round observation, and the human dimension of large-scale change — through agriculture, water security, and the inequitable distribution of exposure — are integrated into this synthesis rather than treated as separate domains {2.15.2, 2.15.3, 2.15.4}. The continental water cycle couples floods and droughts as two expressions of the same intensifying hydrological system, and terrestrial water storage, lakes, and soils are assessed as reservoirs whose decline is both a consequence and a driver of further change {2.11.1, 2.11.3, 2.12.1}.

## TS.3 Regional Climate and Extreme Events

> **Regional warming has departed decisively from the global mean, with the Arctic and the world's high-mountain regions emerging as the fastest-warming large domains of the planet, and the intensity and frequency of hot extremes, heavy sub-daily precipitation, and rapidly developing droughts have increased across most inhabited regions (*high confidence*).** These regional signals reflect distinct physical mechanisms rather than a scaled-down global average, and their human consequences are co-produced by physical hazard and by exposure, vulnerability, and adaptation, so that assessment of the two is inseparable (*high confidence*) {3.1, 3.8.2}.

Recent Arctic warming stands at close to four times the global rate since the late 1970s, while elevation-dependent warming is now documented across mountain systems on every inhabited continent; these signals arise from sea-ice and snow-albedo feedbacks, changes in the vertical structure of the atmosphere, and land-surface modification (*high confidence*) {3.1.1, 3.1.2}. Urbanisation superimposes a further, locally intense warming signal that is distributed inequitably across and within cities (*high confidence*) {3.1.3}.

Hot extremes have become more frequent, more intense, and longer-lasting across most land regions, and their health consequences are amplified in cities, where urban populations experience heat systematically elevated relative to surrounding rural areas (*high confidence*) {3.2.1, 3.7.1}. Extreme heat is assessed as the deadliest and most attributable of the regional extremes, its human toll governed by humidity, urban form, and social vulnerability {3.7.1}. Short-duration rainfall extremes are intensifying at rates consistent with, and in places exceeding, thermodynamic expectations, raising flood hazard; exposure to flooding is concentrated among the poor, so that identical physical hazards translate into sharply divergent human outcomes (*high confidence*) {3.2.2}. Drought is evolving in character as well as frequency, with a documented global shift toward rapidly developing flash droughts and toward multi-year megadroughts in several regions; the early-twenty-first-century southwestern North American drought intensified into one of the most severe multi-decadal dry episodes in the long paleoclimate record, and groundwater, the buffer on which much of humanity relies, is under simultaneous pressure from depletion and a changing climate (*high confidence*) {3.2.3, 3.7.2}.

Tropical cyclone hazard is being reshaped by warming through intensity, rainfall, and coastal impact, even where basin-wide frequency changes remain uncertain (*medium confidence*) {3.2.4, 3.7.4}. Compound and cascading events — where hazards coincide, cluster, or trigger one another — are an increasingly important frame for regional risk that single-hazard methods systematically understate (*medium confidence*); compound climate-fire-smoke sequences illustrate the point, with wildfire smoke shown to damage respiratory health more than equivalent particulate matter from other sources {3.2.5}.

Event attribution has matured into a quantitative discipline capable of estimating the human influence on many individual extremes, and this capability now underwrites both scientific assessment and the economic valuation of climate damages, informing estimates of the social cost of carbon dioxide that comprehensive assessments place substantially above many regulatory values in current use (*high confidence*) {3.3.1, 3.3.3}. The credibility of attribution depends on the fidelity of climate models and on the coordinated experimental frameworks that structure their use {3.3.2}.

Regional monsoon systems, which govern the water security of a majority of the world's population, are responding to competing greenhouse-gas and aerosol forcings in ways that vary by basin and remain a leading source of regional projection uncertainty (*medium confidence*) {3.4.1, 3.7.3}. Because monsoon variability propagates directly into agriculture, the emergence of climate impacts on food production in the latest generation of crop and climate models is of first-order regional importance {3.4.3}. Adaptation is occurring but uneven in coverage, and in some documented cases has increased rather than reduced vulnerability, underscoring that regional risk is produced jointly by physical hazard, exposure, and the social processes governing vulnerability and response (*high confidence*) {3.4.4}.

The regional syntheses identify a small number of coherent regional stories: the Arctic reorganising across its physical, ecological, and human systems at an unmatched pace; the world's mountains transmitting high-elevation warming into lowland water insecurity for billions; dryland and Mediterranean-type regions experiencing a self-reinforcing convergence of heat, drought, and fire; and densely populated coastal and low-lying regions facing compounding flood hazard from rainfall, rivers, surge, and rising seas (*high confidence*) {3.5.1, 3.5.2, 3.5.3, 3.5.4}. The largest regional uncertainties lie in the dynamical and convective processes that govern precipitation, monsoons, and sub-daily extremes, and the foundation on which all regional assessment rests is observational records of adequate length, quality, and homogeneity (*high confidence*) {3.6.1, 3.6.2}. The frontier of regional assessment is the integration of physical change with exposure, vulnerability, and the assessment of catastrophic tail risk: regional hazard is transmitted into food, water, health, energy, and disease through tightly coupled pathways, none of which can be assessed in isolation, so that the same physical signal produces sharply divergent human outcomes depending on the social systems it passes through (*high confidence*) {3.5.5, 3.6.3}. Water is assessed as the medium through which most regional climate change is ultimately felt, from drought and flood to the seasonal reliability of supply, which is why the regional water cycle and the monsoon that integrates it for a majority of humanity are treated as leading assessment priorities {3.7.2, 3.7.3}.

## TS.4 Process Understanding: Forcings, Feedbacks, and Cycles

> **The physical understanding of how an energy imbalance is imposed on the climate system, amplified or damped by feedbacks, and transmitted through the coupled carbon, biogeochemical, and hydrological cycles has consolidated since the previous assessment cycle, and the direction of every major process is now attributed to human activity with progressively narrower uncertainty ranges (*high confidence*).** The dominant control on the present energy imbalance is the accumulation of carbon dioxide and other long-lived greenhouse gases, whose growth rate and airborne fraction are quantified annually to within a few percent by the global carbon budget synthesis {4.1, 4.4.1}.

Effective radiative forcing, which folds rapid atmospheric and land-surface adjustments into the instantaneous perturbation, is assessed as the most policy-relevant forcing metric because it improves the correspondence between a given forcing agent and the surface temperature response it ultimately produces (*high confidence*) {4.1.1}. A forcing expressed in watts per square metre can be compared across agents as dissimilar as carbon dioxide, sulfate aerosol, and a change in surface reflectivity, but the simplest fixed-everything definition is refined by effective radiative forcing to absorb the cloud, stratospheric, and vegetation adjustments that occur within hours to a season and without any change in global-mean surface temperature {4.1.1}. The well-mixed greenhouse gases dominate the positive forcing; ozone, water vapor, and shorter-lived agents contribute additional terms; and land-use and surface-albedo changes impose a biogeophysical forcing that is regionally concentrated {4.1.2, 4.1.3, 4.1.4}. The aerosol contribution remains the single largest source of uncertainty in the total anthropogenic forcing, with mineral dust and other natural aerosol populations now resolved at the level of individual emitting regions and shown to modulate both the shortwave and longwave components (*high confidence*) {4.1.5, 4.3.1, 4.3.2}.

Net climate feedbacks are positive, so the equilibrium warming for a doubling of carbon dioxide is substantially larger than the no-feedback response (*high confidence*) {4.2.1}. The no-feedback Planck response is close to one degree C and is not in dispute; everything above it is the net product of feedbacks, and everything uncertain about the sensitivity is uncertainty in feedbacks {4.2.1}. The assessed sensitivity range has narrowed relative to a decade earlier because the constituent feedbacks have been separately constrained and recombined with the historical-record and paleoclimate lines of evidence {4.2.6}. The water-vapor and lapse-rate feedbacks are strongly positive and well understood; the surface-albedo feedback is expressed most strongly in the Arctic, where near-surface warming has proceeded at nearly four times the global rate over the satellite era; and the cloud feedback is assessed as net positive and remains both the principal amplifier and the principal residual uncertainty (*high confidence*) {4.2.2, 4.2.3, 4.2.4}. The cloud feedback is now decomposed into a robust positive high-cloud-altitude component, a storm-track shift component, and the response of subtropical low marine stratocumulus that has been the dominant source of overall uncertainty; process understanding of this last component has advanced most, so the residual cloud uncertainty, while still dominant, has narrowed {4.2.4}. A pattern effect — the dependence of the global feedback on the spatial pattern of warming — is assessed as a key reconciler of the historical and process-based sensitivity estimates {4.2.1}.

The carbon-cycle feedback on climate is positive and of increasing magnitude (*medium to high confidence*): the land and ocean sinks together remove roughly half of anthropogenic emissions, but the efficiency of both is sensitive to warming, drought, and disturbance {4.2.5, 4.4.1}. The persistence of the global forest carbon sink and the emerging signals that its resilience is declining under compound stress are now documented at global scale {4.4.2}. Terrestrial biogeochemical cycling is governed at the process level by the interaction of organic matter with soil minerals and by the ecology of the soil microbiome, both of which set the residence time of carbon; the modern understanding has shifted decisively toward these mechanistic mineral-association controls and away from the older view based on intrinsic molecular recalcitrance, and disturbances that break organo-mineral associations can convert a long-lived stock into a rapidly respired flux (*high confidence*) {4.4.3}. The ocean carbon sink, inland-water lateral fluxes, and the coupling across the nitrogen, phosphorus, and carbon cycles complete the biogeochemical assessment {4.4.4, 4.4.5, 4.4.6}.

The water cycle is intensifying, with rising atmospheric moisture demand, more rapidly emerging droughts, and warming inland waters, each carrying an anthropogenic fingerprint (*high confidence*); the abrupt onset of flash droughts and the propagation of heat extremes into lakes and rivers are robust across observational and modelling lines of evidence {4.5.1, 4.5.2, 4.5.4}. Earth-system models provide the integrating framework that bridges process understanding and projection, reproducing the observed forcing history while spanning a credible range of feedback strengths, and data-driven methods augment rather than replace them (*high confidence*) {4.6.1, 4.6.2}. Detection and attribution now operate at the process level and in the regional heterogeneity of the response, and the chapter closes on the concepts that carry into projection: cumulative emissions, committed warming, the near-linear response, and the asymmetry between reversible and irreversible components {4.7.1, 4.8.1, 4.8.2}.

## TS.5 Scenarios and Near-Term Projections

> **The scenario framework inherited from the previous assessment remains fit for purpose in the near term, but the plausibility of its highest-emission storylines has continued to erode as observed and pledged trajectories diverge from unmitigated-growth assumptions; near-term global surface temperature is projected to continue rising across all plausible pathways, and the difference between pathways is small before about 2040 because near-term warming is largely committed by past and present emissions (*very likely*).** In this near-term regime, mitigation choices are being made but their temperature signal has not yet emerged from the noise of internal variability (*high confidence*) {5.1, 5.3}.

The Shared Socioeconomic Pathway architecture coupled to the Scenario Model Intercomparison Project experimental design continues to anchor multi-model projections, and its near-term temperature spread is dominated less by scenario choice than by model response and internal variability (*high confidence*) {5.1, 5.2}. Divergence between low- and high-emission pathways becomes assessable only toward mid-century {5.3}. Global fossil carbon dioxide emissions have remained near record levels through the early 2020s, the annual budget accounting shows emissions plateauing rather than falling at the rate implied by the lowest scenarios, and the remaining budget consistent with limiting warming to well below 2 degrees C continues to shrink, so the gap between current-policy trajectories and deep-mitigation pathways is widening in absolute terms (*high confidence*) {5.2}.

Sectoral decarbonization pathways for the hardest-to-abate sectors — cement, iron and steel, and coal power — show that near-term emissions are strongly path-dependent on capital-stock turnover, and committed emissions from existing and under-construction infrastructure constrain the feasible speed of the near-term transition more than long-run technology availability does (*high confidence*) {5.2}. Internal variability is assessed as the largest source of uncertainty in near-term regional projections and can locally mask or amplify the forced trend for a decade or more; in rapidly warming regions such as the Arctic the forced signal is large enough to dominate even at near-term horizons, whereas in mid-latitude land regions the near-term trend can be obscured by variability (*high confidence*) {5.4}.

Initialized decadal prediction adds skill over uninitialized projections for some large-scale quantities in the first few years, but skill for regional precipitation and extremes remains limited, so the present value of the near-term prediction enterprise lies principally in ocean-heat and large-scale circulation indices rather than in actionable regional extremes forecasts (*medium confidence*) {5.4}. The probability of exceeding key near-term thresholds — including individual years above 1.5 degrees C relative to pre-industrial — has become substantial, though a single threshold-exceeding year does not constitute crossing the long-term warming level; near-term extremes, particularly heatwaves and compound drought-heat events, are projected to intensify faster than the mean, so risk-relevant change outpaces the change in global mean temperature (*high confidence*) {5.3, 5.5}. Low-probability, high-impact outcomes — including the possibility of engaging tipping elements and cascading risks — are not excluded on near-term horizons and warrant explicit assessment alongside central projections, with storyline and risk-framing approaches complementing probabilistic projection by making the tails and their societal entanglements assessable (*medium confidence*) {5.5}.

The scenario framework itself has evolved since the previous assessment: while the Shared Socioeconomic Pathway architecture is retained for continuity and comparability, the assessment records the erosion of plausibility at the high-emission end and the corresponding importance of the low- and intermediate-emission storylines for near-term decision-relevance {5.1}. Because near-term forcing is dominated by the slow-changing well-mixed greenhouse gases already in the atmosphere and by short-lived agents whose trajectories are set by air-quality as well as climate policy, the near-term is the regime in which the aerosol-unmasking effect — the warming revealed as cooling aerosols are removed alongside the fossil emissions that produce them — is most consequential and most uncertain, and it is assessed as a cross-cutting consideration that couples the emissions pathway to the realized near-term warming rate {5.2, 5.5}. The central near-term message is therefore asymmetric in time: the temperature difference between pathways is small before about 2040, but the emissions decisions taken in that window largely determine whether the lowest stabilization levels remain physically attainable at all {5.3, 5.5}.

## TS.6 Long-Term Global Projections

> **Global surface temperature will continue to rise until at least mid-century under all assessed emission scenarios, and the level of warming reached by 2100 is determined overwhelmingly by cumulative future carbon dioxide emissions rather than by the pathway taken to any given level (*virtually certain*).** The multi-model ensembles place century-end warming across the plausible low- to high-emission range between roughly 1.4 degrees C and more than 4 degrees C above the late-nineteenth-century baseline, with the spread dominated by scenario choice at the high end and by climate-response uncertainty at the low end (*high confidence*) {6.1, 6.2.1}.

Warming-level framing — organizing projected impacts by global mean temperature increase rather than by scenario or date — is assessed as the most robust way to communicate long-term change, because many hazard metrics scale more tightly with warming level than with the emission trajectory that produced it (*high confidence*) {6.1.2}. Every increment of warming matters: glacier loss, heat-extreme frequency, drought expansion, and the probability of crossing critical thresholds all increase monotonically and, for several systems, super-linearly with each additional tenth of a degree {6.1.2}. The framing has a stated limitation: quantities governed by the rate of change or by integrated exposure — ocean heat content, thermosteric sea level, and ice-sheet response — are not invariant to pathway even at a fixed warming level, so two worlds at the same warming level but reached by different paths can have materially different states in their slow-responding components (*high confidence*) {6.1.2}. The polar and high-mountain regions will continue to warm substantially faster than the global mean, sustaining Arctic amplification at roughly three to four times the global rate and elevation-dependent warming across the world's mountains (*high confidence*) {6.1.3}.

Global precipitation will increase in the ensemble mean, organized into a robust wet-get-wetter, dry-get-drier, and wet-season-wetter pattern superimposed on large regional uncertainty, with the frequency and intensity of both heavy precipitation and drought extremes rising together as a direct consequence of the same warming (*high confidence*); flash-drought onset accelerates under warming across most land regions, and anthropogenic influence on drought frequency, duration, and intensity is already detectable {6.2.3, 6.2.4}. Large-scale overturning circulations, atmospheric and oceanic, are assessed to weaken over the century, with higher confidence in the sign than in the magnitude {6.2.5}.

Global mean sea level will rise through 2100 and for centuries to millennia beyond, and the deep-time commitment is effectively irreversible on human timescales even if temperatures are stabilized (*high confidence*) {6.3.1, 6.3.3}. The assessed likely range for century-end rise spans from roughly half a metre under stringent mitigation to close to a metre or more under high emissions, with the central estimate dominated by the well-constrained thermal-expansion and glacier contributions {6.3.1}. The dominant uncertainty resides in the marine-based sectors of the Antarctic Ice Sheet, whose potential instability governs a low-likelihood, high-impact tail that cannot be excluded and widens sharply beyond 2100 (*medium confidence* in the magnitude; *high confidence* that the risk rises with warming) {6.3.2}. Beyond 2100 the multi-century commitment scales with peak warming to reach magnitudes measured in metres, with the marine-based Antarctic sectors capable of contributing multiple metres over subsequent centuries {6.3.3}.

The ocean will continue to warm, acidify, lose oxygen, and experience more frequent, intense, and prolonged marine heatwaves throughout the century (*virtually certain*), with biological consequences that scale steeply with warming level {6.3.4, 6.3.5}. The Atlantic meridional overturning circulation is assessed to weaken over the century, with consequences for regional sea level, heat transport, and marine ecosystems; an abrupt collapse before 2100 is not the assessed central outcome but cannot be excluded and constitutes a further high-impact tail (*medium confidence* in the weakening; *low confidence* in the likelihood of collapse) {6.3.6}. The world's mountain glaciers will lose a large fraction of their mass under every scenario as a smooth, monotonic function of peak temperature, and the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets will continue to lose mass through the century and beyond, Greenland predominantly through surface melt committing long-term decline above a temperature threshold and Antarctica predominantly through dynamic discharge capable of more abrupt behavior (*high confidence* in continued loss; *medium confidence* in the magnitude and timing of dynamic instabilities) {6.4.1, 6.4.5}. Permafrost thaw adds a frozen-carbon feedback assessed as gradual and pervasive rather than a single abrupt transition {6.4.6}.

The land carbon sink is projected to persist but with declining stability, and forests, wildfire, and regional forest systems such as the Amazon carry the risk of self-reinforcing dieback {6.5.1, 6.5.4}. Several large-scale components carry a non-negligible probability of crossing self-perpetuating thresholds under sustained high warming, including the Amazon forest and marine-based Antarctic ice (*medium confidence* in the thresholds; *high confidence* that the risk rises with warming), and low-likelihood, high-impact and catastrophic outcomes are assessed explicitly rather than omitted (*high confidence* in the value of assessing them) {6.6.1, 6.6.2}. Even under stabilized temperature, committed change in sea level, ice mass, and the deep ocean continues for centuries to millennia, so that peak warming rather than any subsequent stabilized level governs much of the long-term outcome {6.6.4}. Model evaluation and observational and emergent constraints tighten the projected ranges relative to the raw ensemble spread, and machine-learning emulators have matured to complement, though not replace, process-based general circulation models for specific tasks (*high confidence*) {6.7.1, 6.7.2, 6.7.3}. Compound and cascading hazards — heat with drought and fire, extreme precipitation with high sea level — rise in probability faster with warming than any single component (*high confidence*) {6.8.1, 6.8.2}.

## TS.7 Regional Projections and Linking Global to Regional

> **Regional climate change is now detectable across every inhabited continent, and the pattern of regional response is more robustly established than at any previous assessment (*high confidence*).** The central methodological challenge is linking global-mean change to actionable regional information, and the tools for that linkage — dynamical downscaling, statistical downscaling, machine-learning emulation, and structured distillation — have matured substantially, with distillation now recognised as an essential step between raw model output and decision-relevant knowledge (*high confidence*) {7.1, 7.4.1}.

The dominant large-scale features of the warming pattern — amplified warming over land relative to ocean, amplified warming at high northern latitudes, and elevation-dependent warming in mountain systems — are consistently simulated across model generations and corroborated by observations {7.1, 7.5.1}. The Arctic has warmed nearly four times faster than the global mean since 1979, exceeding the factor-of-two heuristic long in use, and this amplification is a fundamental organizing feature of the Northern Hemisphere regional response (*virtually certain*) {7.5.1}. Dynamical downscaling with convection-permitting regional models has emerged as the highest-fidelity approach for representing regional extremes, while machine-learning emulators offer a computationally tractable route to large ensembles; the Shared Socioeconomic Pathway and forcing combinations coordinated through the scenario intercomparison provide the common boundary conditions that make cross-method comparison meaningful (*high confidence*) {7.2.1, 7.2.3}.

Regional projections of the water cycle show intensification of both wet and dry extremes, with flash-drought onset accelerating and drought frequency, duration, and intensity showing detectable anthropogenic signals; flood and drought prediction remain among the hardest regional problems because they integrate across scales and depend sensitively on land-surface and human-management processes (*high confidence*) {7.4.2, 7.8.1}. Regional heat extremes are intensifying faster than mean warming in several mid-latitude regions, driven in part by changes in atmospheric circulation imperfectly captured by coarse-resolution models: accelerated western European heatwave trends have been linked to more-persistent double-jet configurations over Eurasia, the 2021 Pacific Northwest event exceeded prior statistical expectations by a margin that stressed conventional extreme-value frameworks, and the emerging southwestern North American megadrought represents a regional hydroclimate state with few analogues in the instrumental or paleoclimate record (*high confidence*) {7.3.3, 7.5.2, 7.5.3}.

Storylines and physically self-consistent narrative approaches have become a standard complement to probabilistic projection for communicating deeply uncertain, high-impact regional outcomes, because complex, compounding, and cascading risks are not well served by marginal probability distributions alone; interacting tipping elements can raise the risk of cascading regional transitions beyond what any single element would imply, and low-likelihood high-impact outcomes warrant explicit treatment rather than exclusion (*high confidence*) {7.6.1, 7.6.2, 7.6.3}. Distillation reconciles multiple, sometimes conflicting, lines of evidence into fit-for-purpose products, on the principle that no single model, method, or ensemble is authoritative for a given region {7.4.2, 7.4.3}.

The regional syntheses span the Arctic and high northern latitudes, North America, Europe and the Mediterranean, Asia, Africa, Central and South America, and the Australasian, small-island, and coastal domain, each assessed for its characteristic hazards and the confidence attached to them {7.5.1, 7.5.4, 7.5.5, 7.5.7}. Regional impact sectors — water, food and agriculture, health, and coasts — transmit physical change into human outcomes through tightly coupled pathways, and regional detection, event attribution, and monitoring close the information chain from global forcing to local early warning {7.8.1, 7.8.2, 7.8.3, 7.9.1, 7.9.3}. Machine learning is assessed as both an accelerant and a caution, and persistent limitations — in convective processes, in the representation of compound events, and in observational coverage over parts of the Global South — define the regional research priorities (*high confidence*) {7.7.2, 7.7.3}.

## TS.8 Tipping Points and Abrupt Change

> **The climate system contains a set of large-scale components — tipping elements — that can undergo self-sustaining, and in some cases effectively irreversible, transitions once forcing crosses a critical threshold, and several of these elements show observational signals consistent with declining stability as global warming proceeds (*high confidence*).** The scientific basis for treating abrupt and irreversible change as a distinct assessment category rests on the recognition that the responses of ice sheets, the ocean overturning circulation, permafrost, tropical and boreal forests, and warm-water coral reefs to sustained forcing are not always smooth, reversible, or proportionate to the forcing applied (*high confidence*) {8.1, 8.2}.

The chapter distinguishes a tipping point from ordinary climate change by the presence of self-amplifying feedback strong enough to sustain a transition once initiated, and it frames the assessment through hazard, exposure, vulnerability, and cascading pathways within a planetary-boundaries context {8.1.1, 8.1.2, 8.1.4}. Deep uncertainty is treated explicitly rather than collapsed into a single central estimate {8.1.3}.

Warm-water coral reef systems are assessed as the tipping element most imminently at risk, with the biological impacts of marine heatwaves already driving mass mortality at present levels of warming; marine heatwaves have intensified in frequency, duration, and severity, and their ecological consequences — bleaching, disease, and range shifts — are documented across ocean basins (*high confidence*) {8.2.5}. The Amazon forest system shows a pronounced loss of resilience since the early 2000s, with a rising fraction of the biome exhibiting the statistical signatures of critical slowing down; a combination of warming, drought, deforestation, and fire could drive large areas past a threshold into a degraded, lower-biomass state (*medium confidence*) {8.2.4}. Permafrost constitutes a large and vulnerable carbon reservoir whose thaw releases carbon dioxide and methane, producing a positive feedback expected to be gradual and pervasive rather than a single global-scale abrupt transition, though abrupt thaw processes can dominate locally; its magnitude remains a significant source of uncertainty in the assessment of committed warming (*high confidence*) {8.2.3}. The Atlantic meridional overturning circulation and the polar ice sheets carry the committed sea-level and circulation risks assessed in detail alongside the global projections (*medium confidence* in threshold proximity) {8.2.1, 8.2.2}. Terrestrial ecosystems more broadly show emerging, spatially coherent signals of declining resilience, with hotter droughts producing tree mortality across a wide range of forest biomes, carrying implications for the atmospheric carbon dioxide trajectory and for the stability of forest-related tipping elements (*high confidence*) {8.2.6}.

Early-warning indicators grounded in the theory of critical slowing down — rising temporal autocorrelation and variance in system state variables — provide a physically motivated but not infallible basis for detecting approach to a threshold; their interpretation is complicated by observational limitations, confounding trends, and the possibility of tipping without prior warning (*medium confidence*), and where long, high-quality records exist they have been detected in the Amazon and in forest-resilience metrics {8.3.1, 8.3.2, 8.3.3}. Tipping elements do not operate in isolation: interactions among them, and between climatic and non-climatic stresses, can produce cascades and systemic risk that amplify impacts beyond the sum of individual components and couple the climate crisis to biodiversity loss and to broader societal shocks (*medium confidence*) {8.4.1, 8.4.2}. The assessment of interacting risks, cascades, and polycrisis dynamics is an area of rapid development that remains subject to substantial uncertainty {8.4.3}.

The paleoclimate record documents repeated episodes of abrupt regional and hemispheric change and demonstrates that Earth's climate has occupied radically different states, providing evidence that the system is capable of nonlinear reorganization on timescales far shorter than the forcing that triggers it (*high confidence*); it constrains the range of accessible states while leaving the precise thresholds under contemporary forcing uncertain {8.5.1, 8.5.2, 8.5.3}. Because low-likelihood, high-impact outcomes carry disproportionate societal consequences, a comprehensive risk assessment must weight them despite their deep uncertainty, and the possibility of catastrophic or, in the extreme, globally destabilizing outcomes is not excluded by current evidence and warrants dedicated study (*high confidence* in the value of assessing such outcomes) {8.6.1, 8.6.2, 8.6.5}. The chapter is careful to distinguish confidence from reassurance: the absence of a demonstrated imminent threshold for a given element is not evidence that the element is safe, and sustained observation together with emerging analytical tools remains the principal defense against surprise {8.9.1, 8.9.3, 8.7.1}.

## TS.9 Stabilization, Reversibility, and Climate Intervention

> **Stabilizing global surface temperature at any level requires global net anthropogenic carbon dioxide emissions to reach and be sustained at zero, and every additional increment of cumulative carbon dioxide emitted moves the achievable stabilization level upward in near-linear proportion (*virtually certain*).** The remaining budget consistent with limiting warming to 1.5 degrees C has continued to contract as emissions have persisted near record levels, and on present trajectories the budget for a better-than-even chance of holding warming to 1.5 degrees C will be exhausted within the current decade (*high confidence*) {9.1, 9.2}.

The near-linear relationship between cumulative carbon dioxide emissions and peak warming — expressed as the transient climate response to cumulative emissions — is the central organizing principle of stabilization science, and it arises because the diminishing radiative efficacy of additional carbon dioxide at higher concentrations is approximately compensated by the diminishing capacity of the ocean and land to take up additional carbon and heat as they warm (*high confidence*) {9.2, 9.3}. The remaining carbon budget for a given probability of limiting peak warming is being drawn down at close to its full historical rate because global emissions have not entered sustained decline, and the budgets for the lowest stabilization levels are now small enough that their exhaustion is measured in years rather than decades; they are further subject to downward revision from Earth-system feedbacks and from aerosol unmasking (*high confidence*) {9.2}.

When global net carbon dioxide emissions reach zero, the multi-decadal trend in global surface temperature is expected to level off rather than continue rising, because the cooling from declining atmospheric carbon dioxide as the ocean and land continue to take up carbon is approximately balanced by continued ocean heat uptake; this zero-emissions commitment close to zero means that halting warming is achievable within a human generation, but that already-emitted carbon dioxide commits the Earth system to sustained elevated temperatures for centuries (*medium confidence*) {9.3}. Stabilization is achievable along overshoot pathways that peak and decline temperature, but overshoot carries physical risks that are not symmetric on the way up and the way down: peak warming is set by peak cumulative emissions and surface air temperature is largely reversible for the atmosphere on decadal timescales, while committed changes in the deep ocean, ice sheets, permafrost, and biosphere carbon stores mean that returning global mean temperature to a target does not return the full Earth system to its prior state (*high confidence*) {9.4}.

Carbon dioxide removal is assessed as a necessary element of pathways that reach net-zero and net-negative emissions, and land-based and engineered approaches have distinct physical-science signatures, permanence characteristics, and Earth-system side effects that govern their net climatic effect; nature-based and engineered approaches such as direct air capture and geologic storage differ sharply in monitorability, permanence, and reversibility, and enhanced weathering occupies a distinct position on the permanence spectrum (*high confidence*) {9.5, 9.5.7}. Solar radiation modification could in principle rapidly reduce global mean surface temperature by increasing planetary albedo, but it does not address ocean acidification, would impose distinct regional climate and hydrological responses, and would create a termination-related risk of rapid warming if sustained deployment ceased; it is not a substitute for emissions reductions or carbon dioxide removal because it acts on a different lever of the energy balance than the atmospheric carbon dioxide burden that drives long-term change (*high confidence*) {9.6, 9.6.7}.

Earth-system responses to stabilization are heterogeneous in time: global mean surface temperature responds within decades, but sea level, ice sheets, deep-ocean heat content, and some ecosystem carbon stores continue to evolve for centuries to millennia after temperatures stabilize (*high confidence*) {9.7, 9.7.1, 9.7.2, 9.7.8}. Assessing stabilization therefore requires distinguishing the fast-responding components from the slow-responding components whose trajectories are effectively locked in over human planning horizons, and it requires monitoring and verification capable of confirming that net-zero has been reached and sustained {9.7.9}. Among the slow-responding components, sea level and the ice sheets continue to rise and lose mass for centuries after temperature stabilizes; the deep ocean continues to warm and to redistribute heat downward; ocean acidification and the associated biogeochemical shifts persist because they track the atmospheric carbon dioxide burden rather than temperature; and terrestrial carbon stores adjust to a new equilibrium over decades to centuries {9.7.1, 9.7.2, 9.7.3, 9.7.4}. The hydrological cycle and climate extremes under stabilization do not simply freeze at their peak-warming state but continue to adjust to the settling pattern of ocean warming, and large-scale ocean circulation may remain in a weakened state, or recover only slowly, depending on the depth and duration of any overshoot (*medium confidence*) {9.7.5, 9.7.7}. This heterogeneity of timescales is the physical reason that a return of global mean surface temperature to a target level does not constitute a return of the Earth system to its prior state, and it is the basis on which the assessment treats overshoot as carrying irreducible physical risk rather than as a neutral detour {9.4, 9.7.8}. The unifying conclusion is that stabilizing global surface temperature is physically achievable by bringing net carbon dioxide emissions to zero, that the stabilization level is set by cumulative emissions, that warming can be halted within a human generation but reversed only through sustained net-negative emissions, and that many components of the Earth system continue to change under a stabilized temperature (*high confidence*) {9.8}.

## TS.10 Climate Information for Decisions and Services

> **Climate information has moved from being a scientific by-product of Earth-system assessment to a distinct, decision-facing infrastructure that mediates between physical-science understanding and the choices made by governments, firms, communities, and individuals (*high confidence*).** The binding constraint on its value for decisions is no longer primarily the availability of data but the fidelity of the processes that translate physical-climate knowledge into forms that are salient, credible, and legitimate to specific users (*high confidence*) {10.1, 10.2}.

The co-production of climate information — sustained collaboration between information producers and decision-makers — measurably improves the uptake and usability of that information relative to one-directional loading-dock delivery, but is resource-intensive, unevenly distributed, and difficult to sustain (*medium confidence*); frameworks from implementation science provide transferable structure for understanding why some services are adopted and sustained while others are not {10.2.1, 10.2.2, 10.2.3}. Trust, credibility, and the social context of information are assessed as integral to its usefulness rather than as external factors {10.2.4}.

The observational, monitoring, and reanalysis foundation on which decision-relevant information rests has strengthened substantially: global reanalyses and satellite-derived land-surface products now provide internally consistent, decision-usable representations of the recent climate at fine spatial resolution, with continuous global land-cover and soil products at 30-metre and finer resolution and quantitatively reconciled global carbon accounting exemplifying the monitoring backbone now available, though systematic gaps remain over parts of the Global South and for many essential variables (*high confidence*) {10.3.1, 10.3.2, 10.3.5}. Water is assessed as an integrating variable for monitoring, and the geography of information poverty maps onto the geography of vulnerability {10.3.5, 10.3.6}.

Artificial intelligence and machine-learning methods have become established components of the climate-information value chain, delivering skilful short-range prediction, computationally efficient emulation, pattern extraction from large Earth-observation archives, and new modes of downscaling and bias correction (*high confidence*); deep generative models now produce precipitation nowcasts that expert forecasters judge more useful than established numerical alternatives {10.4.1, 10.4.2, 10.4.3}. These gains are accompanied by well-characterized risks — brittle out-of-distribution behaviour, opaque uncertainty, and the non-trivial environmental and equity costs of the computation itself (*high confidence*) {10.4.4, 10.4.5}. Robust, well-calibrated communication of uncertainty is a prerequisite for climate information to support good decisions, and where uncertainty is suppressed, misrepresented, or presented without decision-relevant framing, climate information can degrade rather than improve decisions (*medium confidence*); the calibrated-language framework used throughout this Report is one instrument among several, and decision-making under deep uncertainty requires methods that do not depend on a single agreed probability distribution {10.5.1, 10.5.2, 10.5.3}.

Regional climate risk assessment is the principal interface through which the physical-science findings of this Working Group enter the impact, adaptation, and vulnerability assessments of Working Group II, and the quality of that interface depends on shared definitions of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability and on the traceable propagation of uncertainty across the boundary (*high confidence*) {10.6.1, 10.6.2}. Sectoral evidence in water resources, agriculture and food security and its biological hazards, heat-related mortality, and land and soil degradation demonstrates both the maturity of hazard characterization and the persistent difficulty of converting hazard information into locally actionable risk information (*high confidence*) {10.6.3, 10.6.4}. Equity, capacity, and the inclusion of Indigenous knowledge and local communities are central determinants of whether information is trusted and used and of whether its benefits are distributed fairly, with evidence indicating more effective and more equitable outcomes where Indigenous peoples and local communities are recognised as knowledge-holders and decision-partners rather than passive recipients (*medium confidence*) {10.7.1, 10.7.2, 10.7.3}. Operationalizing climate information requires standards and interoperability, evaluation of information as a service, sustained institutions that solve the problem of continuity, and governance that safeguards accountability and integrity; early warning is assessed as the integrative climate service and the health framing as a route to actionable information {10.8.1, 10.8.2, 10.8.3, 10.9.4, 10.9.5}.

## Synthesis

Read across the ten chapters, the assessment converges on a small number of high-confidence conclusions that are robust to scenario choice and to the specific method of projection. Human influence is detectable across every component of the climate system, and the pattern of observed change is physically consistent with the accumulation of energy driven by rising greenhouse-gas concentrations (*virtually certain*) {2.1}. Warming continues until net carbon dioxide emissions reach zero; the level reached is set by cumulative emissions rather than by the pathway to it; and every increment of warming intensifies hazards across the atmosphere, ocean, cryosphere, and biosphere (*high confidence*) {6.1, 9.1}. Regional change is amplified and spatially structured, with the Arctic warming nearly four times the global rate and the world's mountains transmitting high-elevation warming into lowland water insecurity (*high confidence*) {3.1, 7.5}. A set of slow-responding and potentially threshold-crossing components — the marine-based Antarctic Ice Sheet, the Atlantic overturning circulation, the Amazon forest, permafrost carbon — commit the Earth system to changes that extend for centuries beyond 2100 and concentrate the deepest uncertainties of the assessment (*high confidence* that the risk rises with warming; *medium confidence* in the thresholds) {6.6, 8.2}.

The deliberate methodological posture of this edition is to assess low-likelihood, high-impact outcomes explicitly rather than to omit them because they lie in the tail of the distribution, and to hold every reported mechanism and comparison to a falsification discipline, a ground-truth integrity audit, and honest reporting of variance {1.4, 5.5, 6.6.2, 8.6}. Under this posture the single most consequential conclusion for the human timescale is that the emissions of the coming decades set the trajectory of the Earth system for centuries, and that limiting cumulative emissions and peak warming is the most effective lever for limiting the full range of assessed change (*high confidence*) {6.6, 9.8}.
