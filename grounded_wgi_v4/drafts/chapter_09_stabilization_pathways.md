# Chapter 9: Stabilization, Reversibility, and Climate Intervention

## Executive Summary

**Stabilizing global surface temperature at any level requires global net anthropogenic CO2 emissions to reach and be sustained at zero, and every additional increment of cumulative CO2 emitted moves the achievable stabilization level upward in near-linear proportion (*virtually certain*).** The near-linear relationship between cumulative CO2 emissions and peak warming remains the central organizing principle of stabilization science, and it is on this relationship that the concept of a remaining carbon budget for a given temperature limit rests.

**The remaining carbon budget consistent with limiting warming to 1.5 degrees C has continued to contract as emissions have persisted near record levels, and on present trajectories the budget for a better-than-even chance of holding warming to 1.5 degrees C will be exhausted within the current decade (*high confidence*).** Continued growth in the global carbon budget's emission terms, documented in successive annual assessments (Friedlingstein et al., 2022; Friedlingstein et al., 2023; Friedlingstein et al., 2025), leaves diminishing room for delay if low stabilization levels are to remain physically attainable.

**When global net CO2 emissions reach zero, the multi-decadal trend in global surface temperature is expected to level off rather than continue rising, because the warming effect of declining atmospheric CO2 removal by the ocean and land is approximately balanced by continued ocean heat uptake (*medium confidence*).** This "zero-emissions commitment" close to zero is a physical-science conclusion with profound implications: it means that halting warming is achievable within a human generation, but that already-emitted CO2 commits the Earth system to sustained elevated temperatures for centuries.

**Stabilization is achievable along pathways that peak and decline temperature — overshoot pathways — but overshoot carries physical risks that are not symmetric on the way up and the way down, and some Earth system responses are only partially reversible on policy-relevant timescales (*high confidence*).** Committed changes in the deep ocean, ice sheets, permafrost, and biosphere carbon stores mean that returning global mean temperature to a target level does not return the full Earth system to its prior state.

**Carbon dioxide removal is a necessary element of pathways that reach net-zero and net-negative CO2 emissions, and both land-based and engineered removal approaches have distinct physical-science signatures, permanence characteristics, and Earth system side effects that govern their net climatic effect (*high confidence*).** Nature-based approaches and engineered approaches such as direct air capture and geologic storage differ sharply in monitorability, permanence, and reversibility, as assessed for nature-based solutions by Seddon et al. (2021), for direct air capture by Erans et al. (2022), and for the wider capture-and-storage chain by Dziejarski et al. (2023).

**Solar radiation modification could, in principle, rapidly reduce global mean surface temperature by increasing planetary albedo, but it does not address ocean acidification, would impose distinct regional climate and hydrological responses, and would create a termination-related risk of rapid warming if sustained deployment ceased (*high confidence*).** Solar radiation modification is not a substitute for emissions reductions or carbon dioxide removal; its physical effects act on a different lever of the energy balance than the atmospheric CO2 burden that drives long-term change.

**Earth system responses to stabilization are heterogeneous in time: global mean surface temperature responds within decades, but sea level, ice sheets, deep-ocean heat content, and some ecosystem carbon stores continue to evolve for centuries to millennia after temperatures stabilize (*high confidence*).** Assessing stabilization therefore requires distinguishing the fast-responding components of the system from the slow-responding components whose trajectories are effectively locked in over human planning horizons.

## 9.1 Introduction: what stabilization means in the physical climate system

**Climate stabilization is a property of the whole Earth system, not of the atmosphere alone, and its assessment requires tracking the coupled response of the carbon cycle, the energy budget, and the slow cryospheric and oceanic components together (*high confidence*).** This chapter assesses the physical-science basis for stabilizing the climate: the relationships that connect cumulative emissions to warming, the behavior of the system once emissions cease, the reversibility of changes under declining forcing, and the physical characteristics of the two principal classes of deliberate climate intervention — carbon dioxide removal and solar radiation modification. It does not assess the economic feasibility, governance, or social dimensions of these options, which fall to Working Groups II and III; the focus here is strictly on what the physical climate system does in response to the forcing pathways that different stabilization strategies imply.

The framing of stabilization has shifted over successive assessment cycles from a concentration-based view — in which a target atmospheric CO2 concentration was the organizing quantity — to an emissions-based view centered on cumulative anthropogenic CO2. This shift reflects one of the most robust findings in climate science: that peak warming is controlled, to first order, by the total amount of CO2 that humanity emits, largely independent of the precise pathway by which that total is reached. The practical consequence is that stabilizing temperature is equivalent to bringing net CO2 emissions to zero, and the level at which temperature stabilizes is set by the cumulative total emitted up to that point.

This emissions-centric framing situates stabilization within the broader diagnosis of the Earth system's state. The planetary boundaries synthesis of Richardson et al. (2023) concludes that six of nine boundaries that define a safe operating space for humanity have now been transgressed, with the climate-change boundary among them; this places the stabilization question in the context of a system already displaced substantially from its Holocene reference state. The emissions driving that displacement are documented sector by sector: Lamb et al. (2021) assess trends and drivers of greenhouse gas emissions across sectors from 1990 to 2018, showing sustained growth across energy supply, industry, transport, and buildings, with the pace and distribution of that growth setting the baseline against which stabilization pathways must bend.

Stabilization at a chosen level is, in physical terms, the successful termination of the anthropogenic perturbation to the carbon cycle followed by the slow relaxation of the system toward a new equilibrium. Because that relaxation is incomplete on human timescales for several key components, the language of this chapter distinguishes carefully among three ideas that are often conflated: **halting** warming (achieved when net CO2 emissions reach zero and temperature levels off), **reversing** warming (achieved only through sustained net-negative CO2 emissions that draw down the atmospheric burden), and **reversibility** of impacts (the degree to which specific Earth system components return toward their prior state when forcing declines). These three are governed by different physics and operate on different timescales.

## 9.2 Remaining carbon budgets

**For a given probability of limiting peak warming to a specified level, there exists a finite remaining budget of cumulative net CO2 emissions, and this budget is being drawn down at close to its full historical rate because global CO2 emissions have not yet entered sustained decline (*high confidence*).** The remaining carbon budget is the total quantity of additional net anthropogenic CO2 that can be emitted from a specified start date while keeping peak warming below a chosen limit with a chosen probability. Its magnitude follows from the near-linear proportionality between cumulative CO2 emissions and global mean surface temperature, combined with estimates of the contribution of non-CO2 forcers, the historical warming already realized, and the Earth system feedbacks not fully captured in the core proportionality.

The annual Global Carbon Budget assessments provide the observational anchor for the emission side of this calculation. Friedlingstein et al. (2022), Friedlingstein et al. (2023), and Friedlingstein et al. (2025) document total anthropogenic CO2 emissions from fossil sources and land-use change, together with the partitioning of those emissions among the atmosphere, ocean, and land sinks. Across these successive assessments the central message is consistent: total CO2 emissions have remained near record levels, the atmospheric CO2 growth rate has continued, and the ocean and land sinks, while continuing to absorb a substantial fraction of emissions, have not accelerated enough to offset emission growth. Each year in which emissions remain near their peak subtracts a corresponding quantity from every remaining budget, moving the exhaustion date for the tightest budgets closer.

The distinction between the CO2 and non-CO2 contributions to warming is central to budget estimation. The remaining budget for a temperature limit depends not only on cumulative CO2 but on the co-evolving forcing from methane, nitrous oxide, aerosols, and other agents. Because aerosols exert a net cooling effect that is expected to decline as air-quality measures and fossil-fuel phase-out reduce emissions, the removal of this cooling can unmask additional CO2-driven warming, tightening the effective budget. The indicators synthesis of Forster et al. (2023) provides an annually updated assessment of the large-scale indicators of the state of the climate system and of human influence, including the components of effective radiative forcing and the estimate of human-induced warming to date; these indicators are the quantities from which the remaining budget for any given limit is computed, and their continued updating is what drives the year-on-year revision of budget estimates.

A central physical subtlety in budget science is the treatment of Earth system feedbacks that are not represented, or only partially represented, in the models used to derive the core cumulative-emissions-to-warming relationship. Permafrost carbon release, changes in wetland methane emissions, and shifts in the airborne fraction under high cumulative emissions all act to reduce the remaining budget relative to a calculation that ignores them. These feedbacks introduce an asymmetry: they are more likely to reduce than to increase the budget, and their magnitude grows with the level of warming, so budgets for higher limits carry proportionally larger downward feedback risks. The declining resilience of major carbon-storing biomes compounds this concern; Forzieri et al. (2022) identify emerging signals of declining forest resilience under climate change, a finding directly relevant to whether the land sink can be relied upon to hold its historical share of emissions over the coming decades, and Lapola et al. (2023) document the drivers and impacts of Amazon forest degradation, a process that erodes one of the largest terrestrial carbon stores and thereby the effective budget.

The observational context for budget exhaustion is stark. With cumulative emissions continuing to rise at close to their historical rate, the budget consistent with a better-than-even chance of holding peak warming to 1.5 degrees C is small relative to a single decade of emissions at present rates. This does not, in itself, mean that 1.5 degrees C is physically impossible as a peak; it means that holding to 1.5 degrees C without overshoot requires an immediate and steep decline in net CO2 emissions, and that any pathway which does not achieve such a decline will overshoot the level before, at best, returning to it through net-negative emissions. Riahi et al. (2021) assess the cost and attainability of meeting stringent climate targets without overshoot, and their analysis makes explicit the narrowing physical and technological space within which no-overshoot pathways remain feasible as emissions persist.

The relationship between fossil-fuel reserves and the remaining budget provides a further physical framing. Welsby et al. (2021) estimate the quantity of fossil fuels that must remain unextracted in a 1.5 degrees C world, concluding that a large majority of existing reserves cannot be combusted if the corresponding budget is to be respected. This translates the abstract budget into a constraint on the physical stock of carbon-bearing fuels, and it makes clear that the budget is not merely an accounting convention but a hard limit on how much of the geologic carbon reservoir can be transferred to the atmosphere.

Two additional considerations qualify the interpretation of any single budget number. First, the remaining budget is a distribution, not a point: it carries irreducible uncertainty from the strength of the cumulative-emissions-to-warming relationship, the non-CO2 forcing trajectory, the historical warming estimate, and the feedback terms. Reporting a budget therefore requires stating both the temperature limit and the probability with which it is to be respected, and moving from a 50 percent to a 67 percent probability of holding a limit substantially reduces the allowable emissions. Second, the budget is defined for peak warming, not for warming at a fixed date; a pathway may respect a budget for peak warming while temporarily exceeding the limit if it later draws the atmosphere down, or may hold below the limit at a given date while being committed to exceed it later. Clarity about which quantity — peak warming or warming at a date — a budget refers to is essential to avoid misinterpretation.

The policy-relevant synthesis is that remaining carbon budgets for the lowest stabilization levels are now small enough that their exhaustion is measured in years rather than decades at current emission rates, that the budgets are subject to downward revision from feedbacks and aerosol unmasking, and that the observational record shows no sustained decline in global CO2 emissions of the magnitude required to preserve the tightest budgets. The physical science does not prescribe a policy response, but it establishes unambiguously that the room for delay in reaching net-zero CO2 has largely been consumed for the most stringent stabilization levels.

## 9.3 The zero-emissions commitment and the near-linear response

**The multi-decadal trend in global surface temperature is expected to halt within about a decade of global net CO2 emissions reaching zero, because the continued decline in atmospheric CO2 concentration through ocean and land uptake offsets the warming that would otherwise result from continued ocean heat uptake (*medium confidence*).** This conclusion — that the zero-emissions commitment for CO2 is close to zero — is one of the most consequential results of stabilization science, because it means that the level at which temperature stabilizes is determined by cumulative emissions up to the point of net zero, and that no substantial additional warming is "in the pipeline" from past CO2 emissions once those emissions cease. It stands in contrast to an older intuition, derived from constant-concentration experiments, that large committed warming would continue to be realized for decades after emissions stopped; that intuition confused the constant-concentration commitment with the constant-emissions (zero further emissions) commitment, which are governed by different balances.

The physical basis for a near-zero commitment lies in the compensation between two opposing tendencies once emissions cease. First, in the absence of new emissions, the atmospheric CO2 concentration begins to fall as the ocean and land sinks continue to take up carbon; this declining radiative forcing acts to cool the surface. Second, the ocean continues to absorb heat as the deep ocean slowly equilibrates with the elevated surface temperature; this continued heat uptake, and the reduction in the rate at which the surface warms, means the surface does not immediately reflect the full equilibrium response to the peak concentration. To first order these two effects — declining forcing and continued ocean heat uptake — approximately cancel, leaving global mean surface temperature roughly flat for decades after net-zero CO2. The precise magnitude and even the sign of the residual depend on the relative rates of carbon drawdown and heat uptake, which is why the commitment is assessed with medium rather than high confidence and why models disagree on whether the residual trend is slightly positive or slightly negative.

The near-linear relationship between cumulative CO2 emissions and peak warming — often expressed as the transient climate response to cumulative emissions — is the quantitative expression of this behavior. It arises because the diminishing radiative efficacy of additional CO2 at higher concentrations is approximately compensated by the diminishing capacity of the ocean and land to take up additional carbon and heat as they warm. The result is a proportionality that holds robustly across models and across a wide range of cumulative emissions, and it is this proportionality that underwrites both the remaining carbon budget concept and the conclusion that halting emissions halts warming. The scenario-based projections assessed in the model intercomparison framework of Tebaldi et al. (2021), which synthesizes climate model projections from the Scenario Model Intercomparison Project of the sixth Coupled Model Intercomparison Project, exhibit this near-linear behavior across the scenario range and provide the multi-model basis for characterizing how surface temperature responds as cumulative emissions accumulate and then cease.

Several caveats qualify the near-zero commitment. The result applies to CO2 alone; the total zero-emissions commitment across all forcing agents differs, because non-CO2 forcers have different lifetimes and different committed responses. Short-lived agents such as methane decay rapidly, so ceasing methane emissions produces a relatively fast decline in methane forcing and hence some cooling; aerosols, also short-lived, decay quickly so that ceasing aerosol emissions removes their cooling almost immediately, producing warming. The net non-CO2 commitment therefore depends on the assumed trajectory of these agents, and a realistic assessment of the total commitment must combine the near-zero CO2 term with the non-CO2 terms. The distinct atmospheric behavior and mitigation role of the short-lived, predominantly biogenic methane emissions from agriculture is analyzed by Lynch et al. (2021), who show that agriculture's contribution to climate change and its role in mitigation is distinct from that of the predominantly fossil-CO2-emitting sectors precisely because of these lifetime differences; this distinction matters for the composition of the total commitment.

A further caveat concerns Earth system feedbacks not represented in the models used to derive the commitment. Permafrost carbon release, in particular, could continue to add CO2 and methane to the atmosphere after anthropogenic emissions cease, imparting a positive contribution to the commitment that is not captured in models lacking a fully interactive permafrost carbon module. Similarly, committed changes in the biosphere — including the declining forest resilience documented by Forzieri et al. (2022) and the degradation processes analyzed by Lapola et al. (2023) — could shift the land from a net sink toward a weaker sink or a source, which would slow the post-emissions decline in atmospheric CO2 and thereby push the commitment toward positive values. These feedbacks are the principal reason the zero-emissions commitment is assessed with medium confidence and is regarded as more likely to be revised upward than downward.

The distinction between halting and reversing warming follows directly. Reaching net-zero CO2 halts the warming trend but does not, by itself, cool the planet: temperature stabilizes near its peak. To reduce global mean temperature below its peak requires sustained net-negative CO2 emissions — removing more CO2 from the atmosphere than is emitted — which draws down the atmospheric burden and reduces radiative forcing. This is a physically distinct undertaking from reaching net zero, and it is the point at which carbon dioxide removal shifts from being a means of offsetting residual emissions to being a means of actively reversing warming. The rate at which temperature declines under net-negative emissions is not simply the mirror image of the rate at which it rose, because the slow components of the system — deep-ocean heat content, ice sheets — respond with hysteresis, a point developed in the following section.

## 9.4 Overshoot and reversibility

**Pathways that temporarily exceed a temperature limit before returning to it — overshoot pathways — do not return the Earth system to the state it would have occupied had the limit never been exceeded, because several key components respond with hysteresis and because some changes accumulate irreversibly during the overshoot period (*high confidence*).** Overshoot is a defining feature of many stabilization pathways for the lowest limits, given the small remaining budgets assessed in Section 9.2. Understanding overshoot requires separating three questions: whether the global mean temperature can be brought back down (it can, in principle, through sustained net-negative emissions); whether the return path retraces the outbound path (it does not, for the slow components); and whether specific impacts incurred during overshoot are reversed when temperature returns (some are, many are not, and some continue to worsen even as temperature declines).

The reversibility of global mean surface temperature itself is comparatively high on the timescale of the net-negative emissions that would drive it. Because surface temperature is coupled closely to atmospheric CO2 and to the fast components of the climate system, drawing down atmospheric CO2 through net-negative emissions produces a corresponding decline in global mean temperature within years to a few decades. In this narrow sense, global mean temperature is a relatively reversible quantity. But this reversibility is contingent on achieving and sustaining net-negative emissions at scale, and it does not extend to the slow components of the system, whose response to the overshoot is only partially undone by the subsequent cooling.

The deep ocean is the archetypal slow, partially irreversible component. Heat that enters the ocean during the overshoot penetrates to depth over decades to centuries, and the deep ocean continues to warm even as the surface cools under declining forcing, because the deep ocean is still equilibrating with the elevated temperatures of the overshoot period. As a result, ocean heat content and thermosteric sea level continue to rise for a period after surface temperature peaks and begins to decline, and they do not return to their pre-overshoot values on policy-relevant timescales. This hysteresis in ocean heat content is a central reason that sea-level rise is among the least reversible consequences of an overshoot: the thermal expansion component is committed by the heat already sequestered, and the ice-sheet component responds to the integrated temperature history rather than to the instantaneous temperature.

Marine ecosystems illustrate the asymmetry between temperature reversibility and impact reversibility. Even a temporary excursion to higher temperatures can produce marine heatwaves whose biological consequences persist long after the temperature anomaly has passed. Smith et al. (2022) assess the biological impacts of marine heatwaves and document mortality, range shifts, and community reorganization that do not simply reverse when temperatures return to prior levels; a coral reef killed during an overshoot does not regrow merely because the water cools again on a decadal timescale. The overshoot therefore incurs biological costs that are, in ecological terms, effectively permanent even where the physical temperature is reversible.

The terrestrial biosphere exhibits comparable irreversibilities. Forest dieback triggered during an overshoot releases stored carbon and reduces the resilience of the remaining forest, and the recovery of a degraded or burned forest to its prior carbon density takes decades to centuries, if it occurs at all. Forzieri et al. (2022) document emerging signals of declining forest resilience under climate change, which implies that forests subjected to the stress of an overshoot may be less able to recover afterward; Lapola et al. (2023) document the drivers and impacts of Amazon forest degradation, a process that, once advanced, is not readily reversed by a subsequent decline in temperature. Because these carbon stores feed back on atmospheric CO2, their partial irreversibility means that an overshoot can leave the carbon cycle in a state that makes the subsequent drawdown harder — the land sink is weaker on the return path than it was on the outbound path.

The broader biodiversity consequences of overshoot are similarly asymmetric. Cowie et al. (2022) assess the evidence bearing on whether the current episode of biodiversity loss constitutes a sixth mass extinction, and the extinctions that occur during a period of elevated temperature and associated stress are, by definition, irreversible: a species lost during an overshoot is not recovered when temperature returns. Harvey et al. (2022) issue a scientists' warning on climate change and insects, documenting the sensitivity of insect populations — foundational to terrestrial food webs and pollination — to climatic stress, and Lees et al. (2022) assess the state of the world's birds, a taxon whose population declines integrate multiple climatic and land-use pressures. These lines of evidence establish that the ecological ledger of an overshoot is not balanced by the subsequent cooling; the losses incurred at the peak are carried forward.

A crucial physical point is that the reversibility of the carbon cycle response to net-negative emissions is itself imperfect. When CO2 is removed from the atmosphere, the ocean and land, which had been net sinks, begin to release some of their stored carbon back to the atmosphere in response to the declining atmospheric concentration — an outgassing that partially offsets the removal. This means that removing a given quantity of CO2 from the atmosphere does not reduce the atmospheric burden by that full quantity, because the natural reservoirs give back part of what they had absorbed. The efficiency of net-negative emissions in reducing atmospheric CO2 is therefore lower than the efficiency with which emissions raised it, introducing an inherent asymmetry between emitting and removing. This asymmetry is a physical property of the coupled carbon cycle and is independent of the technology used to achieve the removal; it means that reversing a given amount of warming requires removing more CO2 than was emitted to cause it.

The magnitude and duration of an overshoot govern the severity of these committed and partially irreversible responses. A shallow, brief overshoot commits less deep-ocean heat, triggers fewer irreversible ecological transitions, and leaves the carbon cycle closer to its pre-overshoot state than a deep, prolonged overshoot. This establishes a physical rationale for minimizing both the peak level and the duration of any overshoot, independent of the eventual stabilization level: the integrated exposure of the slow components to elevated temperature, not merely the endpoint, determines how much of the change is locked in. The assessment that some Earth system components respond to the time-integral of temperature rather than to its instantaneous value is central to why overshoot is treated as a distinct and consequential feature of stabilization pathways rather than as a mere transient that is fully undone on return.

The catastrophic tail of the overshoot risk distribution warrants explicit assessment. Kemp et al. (2022) argue for systematic exploration of catastrophic climate change scenarios — the "climate endgame" — on the grounds that the high-impact, deep-uncertainty tail of the warming distribution has been comparatively neglected relative to central estimates. In the context of overshoot, the concern is that a deep or prolonged overshoot raises the probability of encountering threshold behaviors and self-amplifying feedbacks whose reversibility is unknown and potentially very low. The physical science cannot at present rule out that a sufficiently large overshoot could trigger responses that persist even under aggressive subsequent drawdown, and the precautionary implication is that reliance on overshoot-and-recovery pathways carries a tail risk that is qualitatively different from the risk of a pathway that avoids overshoot altogether.

## 9.5 Carbon dioxide removal: physical-science aspects

**Carbon dioxide removal — the deliberate removal of CO2 from the atmosphere and its durable storage in geological, terrestrial, or ocean reservoirs — is a physically necessary element of any pathway that reaches net-zero CO2 emissions in the presence of residual hard-to-abate emissions, and it is the only means by which warming can be actively reversed after net zero (*high confidence*).** The physical assessment of carbon dioxide removal turns on three quantities that are distinct from the economics or governance of the approaches: the gross quantity of CO2 removed from the atmosphere, the durability or permanence of the storage, and the Earth system side effects — including the carbon-cycle feedback that returns part of the removed carbon to the atmosphere and the local biogeochemical and biophysical consequences of the removal itself. A removal method's net climatic benefit is the gross removal discounted by the carbon-cycle giveback and by any co-emissions or albedo changes it induces, integrated over the storage lifetime.

The concept of net zero itself must be defined precisely for carbon dioxide removal to be assessed coherently. Fankhauser et al. (2021) examine the meaning of net zero and the conditions under which a net-zero claim is physically robust, emphasizing that the climatic effect of a net-zero portfolio depends on the permanence of the removals used to balance residual emissions and on whether like is balanced with like — durable geological storage balancing fossil emissions, rather than temporary biological storage balancing permanent fossil releases. This distinction is central: balancing a permanent addition of geologic carbon to the atmosphere with a temporary removal into a biological store that may later be released does not achieve durable net zero, because the two flows have different permanence. The physical equivalence of a removal to an avoided emission holds only to the extent that the storage endures on the same timescale as the atmospheric perturbation it offsets.

Land-based and ecosystem approaches constitute one principal class. Afforestation, reforestation, improved forest and soil management, and other nature-based approaches remove CO2 through photosynthesis and store it in biomass and soils. Seddon et al. (2021) assess the evidence on nature-based solutions to climate change and emphasize the conditions under which they deliver genuine, durable climate benefit, cautioning against overstating their mitigation potential and against treating biological carbon storage as equivalent to permanent geological storage. The physical limitations of these approaches are intrinsic: biological carbon stores are vulnerable to reversal through fire, drought, pest outbreaks, and land-use change, and their vulnerability increases with warming itself. The declining forest resilience documented by Forzieri et al. (2022) and the forest degradation processes analyzed by Lapola et al. (2023) directly undermine the permanence of land-based removal, because a warming climate erodes the very stores on which the approach depends. Land-based removal also has biophysical side effects — changes in surface albedo, evapotranspiration, and regional hydrology — that can partially offset or, in some regions such as high-latitude afforestation, even reverse the intended cooling, so that the net effect on temperature is not captured by the carbon accounting alone.

Ocean-based approaches form a second class, encompassing the enhancement of biological and chemical carbon uptake by the ocean. These approaches exploit the ocean's role as the largest active carbon reservoir, but they interact with the marine biosphere in ways that carry their own risks; the sensitivity of marine ecosystems to perturbation is documented by Smith et al. (2022) in their assessment of marine heatwave impacts, and the broader question of ocean stewardship for biodiversity, food, and climate is framed by Sala et al. (2021), whose analysis of protecting the global ocean underscores that interventions in ocean biogeochemistry occur within a system that also supports fisheries and biodiversity. The food-provisioning role of the ocean assessed by Boyd et al. (2022), who quantify the contribution of fisheries and aquaculture to the global protein supply, is a reminder that ocean-based carbon removal cannot be assessed on carbon grounds alone; its physical side effects on ocean chemistry and ecosystems bear directly on other services the ocean provides. The permanence of ocean removal depends on whether the carbon is transferred to the deep ocean or to sediments on timescales long compared to surface-ocean exchange.

Engineered approaches constitute a third class, of which direct air capture with geological storage is the archetype. Erans et al. (2022) assess the process technology and techno-economic and socio-political challenges of direct air capture, describing the physical operation of the capture step — the separation of CO2 from ambient air at its low atmospheric concentration — and the energy requirements that this dilute-stream separation entails. The physical advantage of engineered removal with geological storage is permanence and monitorability: CO2 stored in suitable geological formations is durable on timescales of centuries to millennia and its storage can be measured and verified, in contrast to the diffuse and reversible storage of biological approaches. The physical constraints are the energy demand of capturing a dilute gas and the availability and integrity of geological storage. The broader status of carbon capture, utilization, and storage technologies is surveyed by Dziejarski et al. (2023), whose technical assessment of the current status of these technologies in the global economy characterizes the maturity of the capture and storage chain on which engineered removal depends. Yan et al. (2021) review the application of machine learning to carbon capture, utilization, and storage, illustrating that optimization of the capture process and the characterization of storage reservoirs are active areas of methodological development that bear on the achievable efficiency and cost of the physical removal.

A cross-cutting physical result governs all removal approaches: the carbon-cycle response to removal is not the mirror image of the response to emission. When CO2 is withdrawn from the atmosphere, the ocean and land — previously net sinks — reduce their uptake and begin to outgas carbon in response to the lowered atmospheric concentration, returning a fraction of the removed carbon to the atmosphere. This giveback means that the reduction in atmospheric CO2 achieved by a given gross removal is smaller than the removal itself, and the efficiency of removal in lowering the atmospheric burden is lower than the efficiency with which emissions raised it. The practical implication, developed in Section 9.4, is that reversing a given amount of warming requires removing more CO2 than was emitted to cause it. This asymmetry is a robust feature of the coupled carbon cycle and is independent of the removal technology.

The scale and durability required for carbon dioxide removal to contribute meaningfully to stabilization are large. To reach net-zero CO2, removal must offset the residual emissions from sectors that cannot be fully decarbonized; to reverse warming, removal must exceed emissions on a sustained basis. The material and land footprints of these approaches, and their interactions with other Earth system boundaries, mean that carbon dioxide removal cannot be assessed in isolation from the broader planetary context diagnosed by Richardson et al. (2023). Removal deployed at climatically significant scale interacts with the water, land, and biosphere boundaries, and an assessment confined to the carbon boundary alone would misrepresent the net Earth system consequences. The physical-science conclusion is that carbon dioxide removal is necessary for stabilization at low levels and indispensable for any reversal of warming, but that its net climatic benefit is materially less than its gross removal once carbon-cycle giveback, permanence risk, and biophysical side effects are accounted for, and that these discounts differ substantially among approaches.

### 9.5.7 The permanence spectrum and enhanced weathering

**Carbon dioxide removal approaches occupy a spectrum of storage permanence, from the decades-to-centuries vulnerability of biological stores to the millennial durability of carbon mineralized into stable carbonate minerals or stored in deep geological formations, and this permanence spectrum — rather than the gross quantity removed — is the physical property that determines whether a removal genuinely offsets a fossil-carbon emission on the timescale of the atmospheric perturbation (*high confidence*).** The permanence of storage is the single most important physical discriminator among removal approaches, because a removal offsets an emission only for as long as the removed carbon remains out of the atmosphere. Biological stores in vegetation and soils sit at the low-permanence end of the spectrum: they are vulnerable to reversal through fire, drought, pest outbreaks, harvest, and land-use change, and their vulnerability is amplified by the warming they are intended to counter, as the declining forest resilience documented by Forzieri et al. (2022) and the forest degradation documented by Lapola et al. (2023) make clear. Geological storage of captured CO2 sits at the high-permanence end, with retention on timescales of centuries to millennia when the storage formation and its seal are geologically suitable; the technology status of the capture-and-storage chain that delivers this permanence is surveyed by Dziejarski et al. (2023) and its physical operation at the capture step by Erans et al. (2022).

Mineralization approaches, in which CO2 is reacted with alkaline minerals to form stable solid carbonates, occupy the most durable end of the permanence spectrum, because the carbon is converted into a thermodynamically stable mineral form that is not vulnerable to the biological and physical reversals that threaten organic stores. Enhanced weathering — the acceleration of the natural chemical weathering of silicate minerals that consumes atmospheric CO2 over geological time — seeks to draw down CO2 by exposing finely ground reactive minerals to the atmosphere and to soil and ocean water, converting CO2 into dissolved bicarbonate and ultimately into stable carbonate. The physical attraction of these approaches is the durability of the resulting store; their physical constraints are the energy required to grind and transport large quantities of mineral, the rate at which the weathering reaction proceeds, and the local biogeochemical consequences of introducing large quantities of reactive mineral into soils and coastal waters. The optimization of these and other capture and storage processes is an active methodological area, and the application of machine learning to characterize reaction rates, capture materials, and storage reservoirs is reviewed by Yan et al. (2021); the adsorbent and separation materials on which several engineered capture routes depend are the subject of intensive materials research of the kind that governs the achievable energy efficiency of the capture step.

The permanence spectrum interacts with the carbon-cycle giveback assessed in Section 9.5 to determine the net, durable atmospheric drawdown achieved by a removal portfolio. A removal into a low-permanence biological store that is later reversed returns its carbon to the atmosphere and, over the reversal, provides no lasting offset; a removal into a high-permanence mineral or geological store provides a durable offset discounted only by the carbon-cycle giveback. The robust net-zero framing of Fankhauser et al. (2021) — that durable removals must be matched to durable emissions — is the direct expression of this principle: balancing the permanent addition of geologic carbon to the atmosphere with a temporary biological removal does not achieve durable net zero. The physical-science assessment is therefore that removal approaches cannot be aggregated by gross tonnage alone; the permanence of each tonne, and its matching to the permanence of the emission it is intended to offset, is the property that determines the contribution of removal to genuine, durable stabilization. This places a premium on the high-permanence end of the spectrum — geological and mineral storage — for the offsetting of fossil emissions, while assigning the lower-permanence biological approaches a role that must be understood as inherently reversible and vulnerable to the very climate change they are deployed to limit.

## 9.6 Solar radiation modification: physical-science aspects

**Solar radiation modification could rapidly lower global mean surface temperature by increasing the fraction of incoming solar radiation reflected to space, but it addresses only the temperature symptom of elevated CO2 and not the underlying atmospheric burden, leaves ocean acidification unchecked, produces a pattern of regional climate and hydrological change distinct from that of the warming it offsets, and creates a termination-related risk of rapid warming should sustained deployment cease (*high confidence*).** Solar radiation modification is a fundamentally different intervention from carbon dioxide removal: it acts on the shortwave side of the energy balance, reducing absorbed solar radiation, rather than on the longwave side by reducing the CO2 that traps outgoing radiation. Because it does not remove CO2, it does not reduce ocean acidification or the other non-thermal consequences of elevated atmospheric CO2, and it must be sustained for as long as the elevated CO2 persists if the masked warming is not to be realized.

The principal proposed methods increase planetary albedo by different physical means. Stratospheric aerosol injection would introduce reflective particles into the stratosphere to scatter sunlight, mimicking the transient cooling observed after large explosive volcanic eruptions; marine cloud brightening would increase the reflectivity of low marine clouds. The physical efficacy of these methods rests on well-understood radiative principles — increasing albedo reduces absorbed solar energy and hence surface temperature — and the volcanic analogue provides observational support that stratospheric aerosols can cool the surface measurably and rapidly. This rapidity is the defining physical characteristic that distinguishes solar radiation modification from all emissions-based approaches: whereas the temperature response to emissions reductions and to carbon dioxide removal unfolds over decades, the response to a change in planetary albedo is realized within a few years.

That rapidity is inseparable from a corresponding risk. Because solar radiation modification masks rather than removes the warming, the elevated CO2 continues to exert its radiative forcing; if deployment were sustained for a period and then abruptly stopped, the masked warming would be realized over a few years — the termination effect — producing a rate of warming far exceeding anything in the emissions-driven trajectory. The magnitude of this committed rebound grows with the amount of warming being masked and with the duration of deployment, because the accumulated gap between the actual CO2 forcing and the albedo-suppressed temperature widens over time. The physical assessment is therefore that solar radiation modification, once begun at a scale sufficient to mask substantial warming, would create a dependency: ceasing it would expose the system to a rate of temperature change that could exceed the adaptive capacity of ecosystems whose sensitivity to rapid change is documented by Smith et al. (2022) for marine systems, by Harvey et al. (2022) for insects, and by Lees et al. (2022) for birds.

The regional and hydrological consequences of solar radiation modification are a second central physical concern. Reducing incoming solar radiation does not simply reverse greenhouse warming region by region, because the two forcings have different spatial and seasonal structures: greenhouse forcing is distributed by the CO2 field and acts on the longwave, whereas solar reduction acts on the shortwave with its own latitudinal and diurnal pattern. The result is that a deployment tuned to restore global mean temperature would not restore the pre-warming climate everywhere; some regions would be over-cooled and others under-cooled relative to the pre-industrial baseline, and the global hydrological cycle would in general be suppressed, because reducing the shortwave flux at the surface reduces the energy available to drive evaporation. This tendency to weaken the global water cycle is a robust physical feature of solar radiation modification and means that even a deployment that perfectly restored global mean temperature would leave a residual pattern of climate change, particularly in precipitation, that differs from both the warmed and the unwarmed states.

Solar radiation modification also interacts with atmospheric chemistry and with the aerosol-cloud system in ways that carry their own uncertainties. Stratospheric aerosol injection would perturb stratospheric chemistry and could affect the ozone layer; the physical behavior of aerosols in the climate system is itself a source of uncertainty, as illustrated by Brown et al. (2021), whose analysis of biomass-burning aerosols shows that the representation of absorbing aerosols in climate models carries significant biases. This matters for solar radiation modification because the efficacy and side effects of an injected aerosol depend on its optical properties — whether it scatters or also absorbs — and the demonstrated difficulty of representing aerosol absorption accurately implies corresponding uncertainty in the modeled response to deliberate aerosol injection. Marine cloud brightening depends on the aerosol-cloud interaction, one of the least certain elements of the climate system, so the achievable albedo change and its regional distribution are correspondingly uncertain.

The most important physical framing of solar radiation modification is that it is not a substitute for emissions reductions or carbon dioxide removal, because it does not address the quantity — atmospheric CO2 — that governs the long-term state of the climate. It could, in principle, reduce the rate or peak of warming during a period of overshoot, thereby limiting exposure of the slow and biological components to elevated temperature while emissions are brought down and removal is scaled up. But it does so at the cost of introducing the termination risk and the residual regional and hydrological changes, and it leaves ocean acidification — a direct chemical consequence of elevated CO2 rather than of temperature — entirely unaddressed. The vulnerability of ocean ecosystems and the fisheries they support, framed by Sala et al. (2021) and Boyd et al. (2022), would therefore persist under a solar-radiation-modification deployment even if surface temperature were held down. The physical-science assessment is that solar radiation modification is best understood as a potential temporary measure to limit peak warming within a broader strategy dominated by emissions reduction and carbon dioxide removal, and never as an alternative to reducing the atmospheric CO2 burden, because only reducing that burden addresses the full range of physical consequences of elevated CO2.

### 9.6.7 Observational analogues and the limits of inference

**The transient cooling that follows large explosive volcanic eruptions provides the closest observational analogue for stratospheric-aerosol solar radiation modification, but the analogue is imperfect in ways that limit the confidence with which it can constrain the response to a deliberate, sustained deployment (*medium confidence*).** Large eruptions inject sulfate-forming material into the stratosphere and produce a measurable, transient reduction in global mean surface temperature over the following one to two years, confirming that increasing stratospheric aerosol loading cools the surface and that the coupled system responds within the expected physical bounds. This natural experiment provides direct evidence for the sign and approximate magnitude of the radiative and temperature response, and for accompanying effects on the hydrological cycle and on stratospheric dynamics.

The analogue is limited in several respects that bear on its use for assessing deliberate deployment. A volcanic injection is a single, brief pulse, whereas a solar radiation modification deployment intended to mask substantial warming would require sustained injection over decades; the response to a sustained loading is not simply the superposition of pulse responses, because the slow components of the system would have time to adjust and because the microphysics of a continuously replenished aerosol layer differs from that of a decaying volcanic cloud. The optical properties of the injected material govern both its cooling efficacy and its side effects, and the demonstrated difficulty of representing absorbing aerosols accurately in climate models, illustrated by Brown et al. (2021) for biomass-burning aerosols, implies corresponding uncertainty in the modeled response to a deliberately engineered aerosol whose properties would be chosen to maximize scattering and minimize absorption. The inference from the volcanic analogue to a sustained deployment therefore carries an irreducible extrapolation uncertainty.

These observational limits reinforce the assessment of Section 9.6 that solar radiation modification, while physically capable of cooling the surface, entails responses — in regional climate, in the hydrological cycle, in stratospheric chemistry, and in the termination risk — that cannot be fully constrained by the available natural analogue. The ecological systems whose sensitivity to rapid change is documented by Smith et al. (2022), Harvey et al. (2022), and Lees et al. (2022) would be exposed to a climate produced by a forcing combination — reduced shortwave in the presence of elevated CO2 — that has no close analogue in the observational or paleoclimate record, so that the ecological consequences of a sustained deployment are correspondingly uncertain. The physical-science conclusion is that the observational basis for assessing solar radiation modification is materially weaker than that for assessing the response to CO2 forcing, and that this asymmetry in evidence is itself a reason to treat deliberate solar intervention as carrying deep uncertainty distinct from the better-constrained response to changes in the atmospheric CO2 burden.

## 9.7 Earth system responses to stabilization

**When global mean surface temperature is stabilized, the components of the Earth system do not stabilize simultaneously: fast-responding components equilibrate within decades, while sea level, ice sheets, deep-ocean heat content, ocean chemistry, and some biosphere carbon stores continue to evolve for centuries to millennia, so that a stabilized global mean temperature masks a system still in transient adjustment (*high confidence*).** The assessment of stabilization must therefore distinguish between the surface temperature — which is the quantity that carbon budgets and net-zero targets are framed around and which does stabilize when net CO2 emissions cease — and the many other quantities that continue to change even after temperature levels off. This section assesses the response of the principal slow-responding components, and the corresponding fast-responding components, under a stabilized temperature.

### 9.7.1 Sea level and ice sheets

**Global mean sea level will continue to rise for centuries to millennia after global mean surface temperature is stabilized, because the thermal expansion of the deep ocean and the response of the ice sheets are governed by the integrated temperature history rather than by the instantaneous temperature, and both respond with long lags and, in the case of the ice sheets, potential hysteresis (*high confidence*).** Sea-level rise is the archetypal committed response. Its thermosteric component reflects the heat already stored in the ocean, which continues to penetrate to depth and expand the water column long after surface temperature stabilizes; its ice-sheet component reflects the slow dynamic response of grounded ice to the warming already realized. Neither component ceases when temperature stabilizes; both continue for as long as the deep ocean and the ice sheets remain out of equilibrium with the stabilized surface climate, which is a matter of centuries to millennia.

The ice-sheet response carries a distinct physical risk of irreversibility through hysteresis. Once a threshold of sustained warming is crossed, the retreat of a marine-based ice sheet can become self-sustaining, so that returning temperature to below the threshold does not halt or reverse the retreat on policy-relevant timescales. This means that a temporary overshoot of a threshold could commit additional multi-metre sea-level rise even if temperature is subsequently returned to a lower stabilized level — a direct manifestation of the overshoot irreversibility assessed in Section 9.4. The physical assessment is that the sea-level commitment associated with a given stabilization level is larger the higher that level, and that the commitment is realized over such long timescales that a stabilized temperature is fully consistent with continued, substantial sea-level rise over the lifetimes of the communities and ecosystems exposed to it.

### 9.7.2 The deep ocean and ocean heat content

**Ocean heat content continues to increase for centuries after surface temperature stabilizes, as heat mixes from the surface into the deep ocean, and this continued deep-ocean warming is effectively irreversible on human timescales (*high confidence*).** The ocean absorbs the large majority of the excess energy trapped by the enhanced greenhouse effect, and the transfer of that heat from the surface to the deep ocean proceeds over centuries. When surface temperature stabilizes, the deep ocean remains far from equilibrium with the warmer surface, and heat continues to be transferred downward until the deep ocean warms to match. This continued deep-ocean heat uptake is one of the two terms whose balance produces the near-zero zero-emissions commitment assessed in Section 9.3; here its relevance is that the deep ocean is a reservoir of committed change that persists under a stabilized surface temperature. Because the deep ocean overturns on millennial timescales, the heat it absorbs is removed from contact with the atmosphere for centuries, and its warming is not reversed by a stabilization or even a modest reduction of surface temperature.

The continued warming of the deep ocean has consequences beyond thermal expansion. It affects ocean stratification, the solubility of gases including CO2 and oxygen, and the circulation patterns that redistribute heat and carbon. Changes in stratification and circulation feed back on the ocean's capacity to take up heat and carbon, and hence on the trajectory of atmospheric CO2 during the drawdown phase of a stabilization pathway. The physical assessment is that the deep ocean integrates and stores the perturbation, releasing it only slowly, so that the ocean interior remains a signature of the peak warming long after the surface has stabilized.

### 9.7.3 Ocean acidification and biogeochemistry

**Ocean acidification is driven directly by the uptake of atmospheric CO2 rather than by temperature, so it is not addressed by any intervention that lowers temperature without lowering atmospheric CO2, and it stabilizes or reverses only as the atmospheric CO2 burden itself is stabilized or reduced (*high confidence*).** The dissolution of CO2 in seawater lowers ocean pH and reduces the availability of carbonate ions, with consequences for calcifying organisms and marine ecosystems. Because this is a chemical consequence of the CO2 burden, ocean acidification tracks atmospheric CO2, and stabilizing temperature through solar radiation modification while leaving CO2 elevated would leave acidification entirely unchecked — one of the central physical reasons that solar radiation modification is not a substitute for reducing the CO2 burden, as assessed in Section 9.6.

The interaction of acidification with warming and deoxygenation compounds the stress on marine ecosystems. Smith et al. (2022) document the biological impacts of marine heatwaves, and these thermal stresses act in combination with acidification and with changes in oxygen availability driven by the ocean warming assessed in Section 9.7.2. The consequences for the services the ocean provides — including the protein supply quantified by Boyd et al. (2022) and the biodiversity and climate functions framed by Sala et al. (2021) — mean that the ocean's biogeochemical response to stabilization is not captured by temperature alone. A pathway that stabilizes temperature but stabilizes CO2 at a high level leaves the ocean in a chemically altered state; only a pathway that reduces the atmospheric CO2 burden begins to reverse acidification, and even then the deep-ocean chemistry adjusts slowly.

### 9.7.4 The biosphere and terrestrial carbon stores

**Under a stabilized climate the terrestrial biosphere continues to adjust, and the resilience and carbon-storage capacity of major biomes may continue to decline even after temperature stabilizes, so that the land carbon sink cannot be assumed to hold its historical strength through and beyond stabilization (*medium confidence*).** The land biosphere has historically absorbed a substantial fraction of anthropogenic CO2 emissions, and the maintenance of this sink is important to the trajectory of atmospheric CO2 during and after a stabilization pathway. But the sink's strength is itself sensitive to the climate. Forzieri et al. (2022) identify emerging signals of declining forest resilience under climate change, indicating that forests are becoming less able to recover from disturbance; Lapola et al. (2023) document the drivers and impacts of Amazon forest degradation, a process that reduces the carbon density and the sink capacity of one of the largest terrestrial carbon reservoirs. These processes imply that the land sink assumed in stabilization pathways may weaken, and a weakening land sink means that a given emissions trajectory results in a higher atmospheric CO2 concentration and hence a higher stabilization temperature than a calculation assuming a constant sink.

The broader integrity of the biosphere bears on stabilization through multiple channels. Folke et al. (2021) assess the human future within the Anthropocene biosphere, framing the biosphere as the active matrix within which the climate system is embedded and on whose functioning the carbon cycle depends. The biodiversity losses documented by Cowie et al. (2022) in their assessment of the sixth-mass-extinction question, the insect declines warned of by Harvey et al. (2022), and the bird population changes assessed by Lees et al. (2022) are all indicators of a biosphere under stress, and a degraded biosphere is generally a less effective and less stable carbon sink. The physical assessment is that stabilizing temperature does not stabilize the biosphere, and that the continued adjustment of ecosystems — including the loss of resilience and the risk of shifting from sink to source — introduces a feedback that can undermine the very stabilization it accompanies.

### 9.7.5 The hydrological cycle and climate extremes under stabilization

**The global hydrological cycle and the statistics of climate extremes continue to adjust as the system approaches equilibrium under a stabilized temperature, and the pattern of regional climate does not become fixed at the moment global mean temperature stabilizes (*high confidence*).** Because different regions and different components of the water cycle respond on different timescales, a stabilized global mean temperature is compatible with continued regional change: precipitation patterns, the frequency and intensity of extremes, and the distribution of aridity continue to evolve as the ocean and land surface equilibrate. The regional character of climate change under continued and stabilized warming is documented in specific settings by Zittis et al. (2022), whose assessment of climate change and weather extremes in the Eastern Mediterranean and Middle East characterizes a region where warming and drying are especially pronounced; such regional analyses show that the extremes which drive impacts are not uniform and do not track global mean temperature in a simple way.

The comprehensive reviews of climate change impacts, adaptation, and mitigation by Abbass et al. (2022) synthesize the breadth of consequences that persist under continued warming and that would only gradually stabilize as the climate equilibrates. The relevance to stabilization is that the impacts which motivate stabilization — extreme heat, drought, flooding, and their cascading effects — respond to the regional expression of warming, and that these regional expressions continue to sharpen for some time after the global mean stabilizes. The assessment is that stabilization of global mean temperature is a necessary but not sufficient condition for stabilization of the regional climate that determines impacts, and that the lag between global-mean stabilization and regional stabilization is itself a physical feature of the system that adaptation must contend with.

### 9.7.6 Pathways to stabilization: the emissions system

**Reaching the net-zero CO2 emissions required for stabilization entails a transformation of the energy and industrial systems that supply the emissions, and the physical characteristics of the low-carbon technologies that would replace fossil combustion — their resource requirements, their resilience to a changing climate, and their own life-cycle emissions — bear on whether and how quickly stabilization can be achieved (*high confidence*).** While the detailed assessment of mitigation options is the province of Working Group III, the physical-science framing of stabilization requires acknowledging that the emissions trajectory is produced by a physical energy system. Chen et al. (2022) review strategies to achieve a carbon-neutral society, and Zhao et al. (2022) analyze the challenges and opportunities of China's energy transitions for carbon neutrality; these assessments describe the scale of the transformation in the sectors identified by Lamb et al. (2021) as the dominant emission sources.

The physical resilience of the replacement energy system is itself a consideration under a changing climate. Osman et al. (2022) assess the cost, environmental impact, and resilience of renewable energy under a changing climate, noting that the low-carbon technologies deployed to reach net zero must themselves function reliably in the altered climate they are intended to stabilize. The feasibility of very high shares of renewable generation is examined by Breyer et al. (2022) in their assessment of the history and future of 100 percent renewable energy systems research, and the life-cycle emissions of bioenergy — relevant to the net carbon accounting of pathways that rely on biomass — are assessed by Osman et al. (2021) in their review of the conversion of biomass to biofuels and its life-cycle assessment. The industrial sources of hard-to-abate emissions include cement, and the physical routes to reducing cement's carbon intensity through supplementary cementitious materials are assessed by Snellings et al. (2023); such industrial emissions are among the residual sources that, if not eliminated, must be balanced by the carbon dioxide removal assessed in Section 9.5 for net zero to be reached. Circular-economy strategies that reduce material throughput, assessed by Yang et al. (2022) for their role in combating climate change, and the application of machine learning across the mitigation landscape reviewed by Kaack et al. (2022), represent additional physical levers on the emissions that must reach zero for temperature to stabilize.

The synthesis of this subsection is that stabilization is achieved not in the abstract but through a physical transformation of the systems that emit CO2, and that the characteristics of that transformation — the resource base of low-carbon energy, its resilience to the climate it operates in, the residual emissions from sectors that resist full decarbonization, and the life-cycle emissions of the replacement technologies themselves — all feed back on the emissions trajectory and hence on the stabilization level that is physically achievable.

### 9.7.7 Large-scale ocean circulation under stabilization

**The large-scale ocean overturning circulation responds slowly to changes in surface forcing and may continue to weaken for a period after global mean surface temperature is stabilized, with consequences for the redistribution of heat, carbon, and nutrients that persist independent of the surface temperature signal (*medium confidence*).** The circulation that carries warm surface water poleward and returns cold, dense water at depth is driven by gradients in temperature and salinity that the warming perturbs through surface heating and through freshwater input from a intensified hydrological cycle and from ice loss. Because the circulation integrates these forcings over the volume of the ocean, its adjustment lags the surface, and a stabilized surface temperature does not imply a stabilized circulation. A weaker overturning redistributes less heat poleward, alters regional sea level through changes in the density structure, and changes the delivery of carbon and nutrients between the surface and the interior.

The circulation's response bears directly on the carbon cycle terms that condition stabilization. A weakening of the overturning reduces the rate at which carbon-rich surface water is transported to depth, which can alter the ocean's capacity to take up anthropogenic CO2 during the approach to net zero and its tendency to outgas during a subsequent drawdown. The partitioning of emissions among the atmosphere, ocean, and land documented in the Global Carbon Budget assessments of Friedlingstein et al. (2022), Friedlingstein et al. (2023), and Friedlingstein et al. (2025) reflects the present-day strength of these transport processes; a change in the circulation would shift that partitioning, and hence the atmospheric CO2 concentration corresponding to a given cumulative emission. The physical assessment is that the ocean's overturning is a slow component whose continued adjustment under a stabilized surface temperature couples back onto the very carbon-cycle quantities that determine the stabilization level, and that this coupling is a source of the medium-confidence qualification attached to the behavior of the carbon cycle during and after stabilization.

The ecological consequences of circulation change compound those of warming and acidification assessed in Sections 9.7.3 and 9.7.4. Changes in the delivery of nutrients to the surface ocean alter primary productivity, with consequences for the marine food web whose provisioning role Boyd et al. (2022) quantify and whose biodiversity Sala et al. (2021) frame as a global stewardship concern. The thermal stresses documented by Smith et al. (2022) act in combination with circulation-driven changes in nutrient supply and oxygenation, so that the marine biosphere under a stabilized surface temperature continues to experience a changing physical and chemical environment. The assessment is that stabilization of surface temperature is compatible with continued reorganization of the ocean interior and its ecosystems over centuries.

### 9.7.8 Committed change and the millennial perspective

**The full response of the Earth system to a given stabilization level unfolds over centuries to millennia, so the climate that is stabilized on the timescale of the surface temperature is not the equilibrium climate that the corresponding forcing would ultimately produce, and substantial committed change remains latent in the slow components long after the fast components have adjusted (*high confidence*).** The distinction between the transient climate that is realized when surface temperature stabilizes and the equilibrium climate that the same forcing would eventually produce is central to interpreting any stabilization target. The fast components — the atmosphere, the surface ocean, sea ice, and the land surface — approach their response to a stabilized forcing within decades. The slow components — the deep ocean, the ice sheets, and the deep terrestrial and permafrost carbon stores — continue to evolve for centuries to millennia, and their eventual contribution to sea level, to the carbon cycle, and to regional climate is not realized at the moment the surface stabilizes.

This millennial perspective reframes the meaning of a stabilization level. Holding surface temperature at a given level does not hold the Earth system at the state associated with that level; rather, it initiates a long adjustment during which sea level continues to rise, the deep ocean continues to warm, and the ice sheets continue to respond to the integrated warming they have experienced. The commitment already incurred by historical emissions therefore includes changes that will only be expressed over many generations, and the choice of a stabilization level determines not only the surface climate of the coming decades but the trajectory of the slow components over the coming millennia. The planetary-boundaries synthesis of Richardson et al. (2023), in placing the climate system alongside the other boundaries that have been transgressed, situates this long-term commitment within the broader, multi-dimensional displacement of the Earth system from its Holocene reference state — a displacement whose full expression, like that of the climate commitment itself, extends far beyond the horizon over which surface temperature stabilizes.

The irreversibilities assessed throughout this chapter acquire their full significance in this millennial frame. The hysteresis of the marine-based ice sheets, the effectively permanent character of deep-ocean heat uptake, the biodiversity losses documented by Cowie et al. (2022), Harvey et al. (2022), and Lees et al. (2022), and the degradation of carbon-storing biomes documented by Forzieri et al. (2022) and Lapola et al. (2023) all represent changes that are not reversed on the timescale over which surface temperature can be stabilized or even modestly reduced. The physical-science conclusion is that the assessment of a stabilization level must extend beyond the surface temperature of the coming century to the committed trajectory of the slow components over the coming millennia, because it is in those slow components that the largest and least reversible consequences of the chosen level are ultimately expressed.

### 9.7.9 Monitoring and verifying stabilization

**Verifying that the climate system is approaching or has reached stabilization requires sustained observation of the coupled system — the atmospheric CO2 burden and its growth rate, the partitioning of emissions among the reservoirs, the planetary energy imbalance, and the indicators of human influence — because no single quantity is sufficient to confirm that emissions have reached net zero and that temperature has ceased to trend (*high confidence*).** Because stabilization is a property of the whole system, its verification cannot rest on surface temperature alone, which is subject to internal variability that can mask or mimic a change in the underlying trend over short periods. The indicators synthesis of Forster et al. (2023) provides the framework for this verification, assembling the large-scale indicators of the state of the climate system and of human influence — including estimates of human-induced warming, the components of effective radiative forcing, and the planetary energy imbalance — into an annually updated assessment against which the approach to stabilization can be judged.

The atmospheric CO2 burden and its partitioning are the primary observational anchor. The Global Carbon Budget assessments of Friedlingstein et al. (2022), Friedlingstein et al. (2023), and Friedlingstein et al. (2025) quantify the total emissions and their partitioning among the atmosphere, ocean, and land, and it is the atmospheric growth rate that would first reflect an approach to net zero: as net emissions decline toward zero, the atmospheric CO2 growth rate would fall toward zero, and a sustained cessation of atmospheric CO2 accumulation would be among the earliest large-scale signatures of an approach to stabilization. The continued measurement of the ocean and land sinks is essential to this interpretation, because a change in the sinks — such as the weakening implied by the declining forest resilience of Forzieri et al. (2022) and the forest degradation of Lapola et al. (2023) — would alter the relationship between emissions and atmospheric concentration and hence the atmospheric signature of a given emissions trajectory.

The planetary energy imbalance provides an independent and physically fundamental measure of the approach to stabilization. So long as the energy imbalance is positive, the system continues to accumulate energy, predominantly in the ocean, and surface temperature has not fully equilibrated with the forcing. A decline of the energy imbalance toward zero would indicate that the system is approaching equilibrium with the stabilized forcing, and the continued warming of the deep ocean assessed in Section 9.7.2 means that the energy imbalance persists even as surface temperature levels off. The physical assessment is that confirming stabilization requires observing the convergence of several independent quantities — the atmospheric CO2 growth rate, the emissions partitioning, the planetary energy imbalance, and the derived indicators of human influence — and that a sustained observing system spanning the atmosphere, the ocean interior, and the terrestrial and marine carbon stores is a prerequisite for distinguishing genuine stabilization from the transient signatures of internal variability. The reviews of the observational and modeling basis for climate assessment, including the comprehensive synthesis of impacts and mitigation by Abbass et al. (2022), underscore that the verification of stabilization is an ongoing observational undertaking rather than a one-time determination.

## 9.8 Synthesis and key uncertainties

**Taken together, the evidence assessed in this chapter establishes that stabilizing global surface temperature is physically achievable by bringing net CO2 emissions to zero, that the level of stabilization is set by cumulative emissions, that warming can be halted within a human generation but reversed only through sustained net-negative emissions, and that many components of the Earth system continue to change under a stabilized temperature (*high confidence*).** The near-linear relationship between cumulative CO2 emissions and peak warming is the unifying result: it underwrites the remaining carbon budget, it implies the near-zero zero-emissions commitment, and it makes clear that the central determinant of the stabilization level is the total quantity of CO2 emitted before net zero is reached. The successive Global Carbon Budget assessments of Friedlingstein et al. (2022), Friedlingstein et al. (2023), and Friedlingstein et al. (2025) show that this total continues to accumulate at close to its historical rate, contracting the budgets for the lowest stabilization levels toward exhaustion within the current decade.

The chapter has emphasized three physically distinct concepts that must not be conflated. Halting warming is achieved at net-zero CO2 and requires no active drawdown; reversing warming requires sustained net-negative emissions and is made harder by the carbon-cycle giveback that returns part of any removal to the atmosphere; and the reversibility of specific impacts ranges from relatively high for global mean surface temperature to very low for deep-ocean heat content, ice-sheet retreat, species extinctions, and the ecological consequences of marine heatwaves. Overshoot pathways, which the small remaining budgets for low limits make likely, incur commitments and irreversibilities that scale with the depth and duration of the overshoot, providing a physical rationale for minimizing both.

Carbon dioxide removal is assessed as physically necessary for net zero in the presence of residual emissions and indispensable for any reversal of warming, with land-based, ocean-based, and engineered approaches differing sharply in permanence, monitorability, and side effects; the net climatic benefit of any approach is materially less than its gross removal once carbon-cycle giveback, permanence risk, and biophysical effects are counted. Solar radiation modification is assessed as capable of rapidly lowering temperature by increasing planetary albedo but as addressing only the thermal symptom of elevated CO2, leaving ocean acidification unchecked, producing distinct regional and hydrological changes, and creating a termination risk of rapid warming if sustained deployment ceased; it is therefore not a substitute for reducing the CO2 burden.

The principal uncertainties that condition these conclusions are the following. First, the strength of the cumulative-emissions-to-warming relationship and the contribution of non-CO2 forcers set the magnitude of the remaining budget, and both carry uncertainty that maps directly onto the allowable emissions for a given limit; the annually updated indicators of Forster et al. (2023) narrow but do not eliminate this uncertainty. Second, the zero-emissions commitment is assessed with medium confidence because Earth system feedbacks — permafrost carbon, and the biosphere feedbacks implied by the declining resilience documented by Forzieri et al. (2022) and the degradation documented by Lapola et al. (2023) — could shift the commitment toward positive values not captured in the models used to derive it. Third, the efficiency of net-negative emissions in reducing atmospheric CO2, governed by the carbon-cycle giveback, determines how much removal is required to reverse a given amount of warming and is a key control on the feasibility of overshoot-and-recovery pathways. Fourth, the thresholds for irreversible ice-sheet retreat and other slow-component hysteresis behaviors are uncertain, and the catastrophic tail of the risk distribution — argued by Kemp et al. (2022) to warrant systematic exploration — remains poorly constrained. Fifth, the permanence of land- and ocean-based carbon storage under a warming climate, and the aerosol-related uncertainties that condition the efficacy and side effects of solar radiation modification illustrated by Brown et al. (2021), limit confidence in the net effect of the deliberate interventions.

The overarching physical-science conclusion is that the levers available for stabilization — emissions reduction, carbon dioxide removal, and solar radiation modification — are not interchangeable. Emissions reduction to net zero is the only lever that halts warming by addressing its cause; carbon dioxide removal is the only lever that can reverse warming and is required to reach net zero given residual emissions; and solar radiation modification can only mask warming temporarily while leaving its cause and its non-thermal consequences in place. The physical evidence assessed here does not prescribe how these levers should be used, but it establishes unambiguously that stabilization at low levels requires the earliest achievable approach to net-zero CO2, that reliance on overshoot and later reversal is physically costlier and riskier than avoiding overshoot, and that the slow components of the Earth system commit the planet to continued change for centuries after the surface temperature is stabilized.
