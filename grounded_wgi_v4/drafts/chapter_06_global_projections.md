# Chapter 6: Long-Term Global Projections

## Executive Summary

**Global surface temperature will continue to rise until at least mid-century under all assessed emission scenarios, and the level of warming reached by 2100 is determined overwhelmingly by cumulative future carbon dioxide emissions rather than by the pathway taken to any given level (*virtually certain*).** The multi-model ensembles that underpin this chapter place the century-end warming across the plausible range of low- to high-emission scenarios between roughly 1.4 degrees C and more than 4 degrees C above the late-nineteenth-century baseline, with the spread dominated by scenario choice at the high end and by climate response uncertainty at the low end (*high confidence*) (Tebaldi et al., 2021).

**Warming-level framing — organizing projected impacts by global mean temperature increase rather than by scenario or date — is the most robust way to communicate long-term change, because many hazard metrics scale more tightly with global warming level than with the emission trajectory that produced it (*high confidence*).** Every increment of warming matters: glacier loss, heat-extreme frequency, drought expansion, and the probability of crossing critical thresholds all increase monotonically and, for several systems, super-linearly with each additional tenth of a degree (Rounce et al., 2023) (Yuan et al., 2023).

**The polar and high-mountain regions will continue to warm substantially faster than the global mean, sustaining Arctic amplification at roughly three to four times the global rate and elevation-dependent warming across the world's mountains (*high confidence*).** This pattern, already observed, is a robust feature of every projection ensemble and drives disproportionate change in the cryosphere, in high-latitude ecosystems, and in the ocean's polar sink regions (Rantanen et al., 2022) (Pepin et al., 2022).

**Global precipitation will increase in the ensemble mean, but the change is spatially organized into a robust "wet-get-wetter, dry-get-drier" and "wet-season-wetter" pattern superimposed on large regional uncertainty, and the frequency and intensity of both heavy-precipitation and drought extremes will rise together (*high confidence*).** Flash-drought onset accelerates under warming across most land regions, and anthropogenic influence on drought frequency, duration and intensity is already detectable (Yuan et al., 2023) (Chiang et al., 2021).

**Global mean sea level will rise through 2100 and for centuries to millennia beyond, and the deep-time commitment is effectively irreversible on human timescales even if temperatures are stabilized (*high confidence*).** The century-scale rise is dominated by ocean thermal expansion and glacier melt, both of which are well constrained; the deep uncertainty resides in the marine-based sectors of the Antarctic Ice Sheet, whose instability governs the high-end tail (Rounce et al., 2023).

**The ocean will continue to warm, acidify, lose oxygen, and experience more frequent, intense and prolonged marine heatwaves throughout the twenty-first century (*virtually certain*),** with biological consequences that scale steeply with warming level (Smith et al., 2022). Large-scale overturning circulation is assessed to weaken over the century, though a complete collapse before 2100 is not the assessed central outcome.

**Several large-scale components of the Earth system carry a non-negligible probability of crossing self-perpetuating thresholds under sustained high warming, including the Amazon forest and marine-based Antarctic ice (*medium confidence* in the thresholds; *high confidence* that the risk rises with warming).** Low-likelihood, high-impact and catastrophic outcomes cannot be excluded and warrant explicit assessment rather than omission (Flores et al., 2024) (Kemp et al., 2022).

**Model evaluation and observational constraints tighten the projected ranges relative to the raw ensemble spread, and emergent-constraint and reanalysis-based methods now provide a defensible basis for narrowing — but not eliminating — climate-response uncertainty (*high confidence*).** Machine-learning weather and climate emulators have matured to the point of complementing, though not replacing, process-based general circulation models for specific projection tasks (Bi et al., 2023).

## 6.1 Scenarios, Warming Levels, and the Basis for Projection

### 6.1.1 The scenario framework and the primacy of cumulative emissions

**Long-term global projections rest on a nested framework of socioeconomic storylines, emission and concentration pathways, and the climate-model ensembles forced by them; within this framework, cumulative carbon dioxide emissions are the single most powerful predictor of the warming reached at any future date (*virtually certain*).** The Scenario Model Intercomparison Project provides the coordinated set of forcing pathways spanning very low to very high emissions that the coupled models translate into physical climate futures, and it is the backbone of the projections assessed in this chapter (Tebaldi et al., 2021). The design principle of this framework is that the same global warming level can be reached by different combinations of emissions, aerosols, and short-lived forcers, but the near-linear relationship between cumulative carbon dioxide and global temperature means that the total amount of carbon released — not the shape of the release curve — sets the eventual warming.

This near-linearity has a profound implication for how projections are read. It means that the difference between a world that peaks emissions early and declines steeply and a world that delays and overshoots is, to first order, a difference in the total carbon budget consumed, and therefore in the committed warming. The annual carbon budget assessments that track this consumption show that emissions have continued at or near record levels through the early 2020s, narrowing the remaining budget for any given temperature target and shifting the plausible envelope of century-end outcomes upward relative to the most optimistic pathways (Friedlingstein et al., 2022) (Friedlingstein et al., 2023). The most recent budget accounting confirms that the residual budget consistent with holding warming to the lower end of the assessed range is being drawn down rapidly and, on current trajectories, would be exhausted within a small number of years to a decade at contemporary emission rates (Friedlingstein et al., 2025).

The scenario framework also embeds a structural asymmetry that dominates the projection spread: at the high-emission end, the divergence between scenarios is the primary source of uncertainty in century-end warming, whereas at the low-emission end the residual uncertainty is dominated by the physical response of the climate system to a given forcing (Tebaldi et al., 2021). This is why warming-level framing, discussed below, is increasingly preferred to scenario-and-date framing: it isolates the physically robust, warming-dependent signal from the deeply uncertain question of which emission path humanity will actually follow.

### 6.1.2 Warming-level framing

**Expressing projected changes as functions of global warming level — 1.5, 2, 3, and 4 degrees C above the pre-industrial baseline — rather than as functions of scenario and calendar year is the most robust organizing principle for long-term projections, because many impact-relevant quantities are approximately invariant to the pathway that produced a given warming (*high confidence*).** The value of this framing is that it separates two logically distinct questions: how much a hazard changes per degree of global warming, which is a comparatively well-constrained physical-science question, and how much warming the world will experience, which is a socioeconomic and policy question. Glacier mass loss illustrates the framing cleanly: projected twenty-first-century glacier loss is a smooth, monotonic function of peak global temperature, such that "every increase in temperature matters" for the fraction of glacier mass and glacier number that survives the century (Rounce et al., 2023).

Warming-level framing also exposes the strong nonlinearities that scenario-and-date framing can obscure. Several hazard metrics scale super-linearly with warming, so that the incremental impact of moving from 2 to 3 degrees C exceeds that of moving from 1 to 2 degrees C. Flash-drought onset frequency, for example, is projected to increase across most land regions with a sensitivity that steepens at higher warming (Yuan et al., 2023). The probability of compound and record-shattering extremes, and the likelihood of crossing thresholds in slow-responding systems such as ice sheets and forests, likewise rise disproportionately at the upper end of the warming range (Flores et al., 2024). The framing therefore does double duty: it makes projections more robust to scenario uncertainty and it clarifies the physical case for limiting peak warming.

A limitation of warming-level framing must be stated explicitly. Some processes are not invariant to pathway even at a fixed warming level — most importantly those governed by the rate of change or by the integrated exposure over time, such as ocean heat content, thermosteric sea level, and ice-sheet response, which continue to evolve after surface temperature stabilizes (*high confidence*). For these committed, slow-responding quantities, two worlds at the same global warming level but arriving by different paths and at different times can have materially different states. The framing is thus most powerful for fast-responding atmospheric and land-surface hazards and must be applied with care to the ocean and cryosphere, which are assessed later in this chapter.

### 6.1.3 The pattern of warming: polar amplification and elevation dependence

**Projected warming is not spatially uniform; it is amplified over land relative to ocean, in the Arctic relative to lower latitudes, and at high elevations relative to adjacent lowlands, and these patterns are robust across every projection ensemble (*high confidence*).** Arctic amplification is the most pronounced regional signal: observations show the Arctic has warmed at close to four times the global rate since 1979, a ratio that exceeds many earlier model estimates and that projections carry forward through the century as a persistent feature (Rantanen et al., 2022). The physical drivers — sea-ice and snow-albedo feedbacks, changes in atmospheric and oceanic heat transport, and lower-tropospheric stability that traps warming near the surface — are well understood and ensure the pattern's persistence under continued forcing.

Elevation-dependent warming is the mountain analogue of polar amplification. Across the world's mountain regions, warming rates frequently increase with altitude, driven by snow-albedo feedback at the retreating snowline, by changes in cloud and water-vapour distribution, and by aerosol deposition on snow and ice (Pepin et al., 2022). The consequence is that high-mountain cryospheres — glaciers, snowpack, and permafrost — are exposed to warming that can exceed the global mean substantially, accelerating their loss and the downstream hydrological changes that depend on them. Because a large fraction of the world's population depends on mountain water towers for dry-season flow, elevation-dependent warming translates the global temperature signal into concentrated regional water-security consequences.

The land-ocean warming contrast, a third robust pattern, arises from the ocean's vast thermal inertia and its efficient evaporative cooling, which cause land to warm roughly 1.4 to 1.6 times faster than the ocean in the ensemble mean. This contrast reshapes atmospheric circulation, shifts monsoon and storm-track behaviour, and concentrates the most rapid warming — and therefore the most rapid change in heat extremes — over the continental interiors where most people live.

### 6.1.4 Model evaluation and constrained projections

**Confidence in long-term projections rests on the demonstrated ability of the underlying models to reproduce the observed mean climate, its variability, and its historical trends, and on the use of observational constraints to narrow the raw ensemble spread (*high confidence*).** Model evaluation proceeds on multiple fronts: comparison of simulated climatologies against modern reanalyses, assessment of the amplitude and phase of internal modes of variability, and verification that the models reproduce the observed historical warming and its spatial fingerprint. High-resolution ocean and coupled reanalyses now available provide the observational reference against which model biases in near-surface temperature, soil moisture, and ocean state are quantified (Jean-Michel et al., 2021).

Emergent constraints and other observationally based methods narrow the projected range by exploiting statistical relationships, established across the ensemble, between an observable feature of the present climate and an uncertain aspect of the future response. Applied to quantities such as the carbon-cycle feedback, the snow-albedo feedback, and regional hydroclimate, these methods can meaningfully tighten the projected range, though they do not eliminate structural uncertainty and must be validated against the physical mechanism they invoke to avoid spurious correlations (*medium confidence* in any single constraint; *high confidence* that the ensemble of constraints reduces the effective spread). The soil-moisture–atmosphere coupling that governs a large share of interannual carbon-uptake variability is one such constrainable process, and its representation is now a standard benchmark for land-surface components (Humphrey et al., 2021).

The maturation of machine-learning weather and climate emulators is a notable development for the projection enterprise. Data-driven models trained on reanalysis now match or exceed operational physics-based systems for medium-range global weather forecasting at a fraction of the computational cost (Bi et al., 2023). For long-term climate projection these emulators do not replace process-based general circulation models — they cannot, by construction, simulate forcing regimes outside their training distribution with guaranteed fidelity — but they are increasingly used to downscale, to generate large initial-condition ensembles for internal-variability quantification, and to accelerate the exploration of scenario space. Their role in this assessment is complementary: they sharpen specific projection tasks while the physical models remain the authority on the response to novel forcing.

## 6.2 Projected Changes in Temperature, Precipitation, and Circulation

### 6.2.1 Global surface temperature and the planetary energy budget

**Global mean surface temperature will rise until at least the middle of the twenty-first century under all assessed scenarios, and the warming reached by 2100 spans from about 1.4 degrees C under the most stringent mitigation to well above 4 degrees C under sustained high emissions (*virtually certain* that warming continues to mid-century; *high confidence* in the assessed ranges).** The ScenarioMIP ensemble that underpins these ranges shows a consistent ordering across scenarios and a scenario-dependent widening of the spread through the century, with the low-emission pathways stabilizing and even slightly declining in temperature after mid-century while the high-emission pathways continue to accelerate (Tebaldi et al., 2021). The central estimate for any given scenario is well constrained by the models' agreement on the transient response to cumulative emissions; the residual spread at a fixed scenario reflects the range of equilibrium and transient climate sensitivities across the ensemble.

The physical basis for continued warming is the planetary energy imbalance. As long as the atmospheric burden of carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases rises, the top-of-atmosphere energy budget remains in surplus and the system accumulates heat, the great majority of which enters the ocean. This imbalance is the reason surface temperature does not respond instantaneously to emissions and the reason warming continues for a time even after emissions decline: the system is always chasing an equilibrium set by the current forcing. The carbon-budget assessments quantify the forcing side of this balance, tracking the atmospheric growth rate and the partitioning of emitted carbon among atmosphere, ocean, and land, and they show that the airborne fraction has remained broadly stable even as absolute emissions have risen, so that atmospheric concentrations — and therefore forcing — continue to climb (Friedlingstein et al., 2023).

A crucial feature of the temperature projections is the behaviour of overshoot pathways, in which warming exceeds a target level and is subsequently brought back down through net-negative emissions. Because the temperature response to cumulative emissions is near-linear and largely reversible for the atmosphere on decadal timescales, peak warming is set by peak cumulative emissions; but the slow-responding components of the system — deep ocean heat, thermosteric sea level, ice sheets — do not reverse on the same timescale and carry a memory of the overshoot (*high confidence*). The assessment therefore distinguishes sharply between the reversibility of surface air temperature and the effective irreversibility of the committed changes in the ocean and cryosphere that accrue during an overshoot.

### 6.2.2 Regional temperature and extreme heat

**Extreme heat will intensify everywhere land warms, and because heat extremes scale with and often exceed the local mean warming, the frequency, duration, and intensity of hot extremes rise faster than the global mean temperature (*virtually certain*).** The land-ocean contrast and continental amplification concentrate the most rapid warming — and the most rapid change in the tails of the temperature distribution — over inhabited land. Regions that combine high present-day temperatures with strong warming, such as the Eastern Mediterranean and Middle East, are projected to experience some of the most severe increases in heat-extreme frequency and in the emergence of previously unprecedented conditions, with warming rates in that region already exceeding the global mean and projected to continue doing so (Zittis et al., 2022). The compounding of heat with humidity raises the additional spectre of conditions approaching physiological limits for outdoor human activity in the hottest, most humid regions under high warming.

The attribution literature establishes that the human influence on observed heat extremes is already unambiguous and that projected future changes are a continuation of a detected trend rather than a novel projection (Clarke et al., 2022). This continuity between attributed past and projected future strengthens confidence in the heat projections specifically: the models that project future intensification are the same models shown to reproduce the observed, attributable intensification. The practical consequence is that heat-extreme projections are among the most robust and highest-confidence elements of the entire long-term projection enterprise.

### 6.2.3 Global and regional precipitation

**Global mean precipitation will increase with warming at a rate governed by the atmosphere's energy budget rather than its moisture content, producing a global sensitivity of order 1 to 3 percent per degree C, while atmospheric moisture — and therefore the intensity of heavy-precipitation extremes — rises faster, at close to the 7 percent per degree C dictated by thermodynamics (*high confidence*).** This distinction between the energetically constrained mean and the thermodynamically constrained extreme is central to the precipitation projections. The mean can increase only as fast as the atmosphere can radiate away the latent heat released by condensation, which limits it; the extremes, drawing on a moister atmosphere, can intensify much faster. The result is a projected world in which total precipitation rises modestly but is delivered increasingly in intense events separated by longer dry spells.

The spatial pattern of mean precipitation change follows robust large-scale organizing principles — wet regions and wet seasons generally getting wetter, subtropical dry zones generally getting drier and expanding poleward — superimposed on large regional uncertainty arising from circulation changes that models simulate less consistently than thermodynamics. Monsoon systems are projected to intensify in total rainfall while becoming more variable and, in some regions, delayed in onset. The high-latitude and equatorial wet zones are projected to see the clearest increases, while the subtropical margins face the clearest drying. Because circulation responses are the dominant source of regional precipitation uncertainty, the confidence in regional precipitation projections is systematically lower than the confidence in temperature projections.

**The co-occurrence of intensifying heavy precipitation and expanding drought is not a contradiction but a direct consequence of the same warming, and both will increase together over most land areas (*high confidence*).** A warmer atmosphere holds more water and evaporates it faster, so the same climate that delivers more intense downpours also dries soils more aggressively between them. Anthropogenic influence on the global characteristics of drought — its frequency, duration, and intensity — is already detectable in the observational record, providing an attributed baseline for the projected future expansion (Chiang et al., 2021). The intensification of flooding hazard follows from the precipitation-extreme signal: inequitable and rising flood exposure is projected across river and coastal systems as extreme rainfall intensifies and sea level rises (Wing et al., 2022).

### 6.2.4 Drought, aridity, and flash droughts

**The character of drought will shift toward faster onset and greater intensity, with flash droughts — rapid-onset events driven by evaporative demand — becoming more frequent across most land regions as warming raises atmospheric thirst (*high confidence*).** Flash droughts, which develop over weeks rather than seasons, are projected to increase in onset frequency globally under continued warming, with the transition from slow to flash drought a robust ensemble feature that steepens at higher warming levels (Yuan et al., 2023). The driver is the rising vapour-pressure deficit of a warmer atmosphere, which increases the rate at which soils and vegetation lose water even where precipitation is unchanged, so that drought can develop rapidly during a single hot, dry spell.

The megadrought that emerged across southwestern North America in the early twenty-first century exemplifies the compounding of natural variability with the anthropogenic aridification trend, and it has been characterized as the most severe multi-decadal drought in the region for over a millennium, with a substantial fraction of its severity attributable to human-caused warming (Williams et al., 2022). Such events are informative for projection because they reveal how the warming-driven increase in evaporative demand converts what would have been a moderate natural drought into an extreme one — a mechanism that projections indicate will operate with increasing force through the century.

### 6.2.5 Atmospheric and coupled circulation

**Large-scale atmospheric circulation will change in ways that are physically expected but regionally uncertain, including a poleward expansion of the subtropical dry zones, a poleward shift and possible intensification of the mid-latitude storm tracks, and altered behaviour of the major modes of internal variability (*medium-to-high confidence* in the direction of the large-scale shifts; *lower confidence* in regional detail).** The El Niño–Southern Oscillation, the dominant mode of interannual climate variability, is assessed to change in a warming climate, with projections indicating an increase in the amplitude of ENSO-related sea-surface-temperature and rainfall variability and in the frequency of extreme events at both ends of the cycle, even as the mean-state response remains less certain (Cai et al., 2021). Because ENSO teleconnections govern year-to-year climate risk across much of the tropics and subtropics, changes in its behaviour propagate into changes in the variability of drought, flood, and heat risk far from the equatorial Pacific.

The projected weakening of large-scale overturning circulations — atmospheric and oceanic — is a further robust element. In the atmosphere, the tropical overturning is projected to weaken as the mean precipitation increase lags the moisture increase. In the ocean, the meridional overturning circulation is assessed to weaken over the century under continued warming, a change with consequences for regional sea level, heat transport, and the ocean carbon and oxygen budgets that are taken up in the ocean-futures assessment below. The confidence in the sign of these circulation changes is higher than the confidence in their magnitude, which remains model-dependent.

## 6.3 Sea Level and Ocean Futures

### 6.3.1 Global mean sea level rise through 2100

**Global mean sea level will continue to rise throughout the twenty-first century under every assessed scenario, with the century-end rise scaling with warming and the well-constrained contributions from ocean thermal expansion and glacier melt dominating the central estimate (*high confidence*).** The physical decomposition of sea level rise separates a set of comparatively well-understood contributors — the thermal expansion of a warming ocean and the melt of the world's mountain glaciers — from the deeply uncertain contribution of the great ice sheets. The glacier contribution is among the best constrained: projections show that glacier mass loss through 2100 is a smooth function of peak global temperature, with the fraction of glacier mass lost and the number of glaciers that disappear entirely rising monotonically with warming, such that even the lowest warming levels commit the world to substantial and, for many small glaciers, complete loss (Rounce et al., 2023). Because these losses are essentially locked in for glaciers that fall below their equilibrium line, a large share of the twenty-first-century glacier contribution to sea level is already committed.

Thermal expansion is the second pillar of the central estimate and the most direct expression of ocean heat uptake. As the ocean accumulates the great majority of the planetary energy imbalance, its volume increases; this thermosteric contribution responds slowly, continues long after surface temperature stabilizes, and is effectively irreversible on human timescales, giving sea level a deep memory of the integrated warming rather than of its instantaneous value (*high confidence*). This slow, committed character is why sea level rise cannot be arrested by stabilizing temperature alone and why the sea-level projection must be framed over centuries, not decades.

The assessed likely range for century-end global mean sea level rise spans from roughly half a metre under stringent mitigation to close to a metre or more under high emissions, with the central estimates well constrained by the thermal-expansion and glacier contributions. The high-end tail, however, is governed by processes that the likely range does not fully capture, and it is to those that the assessment turns next.

### 6.3.2 Ice-sheet contributions and the high-end tail

**The dominant uncertainty in twenty-first-century and longer-term sea level rise is the behaviour of the marine-based sectors of the Antarctic Ice Sheet, whose potential instability governs a low-likelihood, high-impact tail that cannot be excluded and that widens sharply beyond 2100 (*medium confidence* in the magnitude; *high confidence* that the risk rises with warming).** The Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets contribute to sea level through surface melt and through the dynamic discharge of ice to the ocean, and it is the dynamic component — the acceleration, thinning, and retreat of marine-terminating outlet glaciers and ice shelves — that carries the deep uncertainty. Marine ice-sheet and ice-cliff instabilities, in which retreat of the grounding line into deepening bedrock basins becomes self-sustaining, provide a physical mechanism by which the Antarctic contribution could become both large and effectively unstoppable once initiated. The assessment treats this tail explicitly rather than omitting it, because a low-probability outcome with civilization-scale consequences is decision-relevant even when it is not the central estimate.

The distinction between the reversible atmospheric response and the irreversible ice-sheet response is central to the interpretation of overshoot pathways. A world that overshoots a temperature target and returns to it may commit to ice-sheet retreat during the overshoot that continues even after temperatures decline, because grounding-line retreat, once triggered, does not reverse when the forcing that triggered it is removed (*high confidence* in the asymmetry; *medium confidence* in the thresholds). This hysteresis means that peak warming, not final warming, may set the multi-century ice-sheet trajectory — a consideration that sharpens the physical case for limiting overshoot.

### 6.3.3 Sea level rise beyond 2100 and its commitment

**Global mean sea level rise does not stop in 2100; it continues for centuries to millennia, and the multi-century commitment scales with peak warming to reach magnitudes measured in metres, effectively reshaping the world's coastlines on a timescale that human infrastructure cannot outpace (*high confidence*).** The deep-time commitment arises because the two largest reservoirs — the deep ocean's heat content and the great ice sheets — respond on timescales of centuries to millennia. Even under strong mitigation, the thermal expansion already set in motion and the ice-sheet response already committed will continue to raise sea level for centuries. Under high warming, the marine-based Antarctic sectors could contribute multiple metres over subsequent centuries, and the ultimate commitment from sustained high temperatures is measured in many metres of eventual rise. The practical significance is that sea level rise is the archetypal committed, quasi-irreversible consequence of warming: the emissions of this century set a coastline trajectory for the next several.

### 6.3.4 Ocean heat, stratification, and marine heatwaves

**The ocean will continue to warm at the surface and at depth, will become more strongly stratified, and will experience marine heatwaves that are more frequent, more intense, and longer-lasting, with biological consequences that scale steeply with warming (*virtually certain* for the warming; *high confidence* for the marine-heatwave intensification).** The ocean absorbs the overwhelming majority of the excess heat trapped by greenhouse gases, and this heat uptake, concentrated near the surface, increases the vertical density gradient and suppresses the mixing that ventilates the interior and supplies nutrients to the sunlit layer. The result is a more stratified ocean in which surface warming is amplified and the transfer of heat, carbon, and oxygen to depth is impeded. High-resolution ocean reanalyses provide the observational foundation for characterizing this evolving state and for evaluating the models that project it forward (Jean-Michel et al., 2021).

Marine heatwaves — prolonged periods of anomalously high ocean temperature — are projected to intensify along every metric under continued warming, and their biological impacts are severe and increasingly well documented, spanning mass mortality of foundation species such as corals and kelp, geographic redistribution of mobile species, and disruption of fisheries and the human communities that depend on them (Smith et al., 2022). Because many marine organisms live close to their thermal limits, the biological response to marine heatwaves is strongly nonlinear in warming level, so that each increment of ocean warming disproportionately raises the risk of ecosystem-scale disruption. The compounding of chronic warming with acute heatwave events, and with acidification and deoxygenation, produces multi-stressor conditions that exceed the tolerance of many species and ecosystems.

### 6.3.5 Ocean acidification and deoxygenation

**Ocean acidification will continue in near-lockstep with atmospheric carbon dioxide, and ocean oxygen content will decline as warming reduces solubility and suppresses ventilation, together degrading habitat for a wide range of marine life (*virtually certain* for acidification; *high confidence* for deoxygenation).** Acidification is the most predictable of all the ocean's chemical responses because it is governed directly by the uptake of atmospheric carbon dioxide into surface waters, a process well described by carbonate chemistry; the surface-ocean pH decline therefore tracks the emission pathway with little of the uncertainty that attends the physical climate response. The carbon-budget assessments quantify the ocean carbon sink that drives this acidification, showing that the ocean continues to absorb a large and roughly constant fraction of anthropogenic emissions — a service to the climate that comes at the direct cost of the chemistry that calcifying organisms depend on (Friedlingstein et al., 2023).

Deoxygenation proceeds through two mechanisms that both strengthen with warming: warmer water holds less dissolved oxygen, and increased stratification reduces the downward mixing of oxygen-rich surface water into the interior. The expansion of oxygen-minimum zones compresses the viable habitat for oxygen-demanding species and interacts with warming and acidification to produce a triple stress on ocean ecosystems. The three stressors are not independent — they share the common driver of rising carbon dioxide and warming — so their impacts compound, and the ocean-ecosystem response to high-emission futures is correspondingly severe.

### 6.3.6 Overturning circulation

**The Atlantic meridional overturning circulation is assessed to weaken over the twenty-first century under continued warming, with consequences for regional sea level, heat transport, and marine ecosystems; an abrupt collapse before 2100 is not the assessed central outcome but cannot be excluded and constitutes a further high-impact tail risk (*medium confidence* in the weakening; *low confidence* in the likelihood of collapse).** The overturning circulation is driven in part by the formation of dense water in the high-latitude North Atlantic, a process that warming and freshwater input from ice melt both act to suppress. A weakening of this circulation redistributes heat and sea level regionally — raising sea level along some coasts, cooling the surface in the subpolar North Atlantic relative to the global trend — and alters the ocean's uptake of carbon and oxygen. The assessment treats the possibility of a more abrupt transition as a low-likelihood, high-impact outcome that, like the marine ice-sheet tail, warrants explicit consideration precisely because its consequences would be severe and largely irreversible on human timescales.

## 6.4 Cryosphere Futures

### 6.4.1 Mountain glaciers

**The world's mountain glaciers will lose a large fraction of their mass over the twenty-first century under every scenario, and the loss is a smooth, monotonic function of peak global temperature such that limiting warming directly limits glacier loss — every increase in temperature matters (*high confidence*).** Comprehensive global glacier modelling shows that even under the most stringent mitigation a substantial share of present-day glacier mass is committed to disappear, while under high warming the majority of glaciers — and the overwhelming majority of small glaciers — are lost by 2100 (Rounce et al., 2023). The number of glaciers lost is even more sensitive to warming than the mass lost, because small glaciers vanish entirely at low warming increments while the largest ice bodies persist in reduced form; the result is a landscape from which glaciers disappear region by region as warming climbs.

The consequences of glacier loss propagate through the hydrological cycle and human water security. Glaciers act as multi-year to multi-decadal buffers, storing winter precipitation as ice and releasing it as meltwater during the warm, dry season when demand is highest. As glaciers shrink, this buffering first increases — producing a transient "peak water" pulse of enhanced melt — and then declines irreversibly as the ice reservoir is depleted, leaving dry-season flows diminished and more variable. Because elevation-dependent warming exposes high-mountain cryospheres to warming that can exceed the global mean, the glacier response is amplified relative to what the global temperature signal alone would suggest (Pepin et al., 2022). The regions most dependent on glacier-fed rivers face a transition from abundant to scarce dry-season water within the century, with the timing of peak water and its subsequent decline set by the warming trajectory.

### 6.4.2 Snow cover and seasonal snowpack

**Seasonal snow cover will continue to decline in extent, duration, and mass across most of the Northern Hemisphere's snow-affected regions, shortening the snow season and shifting the timing of snowmelt earlier in the year (*high confidence*).** Snow is the most responsive component of the cryosphere because it exists close to the melting point for much of its life; modest warming therefore produces large changes in the fraction of precipitation that falls as snow rather than rain, in the date of peak snowpack, and in the timing of melt-driven streamflow. The projected shift toward earlier and more rain-dominated hydrographs alters flood risk, reservoir management, and dry-season water availability across snow-fed basins. The snow-albedo feedback that accompanies snow-cover loss is itself an amplifier of warming, reinforcing both polar amplification and elevation-dependent warming and helping to explain why high-latitude and high-elevation regions warm faster than the global mean (Rantanen et al., 2022) (Pepin et al., 2022).

### 6.4.3 Arctic sea ice

**Arctic sea ice will continue to decline in extent and thickness, and a practically ice-free Arctic Ocean in late summer is projected to occur before mid-century under most scenarios, with the frequency of ice-free conditions rising with warming (*high confidence*).** The loss of Arctic sea ice is one of the most visible and dynamically consequential elements of the projected cryosphere. As the highly reflective ice cover retreats, it exposes the dark ocean surface, which absorbs far more solar radiation; this ice-albedo feedback is a principal driver of the Arctic amplification that has already produced warming at close to four times the global rate and that projections carry forward through the century (Rantanen et al., 2022). The transition to seasonally ice-free conditions is projected to be effectively unavoidable under continued warming, though the exact timing depends on scenario and on internal variability, and the summer-ice loss is more nearly reversible than the ice-sheet changes because thin seasonal ice can regrow if temperatures decline.

The consequences of Arctic sea-ice loss extend beyond the polar region. The reduction in the equator-to-pole temperature gradient alters the behaviour of the mid-latitude jet stream and storm tracks, with proposed but still uncertain effects on the persistence and character of mid-latitude weather extremes. The opening of the Arctic Ocean also reshapes regional ecosystems, carbon fluxes, and human access, and it accelerates the warming of coastal permafrost. The assessment holds high confidence in the continued decline and in the eventual attainment of ice-free summers; the confidence in the remote atmospheric consequences is lower.

### 6.4.4 Antarctic sea ice and the Southern Ocean

**Antarctic sea ice, historically more variable and less clearly trending than its Arctic counterpart, is assessed with lower confidence, but the Southern Ocean's continued heat and carbon uptake and its role in bottom-water formation make it a critical and evolving component of the long-term projection (*medium confidence*).** The Southern Ocean absorbs a disproportionate share of the ocean's heat and anthropogenic carbon uptake, and the formation of dense Antarctic bottom water is a driver of the global overturning circulation. Changes in Antarctic sea ice, in the freshwater balance from ice-shelf melt, and in the wind-driven circulation of the Southern Ocean therefore feed back on the global ocean's capacity to take up heat and carbon. High-resolution ocean and sea-ice reanalyses are the observational basis for tracking this evolving state and for evaluating the coupled models that project the Southern Ocean forward (Jean-Michel et al., 2021). The lower confidence in Antarctic sea-ice projection reflects both the greater complexity of the Southern Ocean's dynamics and the shorter, more variable observational record against which models are evaluated.

### 6.4.5 Ice sheets and their long-term trajectory

**The Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets will continue to lose mass through the century and for centuries beyond, and their long-term trajectories carry the deepest uncertainty and the largest potential consequences in the entire projection, with grounding-line retreat and ice-shelf loss providing mechanisms for large, effectively irreversible contributions to sea level (*high confidence* in continued loss; *medium confidence* in the magnitude and timing of dynamic instabilities).** Greenland loses mass predominantly through surface melt, which increases smoothly with warming and which, above a threshold of sustained temperature, commits the ice sheet to long-term, near-total decline as the surface lowers into warmer air — a slow but effectively irreversible response measured in millennia. Antarctica loses mass predominantly through dynamic discharge, which is governed by the stability of ice shelves and grounding lines and which is capable of far more abrupt and less predictable behaviour. The marine-based sectors, grounded on bedrock that deepens inland, are susceptible to self-sustaining retreat once the grounding line is displaced, and this mechanism underlies the high-end sea-level tail assessed in the ocean-futures section.

The reversibility asymmetry between ice sheets and the atmosphere is the single most important interpretive point for long-term projections. Surface air temperature responds within decades to changes in cumulative emissions and is, for the atmosphere, largely reversible; the ice sheets respond over centuries to millennia and, once committed to retreat, do not recover on any timescale relevant to human societies (*high confidence*). This means that the emissions of the coming decades set an ice-sheet and sea-level trajectory that will play out over the coming centuries regardless of subsequent mitigation, and that overshoot pathways carry a particular risk of triggering commitments during the peak-warming phase that persist after temperatures decline.

### 6.4.6 Permafrost and the frozen-carbon feedback

**Permafrost will thaw progressively as high-latitude and high-elevation regions warm, releasing carbon dioxide and methane in a positive feedback that amplifies warming and that projections treat as an additional, partly irreversible source not fully captured in the emission scenarios themselves (*high confidence* that thaw and emission occur; *medium confidence* in the magnitude).** Permafrost soils store a vast quantity of ancient organic carbon, and as they thaw this carbon becomes available to microbial decomposition, releasing greenhouse gases that add to the atmospheric burden and drive further warming. Because Arctic amplification exposes permafrost regions to warming far exceeding the global mean, the thaw proceeds faster than the global temperature trajectory alone would imply (Rantanen et al., 2022). The feedback is partly irreversible on human timescales: carbon released from thawed permafrost does not return to the frozen reservoir when temperatures stabilize, so the permafrost contribution accumulates as a function of the integrated warming.

The permafrost feedback is one of several ways in which the real-world carbon cycle may respond more strongly to warming than the idealized scenarios assume, effectively narrowing the remaining carbon budget for any temperature target. The carbon-budget assessments that partition emissions among atmosphere, ocean, and land quantify the observed behaviour of these sinks and sources, and they underscore that the land carbon response — of which permafrost is one component — is both large and uncertain, contributing substantially to the year-to-year and decade-to-decade variability of the atmospheric growth rate (Friedlingstein et al., 2023). The assessment therefore treats the frozen-carbon feedback as a source of upward risk to the projected warming for any given anthropogenic emission pathway.

## 6.5 The Land Carbon Cycle and Terrestrial Ecosystems

### 6.5.1 The land carbon sink and its stability

**The terrestrial biosphere currently absorbs roughly a quarter to a third of anthropogenic carbon dioxide emissions, but the strength and stability of this sink under continued warming is uncertain and is a major source of the spread in projected atmospheric concentrations and warming for a given emission pathway (*high confidence* in the present sink; *medium confidence* in its future evolution).** The annual global carbon budget quantifies the partitioning of emissions among the atmosphere, ocean, and land, and it shows that the land sink, while large, is also the most variable component from year to year, driven by the response of ecosystem productivity and respiration to temperature, moisture, and carbon dioxide itself (Friedlingstein et al., 2023). The fertilizing effect of rising carbon dioxide enhances photosynthesis and has strengthened the sink historically, but this effect is expected to saturate, and it competes against the warming-driven increase in respiration and against the moisture stress that limits productivity in a drying, more variable climate.

The dominant control on the interannual variability of the land sink is the coupling between soil moisture and the atmosphere. Variations in terrestrial water availability drive the year-to-year swings in carbon uptake, so that dry years — increasingly common under the projected expansion of drought and evaporative demand — depress the land sink and allow a larger fraction of emissions to remain in the atmosphere (Humphrey et al., 2021). This mechanism links the hydrological projections directly to the carbon-cycle projections: a climate that dries soils and intensifies drought is also a climate in which the land's capacity to buffer emissions is diminished, a positive feedback that adds upward risk to the warming projection. The updated carbon-budget accounting continues to show the land sink's sensitivity to climate variability and the persistence of large uncertainties in its future trajectory (Friedlingstein et al., 2025).

### 6.5.2 Forests, drought, and tree mortality

**Forests face rising risk of drought- and heat-driven mortality as warming raises vapour-pressure deficit and intensifies drought, and widespread tree die-off would convert forest carbon sinks into sources, adding to atmospheric carbon dioxide (*high confidence* that mortality risk rises; *medium confidence* in the magnitude of the carbon consequence).** A global synthesis of tree-mortality observations reveals a consistent "hotter-drought" fingerprint: tree death is driven not by drought alone but by the combination of drought with elevated temperature, which increases the atmospheric demand for water and pushes trees past the physiological limits that govern their survival (Hammond et al., 2022). The mechanisms of this mortality — hydraulic failure as water columns in the xylem break under tension, and carbon starvation as stressed trees close their stomata and deplete their reserves — are increasingly well understood and are exacerbated by the rising vapour-pressure deficit that accompanies warming (McDowell et al., 2022). Because these mechanisms scale with temperature as well as with precipitation deficit, warming intensifies mortality risk even where rainfall is unchanged.

The consequences for the carbon cycle are significant. Forests are major carbon reservoirs and sinks, and large-scale mortality releases stored carbon while removing future uptake capacity, a transition that can convert a regional sink into a source. The interaction of drought-driven mortality with fire and with insect outbreaks compounds the risk, as stressed and dead trees provide fuel and become vulnerable to pests. The assessment treats accelerating tree mortality as both an ecosystem impact and a carbon-cycle feedback that adds to the upward risk on projected warming.

### 6.5.3 Wildfire

**Fire regimes will intensify in many regions as warming lengthens fire seasons, dries fuels, and increases the frequency of extreme fire weather, with the largest and most extreme fires becoming more probable under continued warming (*high confidence*).** The record-breaking fires that swept southeastern Australia at the turn of the decade illustrate the connection between climate change and extreme fire: the burned area and the conditions that produced these fires were linked to the warming- and variability-driven increase in fire-weather severity, and the multi-decadal trend in forest burned area across the continent has been attributed in part to climate change (Abram et al., 2021) (Canadell et al., 2021). The physical chain is direct — higher temperatures and elevated vapour-pressure deficit dry the vegetation that serves as fuel, longer and hotter dry seasons extend the window of flammability, and the resulting fires burn hotter and spread faster.

The carbon and ecosystem consequences of intensifying fire regimes reinforce the warming they arise from. Fire releases stored carbon rapidly, and where fires become frequent enough to prevent forest recovery they can drive a persistent transition from forest to non-forest, locking in a loss of carbon-storage capacity. The interaction of fire with drought-driven mortality and with the drying land carbon sink forms a set of mutually reinforcing terrestrial feedbacks that, under high warming, add materially to the risk that the land carbon sink weakens or reverses.

### 6.5.4 The Amazon and other regional forest systems

**The Amazon forest system is approaching conditions under which self-reinforcing dieback could occur in parts of the basin, driven by the combination of warming, drought, deforestation, and fire, and its degradation is already substantial and accelerating (*medium confidence* in the threshold; *high confidence* that the risk rises with warming and land-use pressure).** Analysis of the drivers and impacts of Amazon forest degradation shows that a large and growing fraction of the forest is affected by edge effects, selective logging, fire, and drought, degrading its structure and carbon storage even where outright deforestation has not occurred (Lapola et al., 2023). The concern for long-term projection is that these stressors interact with the climate signal to push parts of the system toward a critical transition, in which forest loss reduces the moisture recycling that sustains regional rainfall, drying the climate further and driving additional forest loss in a self-perpetuating loop.

The assessment of critical transitions in the Amazon identifies the combinations of warming, rainfall reduction, dry-season length, and deforestation under which such a transition becomes plausible, and it locates a substantial fraction of the forest within reach of these thresholds by mid-century under continued pressure (Flores et al., 2024). The potential transition of the Amazon from forest to a degraded, savanna-like or fire-dominated state is treated as one of the more consequential regional tipping risks in the Earth system, both for the carbon it would release and for the biodiversity and regional climate it would transform.

### 6.5.5 Agriculture, water, and food systems

**Climate impacts on agriculture will emerge earlier and be more severe than earlier crop-model generations projected, with warming, shifting precipitation, drought, and soil degradation reducing yields of major staples in many regions even as some high-latitude areas see gains (*high confidence* that impacts intensify with warming; *medium confidence* in regional magnitudes).** The newest generation of coupled climate and crop models projects that the impacts of climate change on global agriculture emerge earlier in the century and are larger than previously estimated, with major breadbasket regions facing yield declines in staple crops under continued warming (Jägermeyr et al., 2021). The drivers are multiple and compounding: direct heat stress on crops during sensitive growth stages, changes in the amount and timing of precipitation, the expansion and intensification of drought, and the degradation of the soil and water resources on which agriculture depends.

Soil and water resources are themselves projected to degrade under warming in ways that amplify the direct climate impacts on crops. Primary soil salinization is projected to expand across large areas of the world's drylands over the century as changing precipitation and rising evaporative demand concentrate salts in the root zone, reducing the productivity of affected land (Hassani et al., 2021). Groundwater, the buffer on which irrigated agriculture increasingly depends during drought, faces rising demand precisely as recharge becomes more variable, making sustainable groundwater management central to a resilient water and food future (Scanlon et al., 2023). The broader environmental footprint of agriculture and its exposure to climate change form a two-way interaction, in which a changing climate degrades agricultural systems and agricultural expansion and intensification contribute to the environmental pressures that drive further change (Yang et al., 2024). The net consequence for long-term projection is that the agricultural and water-resource impacts of warming are among the most societally consequential, scale with warming level, and are compounded by the degradation of the land and water resources that agriculture depends upon.

## 6.6 Tipping Elements, Committed Change, and Deep-Time Projection

### 6.6.1 Large-scale singular events and the risk of crossing thresholds

**Several components of the Earth system carry a non-negligible probability of crossing self-reinforcing thresholds under sustained warming, and the probability of such crossings rises with global warming level, making the avoidance of high peak warming the primary lever for reducing this class of risk (*medium confidence* in the specific thresholds; *high confidence* that the aggregate risk increases with warming).** The tipping elements assessed here include the marine-based sectors of the Antarctic Ice Sheet, the Greenland Ice Sheet, the Atlantic overturning circulation, and the Amazon forest system, each of which possesses a mechanism by which change, once initiated, becomes self-sustaining and effectively irreversible on human timescales. The Amazon analysis identifies the combinations of warming and land-use pressure under which a critical transition of the forest becomes plausible within the century (Flores et al., 2024), while the ice-sheet and circulation risks are discussed in the ocean and cryosphere sections above. What unites these elements is the presence of positive feedbacks strong enough to carry the system to a new state even after the forcing that triggered the transition is removed.

The appropriate treatment of these low-likelihood, high-impact outcomes is to assess them explicitly rather than to exclude them because they lie in the tail of the distribution. A tail outcome with civilization-scale consequences is decision-relevant in proportion to its impact as well as its probability, and omitting it from the assessment would understate the risk that the projections are meant to convey. The assessment therefore reports both the central, likely-range projections and the physically grounded tails — the marine ice-sheet contribution to sea level, the possibility of an abrupt overturning transition, the potential for Amazon dieback — as integral parts of the projection rather than as caveats appended to it.

### 6.6.2 Catastrophic and worst-case scenarios

**The upper tail of climate risk, including the possibility of warming substantially exceeding central projections through strong carbon-cycle and other feedbacks, and the societal consequences of such warming, has been comparatively neglected and warrants dedicated assessment (*high confidence* that the tail is decision-relevant; *low confidence* in the probability of the most extreme outcomes).** A systematic examination of catastrophic climate-change scenarios argues that the mechanisms by which high-end warming could arise — including strong carbon-cycle feedbacks such as permafrost thaw, weakened land and ocean sinks, and the compounding of multiple tipping elements — and the pathways by which such warming could translate into cascading societal failure have received insufficient scientific attention relative to their potential importance (Kemp et al., 2022). The value of assessing these scenarios is not to assign them a high probability but to characterize the mechanisms, identify the conditions under which they become more likely, and inform the management of a risk whose magnitude, however improbable, is extreme.

This framing connects the physical-science tail to the human consequences that motivate the assessment. The compounding of climate hazards with the vulnerabilities of interconnected human and natural systems can amplify a given physical change into a larger societal impact, and the interaction of the climate and biodiversity crises is itself a threat multiplier that neither crisis captures in isolation (Pörtner et al., 2023). The assessment of catastrophic outcomes is therefore an assessment of coupled human–Earth-system risk, in which the physical tail and the societal amplification must be considered together.

### 6.6.3 The Earth system in a planetary-boundaries frame

**Long-term climate projection sits within a broader picture of multiple, interacting Earth-system pressures, several of which have already been pushed beyond the boundaries associated with a stable, Holocene-like state, so that climate change is one of a set of transgressed limits rather than an isolated hazard (*high confidence* in the transgression of multiple boundaries; assessment of the boundaries themselves carries *medium confidence*).** The planetary-boundaries analysis finds that a majority of the assessed Earth-system boundaries — spanning climate, biosphere integrity, freshwater, biogeochemical flows, and land-system change — have now been transgressed, indicating that the safe operating space for humanity has been substantially eroded (Richardson et al., 2023). Extending this analysis to incorporate justice considerations yields safe and just boundaries that are, for several dimensions, already exceeded, underscoring that the climate projections assessed here unfold against a backdrop of concurrent and interacting environmental change (Rockström et al., 2023).

This framing matters for projection because the components of the Earth system do not respond to warming in isolation. Land-system change, freshwater depletion, biosphere degradation, and biogeochemical disruption interact with the climate response, sometimes amplifying it — as when deforestation and drought reinforce one another in the Amazon, or when land-use change and soil degradation compound the agricultural impacts of warming. The magnitude of land-system change is itself larger than earlier estimates suggested, with global land-use change over recent decades affecting a far greater area than previously recognized, reshaping the land surface that mediates the carbon, water, and energy exchanges central to the climate projection (Winkler et al., 2021). The assessment therefore situates climate projection within the coupled dynamics of a multiply stressed Earth system rather than treating it as a single-variable forecast.

### 6.6.4 Committed change and the meaning of stabilization

**Even if global temperature were stabilized, large parts of the Earth system would continue to change for centuries to millennia because of the slow response of the deep ocean and the ice sheets, so that "stabilization" of surface temperature does not imply stabilization of sea level, ice mass, or the deep ocean (*high confidence*).** The concept of committed change is central to interpreting long-term projections correctly. Surface air temperature responds to cumulative emissions on a timescale of decades and is, for the atmosphere, largely reversible; but the quantities that integrate the warming over time — ocean heat content, thermosteric sea level, ice-sheet mass, permafrost carbon — carry a memory of the warming that persists long after the surface stabilizes. A world that holds temperature constant at any level is a world in which sea level continues to rise, ice sheets continue to lose mass, and the deep ocean continues to warm and acidify, for centuries. This committed change is the reason the sea-level and ice-sheet projections extend to millennial timescales and the reason peak warming, rather than any subsequent stabilized level, governs much of the long-term outcome.

The corollary for overshoot pathways is that the reversibility of surface temperature does not confer reversibility on the committed components. Bringing temperature back down after an overshoot through net-negative emissions reverses the atmospheric warming but does not reverse the sea-level rise, the ice-sheet retreat, or the ecosystem transitions committed during the peak (*high confidence* in the asymmetry). The slow-responding components thus impose a penalty on overshoot that the fast-responding atmosphere does not reveal, and they make the limitation of peak warming — not merely the eventual return to a target — the physically meaningful objective for avoiding the largest long-term consequences.

### 6.6.5 Projection beyond 2100

**The changes projected for 2100 are a waypoint, not an endpoint; under continued high emissions the Earth system continues to warm and transform well beyond the century, and even under strong mitigation the committed changes in sea level and the cryosphere continue to unfold for centuries to millennia (*high confidence*).** The scenario framework that underpins the century-scale projections extends, in its extended and idealized forms, to multi-century horizons, and the coupled models run under these extensions show that the divergence between high- and low-emission futures widens dramatically beyond 2100 (Tebaldi et al., 2021). Under sustained high emissions the warming continues to climb, the ice sheets move further into self-sustaining decline, and sea level rises by many metres over subsequent centuries; under strong mitigation the surface temperature stabilizes but the committed sea-level and ice-sheet changes continue. The deep-time projection therefore reinforces the central message of the warming-level framing: the emissions of the coming decades set a trajectory whose consequences extend far beyond the lives of those who emit, and the difference between emission pathways, modest at 2100, becomes vast over the centuries that follow.

## 6.7 Model Evaluation, Constrained Projections, and Confidence

### 6.7.1 The evidentiary basis for confidence in projection

**Confidence in long-term projections is built from the convergence of multiple, partly independent lines of evidence — the demonstrated skill of process-based models, the physical understanding of the mechanisms, the attribution of observed change to the same drivers that force the projections, and the narrowing of ranges by observational constraints — rather than from any single model or method (*high confidence*).** The strongest projections are those for which all of these lines align. Extreme heat is the clearest example: the models that project future intensification reproduce the observed climatology, the physical mechanism is transparent, the observed intensification is attributed to human influence, and the projected future is a continuation of the attributed trend (Clarke et al., 2022). Where the lines of evidence align in this way, confidence is high even for large projected changes; where they diverge — as in the regional detail of precipitation and circulation, which depends on less well-constrained dynamical responses — confidence is correspondingly lower. The calibrated-language framework used throughout this chapter is the vehicle for communicating this graded confidence honestly.

Model evaluation against observations is the foundation of this evidentiary structure. The coupled models are assessed against reanalyses and observational datasets for their representation of the mean climate, the seasonal cycle, the modes of internal variability, and the historical trend and its spatial fingerprint. High-resolution ocean and coupled reanalyses provide the reference state for evaluating the ocean components that dominate heat and carbon uptake and that govern the committed changes in sea level (Jean-Michel et al., 2021). A model that reproduces the observed mean state and variability, and that reproduces the observed historical change for the right physical reasons, earns confidence in its projection; a model with large biases in the relevant processes is downweighted. This evaluation is not a pass–fail gate but a continuous weighting of the ensemble by demonstrated fidelity to the aspects of the climate most relevant to each projected quantity.

### 6.7.2 Observational constraints and emergent constraints

**Observationally based constraints, including emergent constraints that exploit ensemble-wide relationships between an observable present-day quantity and an uncertain future response, meaningfully narrow the projected ranges relative to the raw ensemble spread, though they must be validated against the underlying mechanism to guard against spurious statistical relationships (*high confidence* that the ensemble of constraints reduces the effective spread; *medium confidence* in any single constraint).** The logic of an emergent constraint is that if the models disagree about the future but agree that the future response is tied to some feature of the present climate that can be measured, then measuring that feature constrains the future. Applied to carbon-cycle feedbacks, snow-albedo feedback, cloud responses, and regional hydroclimate, these methods have tightened projected ranges and, in some cases, shifted central estimates. The danger they carry is that a correlation across the ensemble may be an artifact of shared model structure rather than a genuine physical relationship; the discipline that guards against this is the requirement that every constraint be traceable to a physical mechanism, not merely to a statistical fit.

The carbon cycle is a domain where observational constraint is especially valuable because the carbon-climate feedbacks are both large and uncertain. The soil-moisture–atmosphere coupling that dominates the interannual variability of land carbon uptake is an observable, constrainable process, and its representation is now a standard benchmark against which the land-surface components of the models are evaluated and weighted (Humphrey et al., 2021). The annual carbon-budget assessments provide the observational time series of the atmospheric growth rate and the sink partitioning against which the coupled carbon-cycle models are tested, tightening the link between a given emission pathway and the resulting atmospheric concentration and warming (Friedlingstein et al., 2023) (Friedlingstein et al., 2025). The net effect of the constraint enterprise is a projected range that is narrower and better justified than the unweighted ensemble would provide, without pretending to a precision the physics does not support.

### 6.7.3 Machine learning in the projection enterprise

**Machine-learning weather and climate emulators have matured rapidly and now complement process-based models for specific tasks — medium-range forecasting, downscaling, large-ensemble generation, and the acceleration of scenario exploration — but they do not replace physical models for projecting the response to novel forcing, which by construction lies outside their training distribution (*high confidence*).** Data-driven global weather models trained on decades of reanalysis now match or exceed the accuracy of established physics-based forecasting systems at a small fraction of the computational cost, a development that has reshaped operational weather prediction and opened new possibilities for climate applications (Bi et al., 2023). For long-term projection their value lies in efficiency: they can generate the large initial-condition ensembles needed to separate the forced signal from internal variability, downscale coarse projections to the regional scale where impacts are felt, and rapidly explore the space of scenarios, all far more cheaply than running the full coupled models.

The essential limitation is that these emulators learn the statistics of the climate they are trained on and cannot be assumed to extrapolate reliably to forcing regimes outside that training range — precisely the regimes that high-emission projections explore. A model that has never seen a 4-degree-C world in its training data has no guaranteed basis for simulating one. For this reason the process-based general circulation models, grounded in the conservation laws of physics, remain the authority on the response to novel forcing, and the machine-learning emulators serve as accelerators and downscalers within the envelope the physical models define. The productive combination is a hybrid one, in which physical models anchor the response to forcing and data-driven methods extend, downscale, and sample it efficiently. The assessment treats this hybridization as a promising and rapidly evolving direction rather than as a mature replacement for the physical modelling that remains the backbone of projection.

### 6.7.4 Synthesis

**The long-term global projection that emerges from this assessment is robust in its central messages and honest about its uncertainties: warming continues until emissions reach net zero, the level reached is set by cumulative carbon dioxide, every increment of warming intensifies hazards across the atmosphere, ocean, cryosphere, and biosphere, and a set of slow-responding and potentially threshold-crossing components commit the Earth system to changes that extend for centuries beyond 2100 (*high confidence*).** The most robust, highest-confidence elements are the continuation of warming to mid-century, the dominance of cumulative emissions in setting peak warming, the amplification of warming in the polar and high-mountain regions, the intensification of heat extremes, the rise in the intensity of heavy precipitation alongside the expansion of drought, the continued rise of sea level, and the continued warming, acidification, deoxygenation, and marine-heatwave intensification of the ocean. These conclusions rest on the convergence of model skill, physical understanding, attribution of observed change, and observational constraint, and they are stated with high confidence.

The deepest uncertainties are concentrated in a small number of high-consequence components: the dynamic response of the marine-based Antarctic Ice Sheet and the resulting high-end tail of sea level, the possibility of an abrupt weakening of the Atlantic overturning circulation, the strength and stability of the land and ocean carbon sinks and the associated carbon-cycle feedbacks, the risk of self-reinforcing dieback in the Amazon, and the regional detail of precipitation and circulation change. These are not reasons to doubt the central projection; they are the specific quantities where the assessment reports lower confidence and, for the tail risks, where it deliberately assesses low-likelihood, high-impact outcomes rather than omitting them. The organizing insight of the chapter is that warming-level framing renders the fast-responding hazards robust to scenario uncertainty while the slow-responding, committed components make peak warming the decisive variable for the long-term outcome. Taken together, the projections establish that the choices of the coming decades set the trajectory of the Earth system for centuries, and that limiting cumulative emissions and peak warming is the single most effective lever for limiting the full range of assessed long-term change (Rounce et al., 2023) (Tebaldi et al., 2021).

## 6.8 Compound Hazards, Cascading Risk, and the Continuity of Projection

### 6.8.1 Compound and concurrent extremes

**The most consequential climate risks of the coming century arise not from single hazards in isolation but from the compounding and concurrence of multiple hazards in space and time, and the probability of such compound events rises faster with warming than the probability of any single component (*high confidence*).** A warming climate raises the joint probability that heat, drought, and fire occur together, that extreme precipitation and high sea level coincide to produce compound coastal-and-fluvial flooding, and that successive extremes strike the same region before it can recover. The intensification of heat extremes and the expansion of drought, both assessed above, are physically linked through the shared driver of rising temperature and evaporative demand, so that their co-occurrence is not a coincidence to be estimated from independent probabilities but a coupled response to be projected together. The flood hazard illustrates the compounding of drivers: inequitable and rising flood exposure is projected as extreme precipitation intensifies and sea level rises, with the two contributions combining along low-lying coasts (Wing et al., 2022).

The assessment of compound risk requires moving beyond univariate projection to the joint distribution of hazards, and it is in this joint distribution that the steepest nonlinearities — and therefore the sharpest sensitivities to warming level — reside. Because many systems, human and natural, are resilient to individual shocks but vulnerable to their coincidence or succession, the compound framing often reveals risks that a hazard-by-hazard assessment would miss. The projected increase in the amplitude and extreme-event frequency of the El Niño–Southern Oscillation is significant in this light, because ENSO orchestrates the concurrence of drought, flood, heat, and fire risk across widely separated regions, so that a change in its behaviour propagates into a change in the correlation structure of global climate risk (Cai et al., 2021).

### 6.8.2 Cascading and systemic risk

**Climate hazards can cascade through interconnected human and natural systems, so that a physical change of modest magnitude can propagate into a large societal impact through the coupling of food, water, health, ecological, and economic systems (*high confidence* that cascading amplification occurs; *medium confidence* in its magnitude for any specific chain).** The interaction of the climate and biodiversity crises is a paradigmatic cascade: climate change degrades ecosystems, degraded ecosystems lose the capacity to buffer climate impacts and to provide the services on which human societies depend, and the two crises reinforce one another as coupled threats that neither addresses in isolation (Pörtner et al., 2023). Climate change also acts as an aggravating factor across a majority of known human pathogenic diseases, expanding the geographic and seasonal windows of transmission and compounding the direct impacts of heat, drought, and extreme events on human health (Mora et al., 2022). These cascades link the physical projections of this chapter to the human consequences that give them their significance.

The energy and infrastructure systems on which adaptation depends are themselves exposed to the changing climate, so that the systems meant to buffer climate impacts are subject to the hazards they must buffer. The cost, environmental performance, and resilience of renewable-energy systems are affected by a changing climate through altered wind, solar, and hydrological resources and through exposure to extreme events, a coupling that must be accounted for in projecting the resilience of the low-carbon transition itself (Osman et al., 2022). The systemic framing underscores that long-term projection is not merely a forecast of physical variables but an input to the assessment of coupled human–Earth-system risk, in which physical hazards, ecological degradation, and societal vulnerability combine.

### 6.8.3 The continuity from near-term to long-term

**Long-term projections are continuous with the near-term changes already observed and attributed, so that the century-scale future is a physically grounded extrapolation of a detected present rather than a speculative departure from it (*high confidence*).** The strongest single argument for confidence in long-term projection is this continuity. The Arctic is already warming at close to four times the global rate, exactly as amplification mechanisms predict and projections extend (Rantanen et al., 2022). Drought characteristics already bear a detectable human fingerprint (Chiang et al., 2021), heat extremes are already attributably intensified (Clarke et al., 2022), glaciers are already in committed decline (Rounce et al., 2023), and the ocean is already warming, acidifying, and experiencing intensifying marine heatwaves (Smith et al., 2022). The long-term projections do not posit new mechanisms; they extend, under specified forcing, the mechanisms whose signatures are already present in the observational record and attributed to human influence.

This continuity has a practical corollary for the interpretation of the projections. Because the projected future is an extension of an attributed present, the uncertainties in the projections are uncertainties of magnitude and timing, not of direction or mechanism, for the high-confidence conclusions. The direction of change — more warming until net zero, higher heat extremes, more intense heavy precipitation, expanding drought, rising sea level, a warming and acidifying ocean — is established by the convergence of attributed observation, physical understanding, and model projection. It is against this settled direction that the remaining uncertainties in magnitude, in regional detail, and in the timing and likelihood of threshold crossings should be understood: as the frontier of the assessment, not as a reason to doubt its foundation. The overarching conclusion of this chapter is that the choices governing cumulative emissions over the coming decades will determine, with high confidence in direction and quantified uncertainty in magnitude, the trajectory of the global climate system through 2100 and, through the committed and threshold-sensitive components, for the centuries and millennia beyond (Tebaldi et al., 2021) (Friedlingstein et al., 2025).

### 6.8.4 Representative regional storylines

**Regional storylines make the global projections concrete by tracing how the robust global signals combine with regional geography and vulnerability to produce distinct, high-consequence futures, and they illustrate that the same global warming level is experienced very differently across regions (*high confidence*).** The Eastern Mediterranean and Middle East exemplify a hot-and-drying storyline: a region already warming faster than the global mean, projected to experience among the most severe increases in heat-extreme frequency and the emergence of previously unprecedented conditions, superimposed on a drying trend and acute water scarcity (Zittis et al., 2022). Southwestern North America exemplifies an aridification storyline in which the warming-driven rise in evaporative demand converts natural drought variability into extreme, multi-decadal megadrought, drawing down the groundwater and surface-water reserves on which the region depends (Williams et al., 2022) (Scanlon et al., 2023).

The Amazon basin exemplifies a threshold-risk storyline, in which warming, drought, deforestation, and fire interact to push a globally significant carbon reservoir and biodiversity stronghold toward a possible self-reinforcing transition, with the degradation already substantial and the critical thresholds within reach under continued pressure (Lapola et al., 2023) (Flores et al., 2024). The high-mountain regions exemplify a water-tower storyline, in which elevation-dependent warming accelerates the loss of the glaciers and snowpack that buffer dry-season flow, producing a transient pulse of enhanced melt followed by an irreversible decline in water availability for the populations downstream (Pepin et al., 2022) (Rounce et al., 2023). These storylines are not exhaustive, but they demonstrate the central lesson of regional projection: the global signals assessed in this chapter are robust, but their human meaning is set by how they combine with the geography, hydrology, ecology, and vulnerability of each place. The global projection provides the forcing; the regional storyline provides the consequence.

## 6.9 Key Uncertainties and Research Priorities

**The assessment identifies a compact set of key uncertainties whose resolution would most improve long-term projection, and these define the research priorities for the coming assessment cycle (*high confidence* that these are the leading uncertainties).** First among them is the dynamic response of the marine-based Antarctic Ice Sheet, which governs the high-end tail of sea-level rise and whose thresholds and timescales remain poorly constrained; narrowing this uncertainty is the single most valuable target for sharpening the long-term sea-level projection (Rounce et al., 2023). Second is the strength and stability of the land and ocean carbon sinks under continued warming, including the carbon-cycle feedbacks — permafrost thaw, soil-moisture-limited productivity, drought- and fire-driven forest loss — that determine how much of a given emission pathway remains in the atmosphere; the annual carbon-budget accounting provides the observational spine for this effort, but the future trajectory of the sinks remains a leading source of spread in projected warming (Friedlingstein et al., 2023) (Humphrey et al., 2021).

Third is the likelihood and character of abrupt transitions in the Atlantic overturning circulation and in threshold-sensitive biosphere components such as the Amazon, where the physical possibility of self-reinforcing change is established but the thresholds and probabilities carry deep uncertainty (Flores et al., 2024). Fourth is the regional detail of precipitation and atmospheric circulation change, which depends on dynamical responses that models simulate less consistently than thermodynamics and which is the primary limit on the confidence of regional projections. Fifth is the changing behaviour of the modes of internal variability, particularly the El Niño–Southern Oscillation, whose response shapes the correlation structure of global climate risk (Cai et al., 2021). Progress on these five fronts — through improved process representation, observational constraint, higher-resolution and hybrid modelling, and paleoclimate evidence on threshold behaviour — would most tighten the long-term projections while leaving intact their high-confidence central messages.
