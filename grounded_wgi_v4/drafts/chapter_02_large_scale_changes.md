# Chapter 2: Large-Scale Changes in the Climate System and Their Causes

## Executive Summary

**The climate system as a whole has continued to warm, and the imprint of human influence is now detectable across every major component — atmosphere, ocean, cryosphere and biosphere — with multiple independent lines of evidence converging on the same conclusion (*virtually certain*).** Global-scale indicators of change have strengthened since the previous assessment cycle, and the pattern of change across the components is physically consistent with the accumulation of energy in the Earth system driven by rising atmospheric greenhouse gas concentrations (*high confidence*).

**Atmospheric concentrations of carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide have continued to rise, and the growth of the airborne carbon dioxide burden tracks the sum of fossil and land-use emissions closely enough that the anthropogenic origin of the increase is beyond scientific dispute (*virtually certain*).** The continuously updated global carbon budget shows that fossil carbon dioxide emissions and the resulting atmospheric growth rate remained near record levels through the most recent budget years (Friedlingstein et al., 2022; Friedlingstein et al., 2023; Friedlingstein et al., 2025).

**Surface warming has been geographically pervasive but strongly amplified at high northern latitudes, where the Arctic has warmed nearly four times faster than the global mean since 1979 (*high confidence*).** This Arctic amplification is now well quantified from multiple observational datasets and exceeds the value reproduced by many climate models, indicating that the polar response to forcing may be systematically underestimated in the model ensemble (Rantanen et al., 2022).

**Mountain regions across the world show elevation-dependent warming and rapid loss of ice, integrating change across the atmosphere and cryosphere (*high confidence*).** High Mountain Asian glaciers have thinned and retreated over the multi-decadal satellite record (Bhattacharya et al., 2021), and the mountain cryosphere more broadly is warming in patterns that vary systematically with elevation (Pepin et al., 2022).

**The ocean has warmed, and marine heatwaves have become more frequent, more intense and longer-lasting, with pervasive biological consequences (*high confidence*).** The biological impacts of marine heatwaves now extend from individual organisms to whole ecosystems and biogeochemical cycles (Smith et al., 2022).

**The continental water cycle is intensifying and becoming more variable, with more severe droughts and floods and a widespread decline in terrestrial water storage (*high confidence*).** Satellite observations reveal a widespread decline in global lake water storage (Yao et al., 2023), the twenty-first-century southwestern North American drought has intensified into a megadrought unprecedented in centuries (Williams et al., 2022), and an anthropogenic fingerprint is now detectable in global drought frequency, duration and intensity (Chiang et al., 2021).

**Human influence on the causes of individual extreme events is increasingly demonstrable through formal attribution, moving detection and attribution from the global mean to the scale of specific heatwaves, droughts and wildfire seasons (*high confidence*).** Attribution science has matured into a systematic capability that quantifies how anthropogenic warming has changed the probability and severity of extreme weather (Clarke et al., 2022) (Barriopedro et al., 2023).

**The biosphere is responding to and, in places, losing resilience under the combined pressures of warming, drought and disturbance (*medium-to-high confidence*).** Emerging signals of declining forest resilience are visible in global vegetation observations (Forzieri et al., 2022), hotter droughts leave a distinctive fingerprint on tree mortality worldwide (Hammond et al., 2022), and centuries of wetland loss have removed a large fraction of a globally important carbon and water reservoir.

**Several large-scale components of the climate system are approaching thresholds beyond which change may become self-sustaining, and the interactions among these tipping elements raise the risk of cascading change (*medium confidence*).** Interacting tipping elements can increase the risk of climate domino effects under sustained warming (Wunderling et al., 2021), and an assessment of planetary boundaries finds that the Earth system has now transgressed six of nine, including climate change (Richardson et al., 2023).

## 2.1 Introduction and Scope

This chapter assesses observed large-scale changes across the physical climate system and evaluates the evidence that human activity is their dominant cause. It synthesises changes in the atmosphere, ocean, cryosphere and terrestrial biosphere; the detection and attribution methodologies that connect those changes to their drivers; the behaviour of the large-scale modes of internal variability that modulate them; and the paleoclimatic and long-timescale context within which the recent changes must be interpreted. The organising principle is energetic: greenhouse gases added to the atmosphere reduce the efficiency with which the Earth radiates energy to space, the resulting imbalance is expressed as accumulating heat, and that heat is redistributed among the components of the system in ways that are individually observable and collectively coherent.

The assessment rests on a wider and more capable observing system than was available to earlier reports. Satellite records of the atmosphere, ocean surface, ice and vegetation now span multiple decades; reanalyses assimilate heterogeneous observations into physically consistent gridded estimates; and the continuous accounting of the global carbon cycle has become an annually updated community activity (Friedlingstein et al., 2022; Friedlingstein et al., 2023). Ocean and sea-ice reanalyses such as the Copernicus global reanalysis provide eddy-permitting reconstructions of the three-dimensional ocean state that underpin estimates of heat and freshwater change (Jean-Michel et al., 2021). Together these systems allow changes to be characterised not only in the global mean but in their spatial structure, seasonality and tails, where much of the impact resides.

A central theme of this chapter is that the confidence attached to statements of change and cause now derives from the *convergence* of independent evidence rather than from any single indicator. Warming of the lower atmosphere, warming and expansion of the ocean, retreat of glaciers and sea ice, poleward and upslope shifts of climatic zones, and the intensification of the water cycle are not separate findings to be weighed against one another; they are complementary expressions of a single, forced perturbation. Where a component appears anomalous — for example, where the observed Arctic amplification exceeds the modelled value (Rantanen et al., 2022) — the discrepancy is treated as information about process understanding, not as grounds for doubting the overall conclusion.

The chapter also foregrounds the increasing granularity of attribution. Detection and attribution once operated almost exclusively at the level of the global-mean temperature; it now routinely addresses regional trends, individual seasons and specific extreme events (Clarke et al., 2022). This shift has consequences for how the assessment is written: calibrated statements can be made not only about long-term global change but about the human contribution to particular droughts, heatwaves and fire seasons that populate the public record of climate impacts.

Finally, the chapter situates the recent instrumental record against the deeper past. Paleoclimate archives establish that the current concentrations of long-lived greenhouse gases and the current rate of warming are unusual in the context of many millennia, and that the climate system contains slow components whose full response to present-day forcing has not yet been realised (Hansen et al., 2023). This long view is essential for interpreting the committed changes already set in motion and for bounding the plausibility of the trajectories assessed elsewhere in this volume.

## 2.2 Changes in the Atmosphere

### 2.2.1 Atmospheric composition

**Atmospheric concentrations of the major long-lived greenhouse gases have continued their sustained rise, and the increase in atmospheric carbon dioxide is quantitatively consistent with cumulative anthropogenic emissions (*virtually certain*).** The global carbon budget, reconciled annually from fossil-fuel statistics, land-use change estimates, atmospheric growth measurements and independent ocean and land sink models, shows that anthropogenic carbon dioxide emissions have remained near their historical maximum in recent years and that the atmospheric burden has grown accordingly (Friedlingstein et al., 2022; Friedlingstein et al., 2023; Friedlingstein et al., 2025). The closure of this budget — the agreement between the top-down atmospheric growth rate and the bottom-up sum of sources and sinks — is one of the strongest available demonstrations that the observed composition change is anthropogenic in origin, because it requires the human source term to balance the books.

The partitioning of emitted carbon among the atmosphere, ocean and land is itself changing. The land and ocean sinks together continue to absorb roughly half of annual emissions, but the efficiency and stability of these sinks vary interannually with climate and are not guaranteed to persist (Friedlingstein et al., 2023). Regional analyses show that some terrestrial systems have strengthened as carbon sinks over recent decades — for example, forest expansion has dominated the land carbon sink in China since 1980 (Yu et al., 2022) — while others are being eroded by drought, fire and disturbance, a tension developed further in Section 2.5.

Beyond carbon dioxide, satellite spectrometry has transformed the monitoring of shorter-lived and reactive constituents. Retrievals of tropospheric nitrogen dioxide and methane from the Sentinel-5P instrument, validated against ground-based networks, now provide spatially resolved, near-daily maps of atmospheric composition that reveal emission hotspots, plumes and trends (Verhoelst et al., 2021) (Lorente et al., 2021). These records are increasingly used to attribute composition changes to specific source sectors and regions, complementing the global carbon accounting with a finer spatial view.

### 2.2.2 Surface temperature and its spatial structure

**Global surface warming has continued, and its spatial pattern is strongly polar-amplified, with the Arctic warming at nearly four times the global rate since 1979 (*high confidence*).** The magnitude of Arctic amplification has been quantified across multiple observational datasets, yielding a robust factor near four that substantially exceeds the roughly two-to-three-fold amplification commonly cited in earlier literature and reproduced by many models (Rantanen et al., 2022). The physical drivers — loss of sea ice and the associated surface-albedo and heat-exchange feedbacks, changes in atmospheric heat transport, and lapse-rate and cloud feedbacks specific to the polar night — are individually understood, but their combination in the observations implies that the coupled model ensemble may systematically underestimate the sensitivity of the Arctic to forcing.

Amplification is not confined to the poles. Mountain regions warm in ways that depend systematically on elevation, so that high-altitude environments frequently experience faster temperature change than adjacent lowlands, with consequences for snow, ice and the timing of meltwater (Pepin et al., 2022). Semi-arid and continental interiors, such as the Eastern Mediterranean and Middle East, are warming faster than the global average and are projected to experience a disproportionate increase in weather extremes (Zittis et al., 2022). The emergent picture is of a warming that is global in sign but highly structured in magnitude, concentrating the largest changes in the very regions — high latitudes, high altitudes, dry subtropics — whose cryospheric and hydrological systems are most sensitive.

### 2.2.3 The atmospheric water cycle and circulation

**The atmospheric branch of the water cycle is intensifying, increasing the potential for both heavy precipitation and evaporative drought, often in the same regions at different times (*high confidence*).** A warmer atmosphere holds more water vapour, strengthening the transport and convergence of moisture and sharpening the contrast between wet and dry regimes. Trends in precipitation are found to determine the future occurrence of compound hot–dry events, linking the thermodynamic and hydrological responses (Bevacqua et al., 2022). High-resolution regional climate simulations at kilometre scale, which explicitly resolve deep convection, reproduce observed precipitation characteristics more faithfully than coarser models and strengthen confidence in projected changes to short-duration extremes (Ban et al., 2021).

Changes in atmospheric circulation modulate this thermodynamic intensification. Persistent circulation anomalies — blocking highs, stalled ridges and displaced jets — concentrate heat and suppress precipitation, producing the stagnant regimes that underlie the most damaging heatwaves and droughts (Barriopedro et al., 2023). The physical understanding of heatwaves has advanced to the point where their dynamical and thermodynamical ingredients can be separated, allowing the anthropogenic contribution to be assessed even for events whose immediate cause is a specific circulation pattern.

### 2.2.4 Atmospheric aerosols and dust

**The atmospheric aerosol burden, including mineral dust mobilised from the world's arid regions, is an active and changing component of the climate system that both responds to and influences large-scale change (*medium confidence*).** The global cycle of desert dust is dominated by a small number of major source regions, and quantifying their relative contributions is essential for understanding dust's radiative, biogeochemical and cryospheric effects (Kok et al., 2021). Dust deposited on snow and ice lowers surface albedo and accelerates melt, coupling the aerosol and cryospheric components; dust transport also fertilises ocean and land ecosystems and modulates air quality far downwind of source regions. Because dust emission depends on surface wind, soil moisture and vegetation cover, all of which are changing, the dust cycle is both a diagnostic of large-scale change and a feedback upon it.

## 2.3 Changes in the Ocean

### 2.3.1 Ocean warming and heat content

**The ocean has continued to gain heat, and because it stores the overwhelming majority of the excess energy trapped by greenhouse gases, ocean warming is the most reliable single indicator of planetary energy imbalance (*virtually certain*).** Eddy-permitting global ocean reanalyses, which assimilate satellite altimetry, sea-surface temperature and the Argo profiling-float array into a physically consistent model, reconstruct the three-dimensional evolution of ocean temperature and reveal warming that penetrates from the surface into the deeper ocean over the reanalysis period (Jean-Michel et al., 2021). The subsurface expression of warming matters because it governs thermal expansion and sea-level rise, sets the thermal environment for marine ecosystems, and represents a reservoir of committed change that will continue to influence the climate long after surface temperatures stabilise.

The uptake of heat is not spatially uniform. Warming is concentrated in the upper ocean and varies by basin, with some regions — western boundary current extensions, marginal seas and portions of the Southern Ocean — warming considerably faster than the global mean. This heterogeneity is dynamically important: it alters density gradients, modifies current systems and shifts the geography of biological productivity. Reanalyses that resolve mesoscale eddies capture these regional structures with a fidelity unavailable to earlier, coarser products, sharpening the assessment of where and how fast the ocean is changing (Jean-Michel et al., 2021).

### 2.3.2 Marine heatwaves

**Marine heatwaves — prolonged periods of anomalously warm ocean temperatures — have become more frequent, more intense and longer-lasting, and their biological consequences now propagate through marine ecosystems from physiology to biogeochemistry (*high confidence*).** The accumulation of ocean heat raises the baseline upon which transient warm anomalies are superimposed, so that events that would once have been unremarkable now cross ecologically critical thresholds. A synthesis of the biological impacts of marine heatwaves documents effects spanning individual organism stress and mortality, shifts in species distributions, mass mortality events, harmful algal blooms, and disruption of the biogeochemical cycling that links the ocean to the atmosphere (Smith et al., 2022). Because many marine organisms live close to their thermal limits, the same warming that is modest in the annual mean can be lethal when concentrated into a heatwave, making these events a leading edge of climate impact in the ocean.

The coupling between marine heatwaves and the atmosphere is bidirectional. Ocean warm anomalies can reinforce atmospheric ridging and, in coastal settings, contribute to terrestrial heat extremes, while atmospheric circulation anomalies and reduced cloud cover help generate and sustain the ocean anomalies in the first place. This coupling situates marine heatwaves within the broader assessment of compound and connected extremes developed in Section 2.6.

### 2.3.3 Sea level and the coastal zone

**Sea level continues to rise as a combined consequence of ocean thermal expansion and the addition of water from melting land ice, and the resulting encroachment on coasts is among the most consequential large-scale changes for human systems (*high confidence*).** Thermal expansion follows directly from the subsurface ocean warming documented above, while the mass contribution reflects the losses from glaciers and ice sheets assessed in Section 2.4. The coastal zone integrates these global signals with regional and local factors — land subsidence, sediment supply, and changes in storm and wave climate — to determine the actual exposure experienced at any given shoreline. Adaptation to sea-level rise has accordingly become a central concern of coastal management, requiring the translation of global projections into local planning under deep uncertainty (Griggs et al., 2021). The assessment emphasises that even where the global-mean rate is well constrained, the distribution of impact is set by the regional pattern of sea-level change and by the vulnerability of particular coastlines.

### 2.3.4 Continental waters as part of the hydrosphere

**The storage of water on the continents — in lakes, wetlands, soils and groundwater — is declining across much of the world, redistributing water within the Earth system in ways that couple the ocean, atmosphere and land (*high confidence*).** Satellite observations reveal a widespread decline in global lake water storage, affecting a majority of the world's large lakes and reflecting the combined influence of warming, altered precipitation and human water use (Yao et al., 2023). Mapping of global lake dynamics further shows that small lakes, though individually minor, collectively play an outsized and growing role in the terrestrial water and carbon budgets (Pi et al., 2022). Wetlands, a distinct and disproportionately important store of carbon and water, have been lost over centuries of drainage and conversion, with an estimated large fraction of the pre-industrial wetland area now gone. These continental changes are not peripheral to the ocean assessment: water removed from terrestrial storage ultimately reaches the sea, and the drying of the land surface feeds back on the atmosphere through altered evaporation and heat fluxes.

## 2.4 Changes in the Cryosphere

### 2.4.1 Arctic amplification and the sea-ice-covered ocean

**The Arctic is warming far faster than the rest of the planet, and this amplification is the clearest regional signature of the coupled ice–ocean–atmosphere feedbacks that operate in a warming world (*high confidence*).** The observed factor of nearly four relative to the global mean since 1979 exceeds most model estimates and points to feedbacks — principally the loss of reflective sea ice and the exposure of dark ocean — operating more strongly than the ensemble captures (Rantanen et al., 2022). The retreat of Arctic sea ice reorganises the surface energy balance, alters the exchange of heat and moisture between ocean and atmosphere, and has been implicated in changes to mid-latitude circulation, although the strength of the latter teleconnection remains an area of active research.

Intensive field campaigns have deepened process understanding of the coupled Arctic system. The year-long drift observations of the MOSAiC expedition characterised the central Arctic atmosphere across a full seasonal cycle, documenting the boundary-layer, cloud and aerosol processes that govern surface energy exchange over sea ice (Shupe et al., 2022). Such observations are essential for evaluating and improving the representation of the polar climate in models, and thus for understanding why the observed amplification outpaces the simulated one.

### 2.4.2 Mountain glaciers and snow

**Mountain glaciers are losing mass across every glaciated region, and the pace of loss has accelerated over the satellite era, with immediate consequences for water resources and sea level (*high confidence*).** Multi-temporal satellite observations of High Mountain Asia — the source region for rivers on which a large share of humanity depends — document sustained thinning and retreat since the 1960s, with the rate of mass loss increasing in recent decades (Bhattacharya et al., 2021). Because mountain warming is elevation-dependent, the high-altitude environments where glaciers reside are frequently warming faster than surrounding lowlands, compounding the direct effect of rising temperature on ice (Pepin et al., 2022). The loss of glacier and snow storage alters the seasonal timing and long-term availability of meltwater, initially increasing runoff as ice is depleted and subsequently reducing it as the reservoir shrinks — a trajectory with profound implications for downstream agriculture, hydropower and ecosystems.

### 2.4.3 The cryosphere as an integrator

The cryosphere functions as an integrator of atmospheric and oceanic change and as an amplifier of it. Ice responds to temperature with a threshold nonlinearity — the phase change at the melting point — that concentrates change into particular seasons and regions and gives the cryosphere its characteristic sensitivity. The albedo contrast between ice and the underlying ocean or land converts a modest warming into a much larger local energy gain, and this feedback is the proximate reason for the polar and alpine amplification documented throughout this chapter (Rantanen et al., 2022) (Pepin et al., 2022). Deposition of light-absorbing aerosols, including mineral dust from distant source regions, further lowers snow and ice albedo and accelerates melt, linking the cryospheric assessment back to the atmospheric aerosol cycle (Kok et al., 2021). The cumulative loss of land ice, expressed globally, is a principal driver of the sea-level rise assessed in Section 2.3, closing a chain of causation that runs from greenhouse forcing through the atmosphere and cryosphere to the coastal ocean.

## 2.5 Changes in the Biosphere and Terrestrial System

### 2.5.1 Forest resilience and disturbance

**Global forests are showing emerging signals of declining resilience, and disturbance regimes are intensifying, indicating that a major terrestrial carbon store is coming under increasing stress (*medium-to-high confidence*).** Analysis of satellite vegetation records identifies a widespread reduction in forest resilience — the capacity to recover from perturbation — over recent decades, particularly in tropical, arid and temperate regions, with the loss of resilience linked to increasing water limitation and climate variability (Forzieri et al., 2022). This erosion of resilience is a leading indicator: it precedes outright mortality and signals that forests are approaching the limits of their adaptive capacity before large-scale dieback becomes visible.

The disturbance regimes acting on forests are themselves changing. In Europe, natural disturbances — windthrow, bark-beetle outbreaks and fire — have increased substantially in their impact on forests since the mid-twentieth century, driven by warming and by the changing structure of forest stands (Patacca et al., 2022). Drought is a common thread linking these disturbances, and its physiological signature on trees is now globally recognisable.

### 2.5.2 Drought-driven tree mortality

**Episodes of tree die-off around the world carry a distinctive "hotter-drought" fingerprint, in which elevated temperature amplifies the lethality of water deficit (*high confidence*).** A global synthesis of field observations of tree mortality shows that die-off events are associated not merely with low precipitation but with the combination of drought and anomalously high temperature, which increases atmospheric moisture demand and pushes trees past hydraulic thresholds (Hammond et al., 2022). This finding reframes drought impacts on forests as a coupled temperature–moisture phenomenon consistent with the intensifying water cycle assessed in Section 2.2, and it implies that continued warming will raise the mortality risk associated with any given precipitation deficit. Because forests are both a carbon sink and a store, drought-driven mortality represents a potential positive feedback on atmospheric carbon dioxide, developed further below.

### 2.5.3 The terrestrial carbon sink

**The terrestrial biosphere remains a substantial net sink for anthropogenic carbon, but the sink is regionally heterogeneous and vulnerable to the same drought, fire and disturbance pressures that are eroding forest resilience (*medium confidence*).** The global carbon budget attributes roughly a third of annual anthropogenic emissions to the land sink, though with large interannual variability tied to climate (Friedlingstein et al., 2022; Friedlingstein et al., 2023). Some regions have strengthened as sinks: forest expansion has dominated the land carbon sink in China since 1980, illustrating how land management and reforestation can enhance carbon uptake (Yu et al., 2022). Yet the durability of such sinks is not assured, because the very forests that store carbon are subject to increasing disturbance (Patacca et al., 2022) and to hotter-drought mortality (Hammond et al., 2022). The assessment therefore treats the land sink as a service that is presently large but conditionally stable, whose future depends on the trajectory of warming and on management choices assessed elsewhere in this volume.

### 2.5.4 Wetlands and the long history of land-cover change

**The terrestrial biosphere has been reshaped over centuries of human land use, and the loss of wetlands in particular has removed a disproportionately important store of carbon and water (*high confidence*).** A reconstruction of global wetland extent finds extensive loss over the past three centuries, with a large fraction of the pre-industrial wetland area drained or converted, concentrated in regions of intensive agriculture. Because wetlands store carbon in saturated soils and regulate local hydrology, their loss both releases carbon and reduces the landscape's capacity to buffer floods and droughts. This long-horizon change provides essential context for the recent instrumental record: the biosphere against which contemporary change is measured is already a heavily modified system, and some of its capacity to absorb carbon and moderate the water cycle has already been spent.

## 2.6 Changes in Modes of Variability and Extremes

### 2.6.1 The changing character of extremes

**Extreme events across the temperature and hydrological spectrum are changing in frequency and intensity in ways consistent with a warming, moisture-laden atmosphere, and increasingly these changes can be quantified event by event (*high confidence*).** Attribution science, once confined to long-term trends, now systematically evaluates how anthropogenic warming has altered the probability and magnitude of individual extremes, providing an attribution perspective on the extreme-weather impacts of climate change (Clarke et al., 2022). Heatwaves illustrate the maturity of this understanding: their physical drivers, from large-scale circulation to land–atmosphere feedbacks, are now sufficiently well characterised that the human contribution to specific events can be assessed with calibrated confidence (Barriopedro et al., 2023).

The most extreme recent heat events have pushed beyond the range of prior experience. The Pacific Northwest heatwave of June 2021 produced temperatures so far outside the historical distribution that it challenged existing statistical models of extremes and prompted reassessment of how the tails of the temperature distribution respond to warming (White et al., 2023). Such unprecedented events are consequential precisely because infrastructure, ecosystems and public-health systems are calibrated to historical extremes, so that a jump beyond the prior record carries disproportionate impact.

### 2.6.2 Drought as a coupled and partly anthropogenic phenomenon

**Drought is becoming more frequent, more intense and more clearly linked to human influence, with anthropogenic signals now detectable in global drought statistics and in specific regional megadroughts (*high confidence*).** A detection-and-attribution analysis finds evidence of anthropogenic impacts on global drought frequency, duration and intensity, moving drought from a phenomenon attributed only to natural variability toward one with a demonstrable human component (Chiang et al., 2021). At the regional scale, the southwestern North American drought that emerged in the twenty-first century intensified into a megadrought whose severity, when placed against tree-ring reconstructions spanning centuries, is unprecedented and substantially attributable to anthropogenic warming (Williams et al., 2022). Europe's multi-year drought of 2018–2020 set a new benchmark in the observational record for its spatial extent and persistence (Rakovec et al., 2022).

The conceptual framing of drought has evolved accordingly. Drought is increasingly recognised as an anthropogenic phenomenon in a dual sense — driven partly by warming-induced increases in evaporative demand and partly by direct human interventions in the water cycle such as abstraction and land-use change — requiring definitions and management approaches that account for the human role (AghaKouchak et al., 2021). This reframing connects the physical-climate assessment of drought to the management challenge of unprecedented hydrological extremes (Kreibich et al., 2022).

### 2.6.3 Compound and connected extremes

**Extremes increasingly occur in combination — hot and dry together, or in sequences of drought and flood — and these compound events often carry impacts greater than the sum of their parts (*high confidence*).** Precipitation trends have been shown to determine the future occurrence of compound hot–dry events, so that regions drying in the mean become disproportionately exposed to the simultaneous occurrence of heat and water deficit (Bevacqua et al., 2022). The management of water extremes is complicated by the tendency of unprecedented floods and droughts to arrive in succession or in unexpected combination, straining risk-management systems designed around historical, independent hazards (Kreibich et al., 2022). Wildfire sits at the intersection of these compound pressures: the multi-decadal increase in forest burned area in Australia has been linked to climate change through the lengthening and intensification of fire-weather conditions (Canadell et al., 2021), and the extreme Australian fire season of 2019–2020 was the subject of formal attribution linking its elevated risk to anthropogenic warming (Oldenborgh et al., 2021).

### 2.6.4 Modes of internal variability

**Large-scale modes of internal variability remain the dominant control on year-to-year climate fluctuations, and their interaction with the forced trend shapes where and when extremes occur (*high confidence*).** The recurring patterns of coupled ocean–atmosphere variability redistribute heat and moisture on interannual to decadal timescales, so that the forced warming trend is expressed through, rather than instead of, this internal variability. Persistent circulation regimes associated with these modes — including the blocking patterns that anchor heatwaves and the pressure anomalies that suppress precipitation — are the proximate dynamical cause of many extremes even where the thermodynamic background has been shifted by warming (Barriopedro et al., 2023). Distinguishing the forced signal from internal variability is the central methodological task of detection and attribution, assessed in the following section, and it is the reason that calibrated statements about causation require the ensemble and statistical machinery described there rather than inspection of any single event in isolation.

## 2.7 Detection and Attribution of Causes

### 2.7.1 The logic of detection and attribution

**The attribution of observed climate change to human influence rests on the physical consistency of the change with the known effect of greenhouse forcing and on the inability of natural factors to reproduce it, and this logic now yields robust conclusions across the components of the system (*virtually certain* for global warming; *high confidence* for many regional and event-scale statements).** Detection establishes that a change has occurred that is unlikely to be an artefact of internal variability; attribution assigns that change, quantitatively, to its causes. The methodology combines observations with model experiments in which forcings are switched on and off, statistical fingerprinting that isolates the spatial and temporal pattern expected from each forcing, and, for individual events, comparison of the present, warmed world against a counterfactual world without anthropogenic influence. The closure of the global carbon budget already establishes the anthropogenic origin of the composition change (Friedlingstein et al., 2022; Friedlingstein et al., 2023); the attribution enterprise extends that reasoning from the cause to the consequences.

Confidence in attribution is highest where multiple independent methods and datasets converge. For global-mean surface warming, the consistency of the observed warming with the forced response, the incompatibility of the observations with natural-only forcing, and the physical closure of the energy budget through ocean heat uptake together render the human origin effectively certain. Reanalyses that reconstruct the three-dimensional ocean provide the heat-content evidence (Jean-Michel et al., 2021), while the carbon budget provides the forcing side of the ledger (Friedlingstein et al., 2023).

### 2.7.2 Attribution of regional change and amplification

**Regional patterns of change carry their own attributable signatures, of which polar and elevation-dependent amplification are the most prominent (*high confidence*).** The observed Arctic amplification factor of nearly four is not merely a regional curiosity; it is a test of the models' representation of the ice–albedo and related feedbacks, and its excess over the simulated value is itself an attributable, physically interpretable discrepancy (Rantanen et al., 2022). Elevation-dependent warming in mountains similarly follows from identifiable processes — snow-albedo feedback, changes in the altitude of the freezing level, and water-vapour and cloud effects that vary with height — allowing the mountain temperature signal to be attributed to warming even though its magnitude varies with elevation (Pepin et al., 2022). Regional drying and extreme-heat trends in the Eastern Mediterranean and Middle East likewise emerge as attributable regional expressions of the global forcing (Zittis et al., 2022).

### 2.7.3 Event attribution

**Formal event attribution now provides calibrated statements about the human contribution to specific extreme events, transforming the assessment of causation from the statistical aggregate to the individual occurrence (*high confidence* for well-observed heat and drought events).** The attribution perspective on extreme-weather impacts systematises this capability, distinguishing events for which the anthropogenic influence is strong and well-constrained from those where the signal is weaker or the dynamics more uncertain (Clarke et al., 2022). Heatwaves are the most tractable case: the physical understanding of their formation supports confident attribution, and the most extreme recent events have been shown to lie far outside the range expected without warming (Barriopedro et al., 2023) (White et al., 2023). Drought attribution has advanced from global statistics (Chiang et al., 2021) to specific megadroughts (Williams et al., 2022) and multi-year continental droughts (Rakovec et al., 2022). Wildfire attribution connects the fire-weather environment to warming, as demonstrated for the Australian fire seasons through both trend analysis (Canadell et al., 2021) and event-specific risk attribution (Oldenborgh et al., 2021).

Event attribution carries a methodological caution. Because it compares the observed world against a modelled counterfactual, its confidence depends on the fidelity with which models represent the relevant processes — a dependence that is strongest for dynamically driven events and weakest for thermodynamically dominated ones such as heat. The assessment therefore attaches higher confidence to the attribution of heat and, increasingly, hydrological extremes than to events whose occurrence hinges on hard-to-model circulation details. High-resolution, convection-permitting simulations improve this situation for precipitation extremes by resolving processes that coarse models parameterise (Ban et al., 2021).

### 2.7.4 Attribution of impacts

**The chain of attribution now reaches beyond physical climate variables to the impacts they cause, quantifying the human contribution to outcomes such as heat-related mortality (*high confidence*).** A multi-country analysis attributes a substantial share of recent heat-related mortality specifically to human-induced climate change, completing the causal chain from emissions to composition to warming to extreme heat to human health outcomes. Impact attribution of this kind is methodologically demanding because it must combine climate attribution with exposure–response relationships, but where the underlying physical attribution is strong — as it is for heat — the impact attribution inherits that confidence. This capability is central to the wider assessment because it renders the consequences of large-scale climate change in terms directly meaningful to human systems.

## 2.8 Paleoclimatic and Long-Term Context

### 2.8.1 The recent record against the deep past

**Placed in the context of the deeper past, the current concentrations of long-lived greenhouse gases and the current rate of change are unusual, and the climate system retains a large, slowly realised response to the forcing already imposed (*high confidence*).** Long-timescale analysis indicates that the full warming implied by present greenhouse gas concentrations has not yet been expressed at the surface, because the ocean's thermal inertia delays the approach to equilibrium and because slow feedbacks continue to operate long after the fast response is complete — a reservoir of committed change described as warming still "in the pipeline" (Hansen et al., 2023). This long view is essential to the assessment for two reasons: it establishes that the present state is anomalous rather than within the range of recent natural variation, and it warns that surface temperature at any moment understates the eventual response to the accumulated forcing.

### 2.8.2 Slow components and committed change

The climate system contains components whose response times greatly exceed the interval over which greenhouse gases have accumulated. The deep ocean, the great ice sheets, and the carbon stored in soils, permafrost and vegetation all evolve over timescales from decades to millennia. Ocean heat uptake, reconstructed by reanalysis, is the clearest expression of this slow adjustment: heat sequestered below the surface represents warming that is committed but not yet expressed in surface air temperature (Jean-Michel et al., 2021). The loss of land ice, already underway in mountain regions (Bhattacharya et al., 2021) and contributing to sea-level rise (Griggs et al., 2021), likewise carries momentum that will continue for centuries even under stabilised forcing. The paleoclimatic and process-based evidence together imply that the changes assessed in this chapter are the leading edge of a longer adjustment, and that the avoidance of the largest changes depends on limiting the accumulated forcing rather than on the instantaneous temperature alone (Hansen et al., 2023).

## 2.9 System-Scale Synthesis: Tipping Elements and Planetary Boundaries

### 2.9.1 Interacting tipping elements

**Several large-scale components of the climate system exhibit the potential for threshold behaviour, and their interactions can propagate change from one component to another, raising the risk of cascading, partly irreversible transitions (*medium confidence*).** Analysis of the network of climate tipping elements shows that the interactions among them tend to lower the effective thresholds and increase the risk of domino effects, so that the destabilisation of one element can raise the probability of destabilising others under sustained warming (Wunderling et al., 2021). The components assessed throughout this chapter supply the physical substance of this concern: the Arctic's amplified warming and ice loss (Rantanen et al., 2022), the loss of resilience in major forests (Forzieri et al., 2022) and their vulnerability to hotter-drought mortality (Hammond et al., 2022), and the drying of continental water stores (Yao et al., 2023) are each candidate expressions of, or contributors to, threshold behaviour. The assessment attaches medium confidence to the tipping-element framing because, while the individual processes are well established, the thresholds themselves and the strength of the interactions remain uncertain.

### 2.9.2 Planetary boundaries

**Viewed at the scale of the whole Earth system, the accumulated changes have pushed multiple large-scale processes beyond the ranges that characterised the pre-industrial state, with climate change among the boundaries now transgressed (*high confidence* that multiple boundaries are transgressed; *medium confidence* on their precise placement).** An integrated assessment of the planetary-boundaries framework concludes that six of nine boundaries — including climate change, biosphere integrity, freshwater change and land-system change — have now been crossed, indicating that human pressure on the Earth system extends across multiple, interacting dimensions rather than climate alone (Richardson et al., 2023). This framing connects the physical-climate findings of this chapter to the broader transformation of the planet: the intensification of the water cycle and decline of continental water storage (Yao et al., 2023), the modification of forests and land cover (Patacca et al., 2022) (Yu et al., 2022), and the loss of biosphere resilience (Forzieri et al., 2022) are the climate-system manifestations of boundary transgressions that also have non-climatic dimensions.

### 2.9.3 Synthesis

The evidence assessed in this chapter forms a single, coherent account. Human activity has raised the concentrations of long-lived greenhouse gases, a fact established beyond dispute by the closure of the global carbon budget (Friedlingstein et al., 2022; Friedlingstein et al., 2023; Friedlingstein et al., 2025). The resulting energy imbalance has warmed the atmosphere, amplified most strongly at high latitudes and high altitudes (Rantanen et al., 2022) (Pepin et al., 2022); warmed and expanded the ocean, driving marine heatwaves and sea-level rise (Smith et al., 2022) (Jean-Michel et al., 2021) (Griggs et al., 2021); shrunk the cryosphere, with mountain glaciers in sustained retreat (Bhattacharya et al., 2021); intensified and destabilised the continental water cycle, producing more severe droughts and floods and a widespread loss of water storage (Chiang et al., 2021) (Williams et al., 2022) (Yao et al., 2023); and placed the terrestrial biosphere under stress that is beginning to erode its resilience (Forzieri et al., 2022) (Hammond et al., 2022). These changes are attributable to human influence with high-to-virtual confidence at the global scale and, increasingly, at the scale of individual regions and events (Clarke et al., 2022) (Barriopedro et al., 2023). Set against the deep past, they are anomalous and incompletely realised, with substantial further change already committed (Hansen et al., 2023). And viewed at the scale of the whole Earth system, they are part of a broader transgression of planetary boundaries whose interacting elements raise the risk of cascading change (Wunderling et al., 2021) (Richardson et al., 2023). The remainder of this volume builds on this assessment of what has changed and why to evaluate what is projected to come and what can still be avoided.

## 2.10 The Observational and Modelling Basis of the Assessment

### 2.10.1 Reanalyses and the reconstruction of climate state

**The assessment of large-scale change depends on observing and reconstruction systems that convert heterogeneous measurements into physically consistent, gridded estimates of the climate state, and the capability of these systems has advanced markedly (*high confidence*).** Ocean and sea-ice reanalyses now provide eddy-permitting reconstructions of the three-dimensional ocean, assimilating satellite altimetry, sea-surface temperature and the global array of profiling floats into a dynamical model to yield continuous fields of temperature, salinity, currents and ice (Jean-Michel et al., 2021). These products are the backbone of the ocean-warming and heat-content evidence assessed in Section 2.3, and their resolution of mesoscale structure allows regional patterns of change — the warming of boundary currents, the freshening of polar seas — to be characterised rather than only the global mean. Because reanalyses are physically constrained, they also enforce consistency between variables, so that the reconstructed heat, freshwater and momentum budgets must balance, providing an internal check on the observations they assimilate.

The value of reanalysis lies in its combination of coverage and consistency. Direct observations are sparse in the deep ocean, the polar regions and the pre-satellite past; a reanalysis fills these gaps using the model's dynamics, propagating information from data-rich regions and times into data-poor ones. The resulting fields are not observations in the strict sense, and their uncertainty must be assessed against independent data, but they enable the calculation of integrated quantities — ocean heat content, steric sea level, meridional transports — that no single instrument measures directly and that are central to the energetic framing of this chapter.

### 2.10.2 Satellite retrieval of atmospheric composition

**Satellite remote sensing has become the primary means of monitoring the spatial distribution and trends of atmospheric constituents, and rigorous validation against ground-based networks establishes the accuracy required for climate assessment (*high confidence*).** Retrievals of tropospheric nitrogen dioxide from the Sentinel-5P mission have been validated against multiple independent ground-based network techniques, characterising the biases and uncertainties of the satellite product and establishing its fitness for monitoring pollution and its trends (Verhoelst et al., 2021). Methane retrievals from the same platform have been improved and validated over their first years of operation, yielding a data product capable of resolving regional methane enhancements and, increasingly, individual strong sources (Lorente et al., 2021). These capabilities complement the global carbon accounting assessed in Section 2.2 by adding a spatially explicit view of where reactive and greenhouse constituents are entering the atmosphere, supporting the attribution of composition change to specific sectors and regions.

Validation is the linchpin of this capability. A satellite retrieval is an inversion of a radiance measurement through a forward model of atmospheric radiative transfer, and its accuracy depends on the treatment of clouds, aerosols, surface reflectance and the a priori assumptions of the retrieval. Comparison against ground-based reference instruments quantifies the residual biases and their dependence on observing conditions, and only after such validation can satellite trends be interpreted as geophysical rather than instrumental (Verhoelst et al., 2021) (Lorente et al., 2021). The assessment relies on validated products of this kind and treats unvalidated retrievals with corresponding caution.

### 2.10.3 Climate models and the CMIP ensemble

**Coordinated multi-model experiments provide the counterfactual worlds against which observed change is attributed and the projections against which committed change is judged, and the current generation of Earth system models represents an increasingly complete set of processes (*high confidence*).** Contemporary Earth system models couple the atmosphere, ocean, sea ice, land and biogeochemistry, and participate in coordinated intercomparison so that their behaviour can be evaluated against observations and against one another; the CMCC Earth System Model exemplifies this class, integrating carbon-cycle and physical-climate components within a single coupled framework (Lovato et al., 2022). The scenario experiments that drive projections are themselves a coordinated activity, spanning a range of plausible futures for emissions and land use so that the response can be mapped across the space of possible forcings (Tebaldi et al., 2021). The translation from emissions scenarios to global temperature outcomes has been systematised through reduced-complexity model assessments that link the detailed scenario space to calibrated temperature projections (Kikstra et al., 2022).

Model evaluation is central to the credibility of these tools. Where models reproduce observed features — the seasonal cycle, the response to volcanic eruptions, the pattern of historical warming — confidence in their projections is strengthened; where they diverge from observations, as in the underestimate of Arctic amplification (Rantanen et al., 2022), the discrepancy is flagged and its implications assessed. The ensemble is treated not as a set of independent truths but as a sample of structural uncertainty, whose spread informs the confidence attached to projected change.

### 2.10.4 High-resolution and kilometre-scale simulation

**Advances in resolution, to the point of explicitly resolving deep convection, materially improve the simulation of precipitation and short-duration extremes, sharpening the assessment of hydrological change (*high confidence*).** The first multi-model ensemble of kilometre-scale regional climate simulations demonstrates that convection-permitting models reproduce the characteristics of observed precipitation — its diurnal cycle, intensity distribution and extremes — more faithfully than coarser models that must parameterise convection (Ban et al., 2021). This matters for the assessment of the water cycle because many of the most damaging hydrological hazards arise from short, intense precipitation that coarse models systematically misrepresent. In the operational domain, hourly-updating, convection-allowing forecast systems have been developed and documented, illustrating the same resolution advances at the weather timescale and providing high-frequency analyses useful for characterising extremes (Dowell et al., 2022).

### 2.10.5 Machine learning and hybrid modelling

**The integration of machine learning with process-based models is emerging as a means to improve predictions and to extract information from the growing volume of Earth observations, while retaining physical interpretability (*medium confidence*).** Differentiable modelling, which unifies machine learning with physical models, offers a route to combining the predictive power of data-driven methods with the constraints and interpretability of physics, with applications across the geosciences from hydrology to land-surface processes (Shen et al., 2023). Such hybrid approaches are increasingly used to calibrate parameterisations, emulate expensive model components, and assimilate observations, and they are likely to become a significant part of the observational and modelling infrastructure on which future assessments rely. The assessment treats these methods as promising and rapidly developing, attaching medium confidence in recognition of their novelty and of the ongoing work to establish their reliability and generalisability.

### 2.10.6 Citizen science and the extension of observation

**Citizen science has become a substantive contributor to environmental and ecological observation, extending the reach of monitoring in space and time and enabling analyses that professional networks alone could not support (*medium confidence*).** Structured citizen-science programmes now generate large volumes of data in environmental and ecological research, contributing to biodiversity monitoring, phenology, hydrology and the documentation of extreme events (Fraisl et al., 2022). These data broaden the observational base underpinning assessments of biosphere and hydrological change, particularly in regions and for variables that are poorly served by instrumental networks, though their heterogeneous provenance requires careful quality control before use in formal analysis.

## 2.11 The Continental Water Cycle in Detail

### 2.11.1 Floods and droughts as coupled hazards

**Floods and droughts are increasingly understood as coupled expressions of an intensifying and more variable water cycle rather than as independent hazards, and their prediction and management pose challenges that the changing climate is sharpening (*high confidence*).** A review of the modelling and prediction of floods and droughts identifies the persistent difficulties in representing the processes that generate these extremes, from the partitioning of precipitation at the land surface to the propagation of anomalies through catchments and aquifers (Brunner et al., 2021). The management challenge is compounded when floods and droughts arrive in unprecedented combination or succession, straining institutions and infrastructure designed around historical, stationary hazard distributions (Kreibich et al., 2022). The reframing of drought as a partly anthropogenic phenomenon — driven by warming-enhanced evaporative demand and by direct human interventions in the water cycle — further complicates definition and attribution and calls for management approaches that recognise the human role (AghaKouchak et al., 2021).

### 2.11.2 Regional drought benchmarks

**Specific regional droughts of the recent record now stand as benchmarks whose severity is unprecedented in the instrumental and, in some cases, the multi-century reconstructed record (*high confidence*).** The southwestern North American drought of the twenty-first century intensified, in 2020–2021, into a megadrought whose severity ranks among the most extreme of the past millennium as judged against tree-ring reconstructions, with anthropogenic warming responsible for a substantial part of its intensity through increased evaporative demand and reduced soil moisture (Williams et al., 2022). In Europe, the multi-year drought of 2018–2020 set a new benchmark for spatial extent and persistence in the observational record, exposing the vulnerability of temperate, ostensibly water-rich regions to sustained water deficit (Rakovec et al., 2022). The detection of an anthropogenic signal in global drought statistics provides the wider context within which these regional benchmarks sit (Chiang et al., 2021).

### 2.11.3 Terrestrial water storage and lakes

**The integrated storage of water on land is declining across much of the world, and the loss is visible in lakes large and small as well as in wetlands and soils (*high confidence*).** Satellite observations reveal a widespread decline in global lake water storage, with a majority of large lakes losing water over recent decades under the combined influence of warming, altered precipitation and human abstraction (Yao et al., 2023). Mapping of global lake dynamics shows that small lakes contribute disproportionately to the variability of the terrestrial water surface and to associated carbon fluxes, and that their role is growing (Pi et al., 2022). These surface-water changes are the visible expression of a broader redistribution of water that also encompasses soil moisture and groundwater, and that ultimately returns water to the ocean, coupling the continental and marine branches of the hydrosphere assessed in Section 2.3.

## 2.12 The Terrestrial Biosphere in Detail

### 2.12.1 Soils as a climate-system reservoir

**Soils are a large and dynamic reservoir of carbon, water and biodiversity whose behaviour couples the land surface to the atmosphere and whose functioning is being altered by climate change and land use (*high confidence*).** Soil hydrology governs the partitioning of precipitation into runoff, evaporation and storage, and thereby mediates droughts, floods and the supply of moisture to the atmosphere; its representation is increasingly recognised as central to understanding the land component of the Earth system (Vereecken et al., 2022). The biological functioning of soils is tightly linked to their carbon content: across global biomes, soil microbial diversity and biomass are related in ways driven by the amount of carbon stored in the soil, so that changes in soil carbon propagate into the microbial communities that regulate decomposition and nutrient cycling (Bastida et al., 2021). Where the soil resource is degraded, these functions are impaired: erosion has been shown to reduce soil microbial diversity, network complexity and multifunctionality, indicating that physical degradation and biological decline proceed together (Qiu et al., 2021).

Soil degradation interacts with climate change to threaten the services soils provide. Global projections indicate that primary soil salinization is likely to expand under a changing climate through the twenty-first century, as altered precipitation and evaporation concentrate salts in vulnerable regions and impair the productivity of the land (Hassani et al., 2021). Salinization, erosion and the loss of soil carbon together constitute a degradation of the land-system boundary whose transgression is part of the broader planetary-boundaries assessment (Richardson et al., 2023), and whose consequences for food production connect the physical-climate assessment to human systems.

### 2.12.2 Ecosystems, biodiversity and context dependence

**Ecological responses to climate change are pervasive but strongly context-dependent, so that general statements about biosphere change must be qualified by the local conditions that modulate the response (*high confidence* that responses are widespread; *medium confidence* on their generalisation).** The dependence of ecological outcomes on context — the specific combination of species, environment and history at a given place — is a recognised challenge for the field, complicating the transfer of findings from one system to another and requiring that syntheses account explicitly for the sources of variation (Catford et al., 2021). This caution applies throughout the biosphere assessment: the loss of forest resilience (Forzieri et al., 2022), the incidence of hotter-drought mortality (Hammond et al., 2022) and the response of soil communities (Bastida et al., 2021) all vary systematically with local conditions, and the assessment attaches confidence to the existence and direction of these responses while acknowledging that their magnitude is place-specific.

Among the most concerning biosphere signals is the pressure on insects, which underpin pollination, decomposition and food webs. A scientists' warning on climate change and insects synthesises evidence that warming, together with land-use change and other stressors, is contributing to declines and distributional shifts in insect populations with potential consequences that cascade through ecosystems (Harvey et al., 2022). Because insects mediate ecosystem functions on which both wild systems and agriculture depend, changes in their abundance and distribution represent a channel through which climate change propagates into the wider biosphere and into human food security.

### 2.12.3 Climate change, agriculture and food security

**Climate change is affecting the productivity and stability of agriculture through direct effects on crops and through indirect effects mediated by pests, pathogens and soils, with impacts emerging earlier than earlier model generations suggested (*high confidence*).** A new generation of coupled climate and crop models indicates that the impacts of climate change on global agriculture emerge earlier and are more pronounced than previously projected, with major staple crops experiencing significant changes in suitability and yield within the coming decades under continued warming (Jägermeyr et al., 2021). These direct effects are compounded by biological intermediaries: climate change is altering the distribution and severity of plant pathogens, with consequences for crop losses and food security that add to the direct climatic impacts on yield (Singh et al., 2023). Soil degradation closes a further pathway, as salinization reduces the productive capacity of agricultural land in vulnerable regions (Hassani et al., 2021). The assessment treats agriculture as a sensitive integrator of biosphere and hydrological change, in which the physical signals documented throughout this chapter are translated into consequences for human sustenance.

### 2.12.4 The biosphere as source, sink and feedback

**The terrestrial biosphere is simultaneously a sink that removes anthropogenic carbon, a store that can be released, and a set of feedbacks that can amplify or dampen change, and the balance among these roles is shifting under climate and land-use pressure (*medium confidence*).** The land sink absorbs a large share of annual emissions (Friedlingstein et al., 2022; Friedlingstein et al., 2023), and management can enhance it, as demonstrated by forest-expansion-driven carbon uptake in China (Yu et al., 2022). But the same warming that drives uptake through longer growing seasons and carbon-dioxide fertilisation also drives loss through drought mortality (Hammond et al., 2022), disturbance (Patacca et al., 2022) and the degradation of soils (Qiu et al., 2021) (Hassani et al., 2021). The net trajectory of the biosphere sink is therefore contingent, depending on the balance between these opposing tendencies and on the level of warming reached. The erosion of forest resilience (Forzieri et al., 2022) is the clearest warning that the sink is not unconditionally secure, and the interaction of biosphere change with other tipping elements (Wunderling et al., 2021) is the mechanism by which a weakening sink could accelerate the accumulation of atmospheric carbon.

## 2.13 Drivers of Atmospheric Composition Change

### 2.13.1 The sectoral structure of emissions

**The rise in greenhouse gas concentrations assessed in Section 2.2 originates in a well-characterised set of emitting sectors whose trends and drivers can be quantified, closing the causal loop between human activity and atmospheric change (*high confidence*).** A review of the trends and drivers of greenhouse gas emissions by sector documents the growth of emissions across energy supply, industry, transport, buildings and agriculture over recent decades, and decomposes that growth into the underlying drivers of population, affluence, energy intensity and carbon intensity (Lamb et al., 2021). This sectoral accounting is the emissions-side complement to the atmospheric-side carbon budget (Friedlingstein et al., 2022; Friedlingstein et al., 2023): where the carbon budget establishes that the atmospheric increase matches the sum of anthropogenic sources, the sectoral analysis identifies which human activities constitute those sources. Agriculture contributes to this total through emissions whose character differs from the predominantly fossil carbon-dioxide-emitting sectors, involving methane and nitrous oxide with distinct atmospheric lifetimes and warming effects (Lynch et al., 2021).

### 2.13.2 Reactive constituents and their monitoring

The composition change assessed in this chapter extends beyond the long-lived greenhouse gases to reactive constituents that influence air quality, atmospheric chemistry and, in some cases, radiative forcing. Satellite monitoring of tropospheric nitrogen dioxide and methane, validated against ground networks, provides the spatially explicit record needed to track these constituents and to associate their trends with the emitting sectors identified in the emissions inventories (Verhoelst et al., 2021) (Lorente et al., 2021). Mineral dust, mobilised from arid source regions whose relative contributions have been quantified, constitutes a further reactive component with radiative, biogeochemical and cryospheric effects that respond to and feed back upon large-scale change (Kok et al., 2021). Together these constituents complete the picture of a changing atmosphere whose composition is being altered across a spectrum of species and lifetimes by human activity.

## 2.14 Key Uncertainties and Confidence

The assessment presented in this chapter combines statements held with virtual certainty and statements more carefully hedged, and it is important to be explicit about where the principal uncertainties lie. The origin of the greenhouse gas increase and the reality of global warming are effectively certain, resting on the closure of the carbon budget (Friedlingstein et al., 2023) and on the convergence of independent indicators. The attribution of global-mean warming to human influence is likewise held with the highest confidence.

Greater uncertainty attaches to the magnitude, though rarely the sign, of regional and process-level changes. The observed Arctic amplification exceeds the modelled value, indicating a gap in the representation of polar feedbacks whose resolution remains incomplete (Rantanen et al., 2022). The precise thresholds of the climate tipping elements, and the strength of their interactions, are uncertain even though the qualitative risk of cascading change is established (Wunderling et al., 2021). The future trajectory of the terrestrial carbon sink is contingent on the competition between enhancement and loss processes whose balance is not yet resolved (Forzieri et al., 2022) (Yu et al., 2022) (Hammond et al., 2022). Event attribution is more confident for thermodynamically driven extremes such as heat than for dynamically driven ones, reflecting the differing fidelity of models across processes (Clarke et al., 2022) (Barriopedro et al., 2023). And ecological responses, while pervasive, are context-dependent in ways that limit their generalisation (Catford et al., 2021).

These uncertainties do not undermine the central conclusions of the chapter; they refine them. The direction of change across the atmosphere, ocean, cryosphere and biosphere is established with high-to-virtual confidence, as is its human origin. What remains uncertain is principally the rate and magnitude of particular regional and process-level responses, and the placement of the thresholds beyond which change may become self-sustaining. It is on the reduction of precisely these uncertainties — through improved observation (Jean-Michel et al., 2021) (Verhoelst et al., 2021), higher-resolution and hybrid modelling (Ban et al., 2021) (Shen et al., 2023), and the continued maturation of attribution (Clarke et al., 2022) — that the value of future assessment most depends.

## 2.15 Cross-Component Integration and the Human Dimension

### 2.15.1 The coherence of change across components

**The strongest basis for the assessment's central conclusions is not any single record but the physical coherence of change across the components of the system, each of which responds to the same forcing in the manner its physics dictates (*virtually certain* for the overall picture).** The atmosphere warms and holds more moisture, intensifying the water cycle (Bevacqua et al., 2022); the ocean absorbs the majority of the excess heat, warming at depth and driving marine heatwaves and thermal expansion (Jean-Michel et al., 2021) (Smith et al., 2022); the cryosphere retreats where warming crosses the melting point, amplified by the albedo feedback in polar and alpine regions (Rantanen et al., 2022) (Bhattacharya et al., 2021); the continental water cycle grows more variable, producing more severe droughts and a decline in stored water (Williams et al., 2022) (Yao et al., 2023); and the biosphere responds and, under compound stress, loses resilience (Forzieri et al., 2022). No one of these changes, taken alone, would compel the conclusion that the system is being forced by rising greenhouse gases. Taken together, and taken in the specific pattern predicted by the physics of greenhouse forcing — surface and tropospheric warming with stratospheric cooling, polar amplification, ocean heat uptake, an intensifying water cycle — they constitute an internally consistent and mutually reinforcing body of evidence that admits no natural explanation.

This coherence is also the reason the assessment can proceed with confidence despite residual uncertainties in individual components. Where one indicator is ambiguous or poorly observed, others constrain the conclusion; where a model underperforms on one process, its performance on others and the direct observational record supply the missing confidence. The convergence of independent evidence, emphasised at the outset of this chapter, is thus not a rhetorical device but the operational logic by which calibrated statements are reached.

### 2.15.2 Marine ecosystems and ocean biogeochemistry

**Warming and marine heatwaves are reorganising marine ecosystems and the biogeochemical cycles they sustain, extending the physical signal of ocean change into the living ocean and its carbon cycling (*high confidence*).** The biological impacts of marine heatwaves span the full hierarchy of ocean life, from the physiological stress and mortality of individual organisms to shifts in species distributions, mass mortality events, and disruption of the biogeochemical processes that link the ocean to the atmosphere (Smith et al., 2022). Because the ocean is both a heat sink and a carbon sink, changes in its biology and chemistry feed back on the atmospheric composition assessed in Section 2.2: the efficiency of the ocean carbon sink documented in the global carbon budget depends in part on biological processes that warming and deoxygenation can alter (Friedlingstein et al., 2023). The eddy-resolving reanalyses that reconstruct the physical ocean provide the environmental context for these biological changes, linking the temperature and circulation anomalies to the ecosystems that experience them (Jean-Michel et al., 2021).

The coupling between marine heatwaves and the atmosphere further integrates the ocean into the extremes assessment of Section 2.6. Ocean warm anomalies can reinforce atmospheric ridging and contribute to coastal heat extremes, while atmospheric circulation anomalies help generate and sustain the ocean anomalies. The unprecedented terrestrial heat events of recent years, such as the Pacific Northwest heatwave (White et al., 2023), occurred against a background of anomalously warm adjacent ocean, illustrating the land–ocean coupling that compound-extremes analysis seeks to capture.

### 2.15.3 Arctic processes and the value of intensive observation

**Intensive, process-resolving observation of the Arctic has advanced understanding of why the region is warming so rapidly and why models struggle to reproduce the observed amplification (*high confidence*).** The year-long MOSAiC drift observations characterised the central Arctic atmosphere across a complete seasonal cycle, documenting the boundary-layer structure, cloud properties and aerosol processes that govern the exchange of energy between the sea-ice surface and the atmosphere (Shupe et al., 2022). These processes are precisely those whose misrepresentation is implicated in the models' underestimate of Arctic amplification (Rantanen et al., 2022): the treatment of mixed-phase clouds, stable boundary layers and surface-flux coupling over sea ice is difficult, and the MOSAiC observations provide the detailed constraints needed to evaluate and improve it. The mineral dust and other aerosols transported into the Arctic, whose source regions have been quantified, further modulate the surface energy balance by altering snow and ice albedo (Kok et al., 2021), adding another process that intensive observation helps to constrain. The assessment treats such campaigns as high-value investments precisely because they target the processes responsible for the largest model–observation discrepancies.

### 2.15.4 The human dimension of large-scale change

**The large-scale physical changes assessed in this chapter are already producing measurable consequences for human systems, and the attribution of those consequences to human-induced climate change is increasingly demonstrable (*high confidence* for heat-related mortality).** The clearest example is heat: a multi-country analysis attributes a substantial fraction of recent heat-related mortality specifically to human-induced climate change, completing the causal chain from emissions through warming to extreme heat to human health outcomes. Systematic monitoring of the health consequences of climate change documents the widening exposure of populations to the hazards that the physical changes generate, from heat to shifting patterns of disease and food and water insecurity (Romanello et al., 2022). These human consequences are not the subject of the present chapter, whose remit is the physical system and its causes, but they establish the stakes of the physical findings and motivate the granular attribution that connects large-scale change to specific, experienced harms.

Agriculture and food security constitute a second channel through which physical change reaches human systems. The earlier-than-expected emergence of climate impacts on crops (Jägermeyr et al., 2021), the climate-driven shifts in plant pathogens (Singh et al., 2023), and the projected expansion of soil salinization (Hassani et al., 2021) together indicate that the intensifying water cycle and warming assessed here are already reshaping the conditions for food production. The assessment notes these connections not to duplicate the work of other chapters but to underscore that the large-scale changes it documents are consequential precisely because human systems are calibrated to a climate that no longer exists.

### 2.15.5 Regional expression of global change

**Global change is experienced regionally, and its regional expression is systematically structured by geography, so that certain regions serve as bellwethers of the wider transformation (*high confidence*).** The Arctic, warming at nearly four times the global rate, is the clearest such bellwether (Rantanen et al., 2022), followed by the world's mountain regions, where elevation-dependent warming concentrates change at high altitudes and drives rapid ice loss (Pepin et al., 2022) (Bhattacharya et al., 2021). Dry subtropical and semi-arid regions form a third category: the Eastern Mediterranean and Middle East are warming faster than the global mean and are projected to experience a disproportionate rise in extremes (Zittis et al., 2022), while southwestern North America has entered a megadrought without precedent in centuries (Williams et al., 2022). Temperate regions long considered water-secure are not exempt, as Europe's benchmark multi-year drought demonstrated (Rakovec et al., 2022). This regional structure is not random; it follows from the physics of amplification, from the geography of the water cycle, and from the sensitivity of particular cryospheric and ecological systems, and it allows the assessment to identify where the leading edge of change is most likely to be found.

### 2.15.6 Concluding assessment

The findings of this chapter establish, with the highest available confidence, that the climate system has changed at large scale across all its major components and that human activity is the dominant cause. The rise in greenhouse gas concentrations is anthropogenic beyond dispute, confirmed by the closure of the global carbon budget (Friedlingstein et al., 2022; Friedlingstein et al., 2023; Friedlingstein et al., 2025). The resulting warming has propagated through the atmosphere, ocean, cryosphere and biosphere in a coherent, physically predicted pattern (Rantanen et al., 2022) (Jean-Michel et al., 2021) (Bhattacharya et al., 2021) (Forzieri et al., 2022). These changes are attributable to human influence at the global scale with virtual certainty and, increasingly, at the scale of regions and individual events (Clarke et al., 2022) (Barriopedro et al., 2023) (Williams et al., 2022). They are anomalous against the deep past and carry substantial committed further change (Hansen et al., 2023). And they form part of a broader transgression of planetary boundaries whose interacting tipping elements raise the risk of cascading, partly irreversible transitions (Richardson et al., 2023) (Wunderling et al., 2021). The chapters that follow build on this foundation to assess the future evolution of the system and the scope that remains to limit further change.

## 2.16 Sea Level, Coasts and the Aerosol–Cryosphere Coupling

### 2.16.1 Sea-level rise as an integrator of system change

**Sea-level rise integrates the warming of the ocean and the loss of land ice into a single, slowly evolving signal whose consequences for coasts are among the most durable of all large-scale changes (*high confidence*).** The two dominant contributions — thermal expansion of the warming ocean and the addition of meltwater from glaciers and ice sheets — are each documented independently in this chapter, through the subsurface ocean warming captured by reanalysis (Jean-Michel et al., 2021) and through the sustained mass loss of mountain glaciers (Bhattacharya et al., 2021). Their combination raises mean sea level globally, while regional patterns of ocean density, circulation and land motion determine the local rate actually experienced. Because both contributions respond slowly to forcing, sea-level rise carries substantial committed change: even under stabilised temperatures the ocean would continue to expand and ice to adjust for centuries, a manifestation of the pipeline of committed change identified in the paleoclimatic assessment (Hansen et al., 2023).

The management response to sea-level rise must translate these global and regional signals into local adaptation under deep uncertainty. Coastal adaptation encompasses protection, accommodation and, in the limit, retreat, and its planning requires projections that resolve the regional pattern of sea-level change and account for the vulnerability of particular coastlines (Griggs et al., 2021). The assessment emphasises that the physical certainty of ongoing rise coexists with genuine uncertainty in its rate, particularly the contribution of the large ice sheets, so that adaptation must be robust across a range of plausible trajectories rather than optimised to a single projection.

### 2.16.2 The aerosol–cryosphere coupling

**Light-absorbing aerosols deposited on snow and ice constitute a coupling between the atmospheric aerosol cycle and the cryosphere that accelerates melt and links distant source regions to polar and alpine change (*medium confidence*).** Mineral dust, mobilised predominantly from a small number of major arid source regions whose relative contributions have been quantified, is transported over long distances and deposited on snow and ice, where it lowers surface albedo and increases the absorption of solar radiation (Kok et al., 2021). This darkening accelerates melt beyond what temperature alone would produce, adding to the albedo feedback that already amplifies warming in the cryosphere (Rantanen et al., 2022) (Pepin et al., 2022). The strength of this coupling depends on the dust cycle, which is itself changing as surface winds, soil moisture and vegetation cover respond to climate and land use, making dust both a driver of and a response to large-scale change. The assessment attaches medium confidence to the magnitude of the aerosol–cryosphere effect while recognising the process as an established contributor to cryospheric change.

### 2.16.3 Observing-system gaps and priorities

The confidence of the assessment is bounded by the completeness of the observing system, and several gaps warrant emphasis. The deep ocean below the routine reach of the profiling-float array is undersampled, so that estimates of full-depth heat content and their trends rely heavily on reanalysis and carry corresponding uncertainty (Jean-Michel et al., 2021). The polar regions, despite intensive campaigns such as MOSAiC (Shupe et al., 2022), remain sparsely observed in space and time relative to the pace of change there (Rantanen et al., 2022). Terrestrial water storage below the surface — soil moisture and groundwater — is inferred rather than directly and comprehensively measured, limiting the characterisation of drought and of the continental water balance (Yao et al., 2023) (Vereecken et al., 2022). And the biosphere's slow and heterogeneous responses are difficult to monitor at the scale and duration required to detect resilience loss before it manifests as dieback (Forzieri et al., 2022). Addressing these gaps — through sustained satellite missions, expanded in-situ networks, intensive process campaigns and the incorporation of citizen-science observation (Fraisl et al., 2022) — is the observational counterpart to the modelling and attribution advances on which future assessments depend.

---

This assessment of large-scale change and its causes provides the observational and mechanistic foundation for the projections, scenario analysis and risk assessment developed in the chapters that follow. The coherence of the evidence across the atmosphere, ocean, cryosphere and biosphere, and the maturation of detection and attribution from the global mean to the individual event, together establish both what has changed and why with a confidence that has strengthened in every assessment cycle.
