# Summary for Policymakers

## Introduction

This Summary for Policymakers presents the key findings of the Working Group I contribution to the AR7 GROUNDED assessment, *Climate Change 202X: The Physical Science Basis*. It synthesizes the assessed statements of the ten underlying chapters, which together establish the framing and methods of the assessment {1}, the observed state of the climate system and its causes {2, 3}, the physical understanding of the forcings, feedbacks, and cycles that govern that state {4}, the projected evolution of the system across near-term and long-term horizons and at global and regional scales {5, 6, 7}, the risk of abrupt and irreversible change {8}, the physical requirements for stabilizing the climate {9}, and the translation of physical-science knowledge into decision-relevant climate information {10}.

The assessment adopts the Variant Earths framing, under which the observed climate is treated as one physically admissible realization drawn from a structured space of Earth states that differ in their forcing histories, in the phasing of internal variability, and in their boundary conditions {1.1}. The purpose of this framing is to make the observed, counterfactual, and projected members of that space commensurable, so that attribution, projection, and scenario analysis share a single calibrated vocabulary and a common uncertainty calculus (*high confidence*).

Calibrated uncertainty language is used throughout. Likelihood terms — such as *virtually certain*, *very likely*, and *likely* — express a quantified probabilistic assessment of an outcome; confidence terms — from *low* through *medium* to *high* and *very high confidence* — express the assessed robustness of the evidence and the degree of agreement among lines of evidence {1.4}. All findings in this Summary are traceable to the underlying chapters, and the curly-brace references locate the supporting assessment.

## A. The Observed State of the Climate System

### A.1 The Earth system has moved outside the envelope of Holocene variability across multiple coupled domains, and this displacement is the physical premise the assessment must explain rather than a conclusion it seeks to prove (*virtually certain*).

Assessment of the aggregate state of the Earth system indicates that several coupled domains — climate, biosphere integrity, land systems, freshwater, and biogeochemical flows among them — have moved into regimes for which Holocene analogues are absent or weak, with a majority of the assessed planetary boundaries now transgressed {1.1, 2.6}. The land surface, the freshwater system, and inland waters show coherent, simultaneous displacement rather than isolated anomalies, which is why the assessment adopts a coupled Earth-system framing rather than treating the atmosphere in isolation (*virtually certain*) {1.1}.

The living biosphere is assessed as an active participant in the system's trajectory, mediating carbon, water, and energy fluxes in ways that feed back on the physical climate rather than merely responding to it (*high confidence*) {1.1}. A variant Earth is therefore specified not only by its physical forcing but by the state of its biosphere, and biological and ecological evidence is treated as part of the assessment's core evidentiary base rather than as impact reporting downstream of the physical conclusions {1.1}.

### A.2 Warming is unequivocal, human influence is now detectable across every major component of the climate system, and confidence in this conclusion derives from the convergence of independent lines of evidence rather than from any single indicator (*virtually certain*).

The climate system as a whole has continued to warm, and the imprint of human influence is detectable across the atmosphere, ocean, cryosphere, and biosphere simultaneously (*virtually certain*) {2.1, 2.2}. Warming of the lower atmosphere, warming and expansion of the ocean, retreat of glaciers and sea ice, poleward and upslope shifts of climatic zones, and intensification of the water cycle are not separate findings to be weighed against one another; they are complementary expressions of a single, forced perturbation whose pattern is physically consistent with the accumulation of energy in the Earth system (*high confidence*) {2.1, 2.2, 2.3}.

Atmospheric concentrations of carbon dioxide, methane, and nitrous oxide have continued to rise, and the growth of the airborne carbon dioxide burden tracks the sum of fossil and land-use emissions closely enough that the anthropogenic origin of the increase is beyond scientific dispute (*virtually certain*) {2.2, 4.1}. The closure of the global carbon budget — the agreement between the top-down atmospheric growth rate and the bottom-up sum of sources and sinks — is among the strongest available demonstrations that the observed composition change is anthropogenic in origin, because it requires the human source term to balance the account {2.2}.

### A.3 Observed warming is strongly amplified at high latitudes and high elevations, with the Arctic warming nearly four times faster than the global mean since 1979 (*high confidence*).

Surface warming has been geographically pervasive but strongly structured in magnitude, concentrating the largest changes in the regions whose cryospheric and hydrological systems are most sensitive {2.2, 3.1}. Arctic near-surface warming has proceeded at close to four times the global rate since 1979, a factor that substantially exceeds the two-to-threefold amplification cited in earlier assessments and reproduced by many models, indicating that the coupled model ensemble may systematically underestimate the polar response to forcing (*high confidence*) {2.2, 3.1}. Mountain regions warm in an elevation-dependent manner, so that high-altitude environments — and the water towers they sustain for downstream populations — are frequently changing faster than adjacent lowlands (*high confidence*) {2.2, 3.1}.

Superimposed on this regional structure, the urban heat island transforms background warming into a locally intense public-health hazard, and exposure to that excess heat is distributed inequitably across and within cities (*high confidence*) {3.1}. The same degree of background warming therefore produces very different human outcomes depending on where, and to whom, the heat is delivered {3.1}.

### A.4 Hot extremes, heavy short-duration rainfall, and drought are all intensifying across most inhabited regions, and their human consequences are now measurable at the scale of individual cities and populations (*high confidence*).

The intensity, frequency, and duration of hot extremes have increased across most land regions, and the resulting burden on population health and on health systems is documented in successive annual assessments (*high confidence*) {3.1, 3.2}. Short-duration rainfall extremes are intensifying at rates consistent with, and in places exceeding, thermodynamic expectations, raising flood hazard that falls disproportionately on populations in poverty, so that identical physical hazards translate into sharply divergent human outcomes (*high confidence*) {3.2}. Drought is evolving in character as well as frequency, with a documented global shift toward rapidly developing "flash" droughts and toward multi-year megadroughts in several regions, and with groundwater — the buffer on which much of humanity relies during drought — under simultaneous pressure from depletion and from a changing climate (*high confidence*) {2.4, 3.2}.

The ocean has warmed, and marine heatwaves have become more frequent, more intense, and longer-lasting, with biological consequences that now extend from individual organisms to whole ecosystems and biogeochemical cycles (*high confidence*) {2.3, 6.4}. The continental water cycle is intensifying and becoming more variable, with a widespread decline in terrestrial water storage and a detectable anthropogenic fingerprint in global drought frequency, duration, and intensity (*high confidence*) {2.3, 2.4}.

### A.5 Human influence on individual extreme events can increasingly be quantified through formal attribution, moving detection and attribution from the global mean to the scale of specific heatwaves, droughts, and fire seasons (*high confidence*).

Detection and attribution once operated almost exclusively at the level of global-mean temperature; it now routinely addresses regional trends, individual seasons, and specific extreme events, so that calibrated statements can be made about the human contribution to particular events that populate the public record of climate impacts (*high confidence*) {2.4, 3.2}. Compound and cascading events — where hazards coincide, cluster, or trigger one another — are an increasingly important frame for regional risk, and single-hazard assessment methods systematically understate them; a framework treating climate risk as complex, interacting, and cascading is required to capture these dynamics (*medium confidence*) {3.2, 3.3}.

The biosphere is responding to, and in places losing resilience under, the combined pressures of warming, drought, and disturbance, with emerging signals of declining forest resilience and a distinctive fingerprint of hotter droughts on tree mortality worldwide (*medium-to-high confidence*) {2.5}. Adaptation to regional climate change is occurring but is uneven, and in some documented cases has redistributed or deepened vulnerability rather than reducing it, underscoring that regional risk is produced jointly by physical hazard, exposure, and the social processes that govern vulnerability (*high confidence*) {3.4}.

## B. Drivers, Feedbacks, and Human Influence

### B.1 The dominant control on the present energy imbalance is the accumulation of carbon dioxide and other long-lived greenhouse gases, whose forcing is established with the highest confidence of any forcing agent (*virtually certain*).

The physical understanding of how energy imbalance is imposed, amplified or damped by feedbacks, and transmitted through the coupled carbon, biogeochemical, and hydrological cycles has consolidated since the previous assessment cycle, and the direction of every major process is now attributed to human activity with progressively narrower uncertainty ranges (*high confidence*) {4.1}. The forcing exerted by the well-mixed greenhouse gases is the dominant term in the total anthropogenic forcing; both the atmospheric concentrations and the underlying spectroscopy are directly measured, and the carbon dioxide forcing grows approximately with the logarithm of concentration in a manner that is not in scientific dispute (*virtually certain*) {4.1}.

Effective radiative forcing, which folds rapid atmospheric and land-surface adjustments into the instantaneous perturbation, is the most policy-relevant forcing metric {4.1}. Methane occupies a distinctive position because its short atmospheric lifetime gives near-term methane mitigation a leverage over near-term forcing that carbon dioxide mitigation cannot provide (*high confidence*) {4.1}.

### B.2 The aerosol contribution remains the single largest source of uncertainty in the total anthropogenic forcing, and this asymmetry propagates directly into the inference of climate sensitivity from the historical record (*high confidence*).

The total anthropogenic effective radiative forcing is positive and its central estimate is well separated from zero, but its uncertainty is dominated by the negative and poorly constrained aerosol term (*high confidence*) {4.1}. Because the observed warming is the response to the net forcing, a more negative aerosol forcing implies a larger sensitivity for a given observed warming; progress on the aerosol forcing and progress on climate sensitivity are therefore two projections of the same underlying uncertainty {4.1, 4.2}. As controls on sulfur emissions tighten for air-quality reasons, the aerosol cooling term declines faster than the co-emitted warming term, unmasking a fraction of the greenhouse warming that had previously been offset (*high confidence*) {4.1}.

### B.3 Net climate feedbacks are positive, so the equilibrium warming for a doubling of carbon dioxide is substantially larger than the no-feedback response, with the cloud feedback the principal amplifier and the principal residual uncertainty (*high confidence*).

Equilibrium climate sensitivity — the eventual global-mean warming that follows a sustained doubling of atmospheric carbon dioxide — is the number that most compactly summarizes the amplifying and damping processes of the system, and its assessed range has narrowed as the feedbacks that determine it have been separately constrained (*high confidence*) {4.2}. The no-feedback response of close to one degree Celsius is not in dispute; everything above it is the net product of feedbacks, and everything uncertain about the sensitivity is uncertainty in feedbacks {4.2}. Surface-albedo feedbacks are expressed most strongly in the Arctic, where they contribute to the near-fourfold amplification of near-surface warming over the satellite era (*high confidence*) {4.2}.

### B.4 The land and ocean carbon sinks together remove roughly half of anthropogenic emissions, but the efficiency of both is sensitive to warming, drought, and disturbance, introducing a positive carbon-cycle feedback of increasing magnitude (*medium-to-high confidence*).

The persistence of the global forest carbon sink, and emerging signals that its resilience is declining under compound stress, are now documented at global scale (*medium-to-high confidence*) {4.3, 2.5}. Terrestrial biogeochemical cycling is governed at the process level by the interaction of organic matter with soil minerals and by the ecology of the soil microbiome, both of which set the residence time of carbon; disturbance to these interfaces reduces the functional capacity of soils to store carbon (*high confidence*) {4.3}. Because the sinks have historically absorbed roughly half of annual emissions, any weakening under continued warming would raise the airborne fraction and hence the forcing per unit of emissions, a feedback the assessment treats as more likely to reduce than to increase the room remaining for stabilization {4.3, 9.2}.

### B.5 The water cycle is intensifying, with rising atmospheric moisture demand, more rapid drought onset, and warming of inland waters, and each of these process shifts carries an anthropogenic fingerprint (*high confidence*).

A warmer atmosphere holds more water vapour, strengthening the transport and convergence of moisture and sharpening the contrast between wet and dry regimes (*high confidence*) {2.2, 4.4}. The abrupt onset of flash droughts under continued warming, and the propagation of heat extremes into lakes and rivers, are robust across observational and modelling lines of evidence {4.4}. Earth-system model intercomparison provides the quantitative bridge between this process understanding and projection, and the current generation of models reproduces the observed forcing history while spanning a credible range of feedback strengths (*high confidence*) {4.5, 6.1}.

## C. Possible Climate Futures

### C.1 The community scenario framework remains fit for purpose, but the plausibility of its highest-emission storylines has continued to erode as observed and pledged mitigation diverge from unmitigated-growth assumptions (*high confidence*).

The Shared Socioeconomic Pathway architecture, coupled to the coordinated multi-model experimental design, continues to anchor the projections assessed in this volume {5.1, 6.1}. The highest-forcing pathways, constructed as coal-intensive high-reference cases, have become progressively less consistent with observed technology deployment and announced policy; the assessment therefore gives greater weight to intermediate and lower pathways for the most likely outcomes while retaining the full range so that low-probability, high-impact outcomes remain visible (*high confidence*) {5.1, 6.1}. Global fossil carbon dioxide emissions have remained near record levels through the early 2020s, so the near-term trajectory currently tracks the intermediate rather than the low pathways, and the remaining carbon budget consistent with the most stringent targets continues to shrink (*high confidence*) {5.2, 9.2}.

### C.2 Near-term global warming is largely committed by past and present emissions, so pathways diverge little before about 2040 and the mitigation signal has not yet emerged from internal variability (*very likely*).

Near-term global surface temperature is projected to continue rising across all plausible emissions pathways, and the difference between pathways is small before about 2040 because near-term warming is largely already committed (*very likely*) {5.1, 5.2}. Internal variability is the largest source of uncertainty in near-term regional projections and can locally mask or amplify the forced trend for a decade or more; in rapidly warming regions such as the Arctic the forced signal already dominates, whereas in mid-latitude land regions the near-term trend can be obscured by variability (*high confidence*) {5.3}. A distinctive near-term feature of the lowest pathways is that rapid reduction of co-emitted aerosols can produce a transient relative warming before reduced greenhouse-gas forcing dominates; this reflects the different atmospheric lifetimes involved and is not evidence that mitigation is ineffective (*high confidence*) {5.1, 5.2}.

The probability of individual years exceeding 1.5 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels has become substantial, although a single threshold-exceeding year does not constitute crossing the long-term warming level (*high confidence*) {5.4}. Near-term extremes, particularly heatwaves and compound drought-heat events, are projected to intensify faster than the mean, so risk-relevant change outpaces the change in global mean temperature (*high confidence*) {5.4}.

### C.3 The level of long-term warming reached is determined overwhelmingly by cumulative future carbon dioxide emissions rather than by the pathway taken to any given level, and every increment of warming matters (*virtually certain*).

Global surface temperature will continue to rise until at least mid-century under all assessed scenarios, with century-end warming spanning roughly 1.4 degrees Celsius to more than 4 degrees Celsius above the late-nineteenth-century baseline; the spread is dominated by scenario choice at the high end and by climate-response uncertainty at the low end (*high confidence*) {6.1}. Because peak warming is controlled to first order by the total carbon released, warming-level framing — organizing projected change by global mean temperature increase rather than by scenario or date — is the most robust way to communicate long-term change, and it exposes the strong nonlinearities by which several hazards scale super-linearly with warming (*high confidence*) {6.1, 9.1}. Glacier loss, heat-extreme frequency, drought expansion, and the probability of crossing critical thresholds all increase monotonically, and for several systems super-linearly, with each additional tenth of a degree (*high confidence*) {6.1, 6.2}.

The polar and high-mountain regions will continue to warm substantially faster than the global mean, sustaining Arctic amplification at roughly three to four times the global rate and elevation-dependent warming across the world's mountains (*high confidence*) {6.1, 7.1}. Global precipitation will increase in the ensemble mean but is spatially organized into a robust wet-get-wetter, dry-get-drier pattern superimposed on large regional uncertainty, with the frequency and intensity of both heavy-precipitation and drought extremes rising together (*high confidence*) {6.2}.

### C.4 The ocean and sea level carry deep-time commitments that are effectively irreversible on human timescales even if temperatures are stabilized (*high confidence*).

The ocean will continue to warm, acidify, lose oxygen, and experience more frequent, intense, and prolonged marine heatwaves throughout the century, with biological consequences that scale steeply with warming level (*virtually certain*) {6.4}. Global mean sea level will rise through 2100 and for centuries to millennia beyond; the century-scale rise is dominated by well-constrained ocean thermal expansion and glacier melt, while the deep uncertainty and the high-end tail reside in the marine-based sectors of the Antarctic Ice Sheet (*high confidence*) {6.4, 6.5}. Large-scale overturning circulation is assessed to weaken over the century, though a complete collapse before 2100 is not the assessed central outcome {6.4, 8.2}.

### C.5 Regional climate change is now detectable on every inhabited continent, and translating the well-constrained global signal into credible regional information is the central methodological challenge, met through a maturing suite of downscaling and distillation methods (*high confidence*).

The dominant large-scale features of the warming pattern — land amplification, high-latitude amplification, and elevation-dependent mountain warming — are consistently simulated across model generations and corroborated by observations (*high confidence*) {7.1}. The global signal is comparatively well constrained, but the regional signal, especially regional precipitation, carries large model spread arising from differences in circulation, land-surface feedbacks, and unresolved convection; regionalization is therefore not merely spatial refinement but the addition of physically credible information that global models cannot provide {7.1, 7.2}. Convection-permitting dynamical downscaling provides the highest-fidelity representation of regional extremes, statistical methods and machine-learning emulators deliver the large ensembles that expensive dynamical runs cannot, and no single model or method is authoritative for a given region; credible regional information emerges from the expert-mediated distillation of dynamical, statistical, observational, and process-understanding evidence (*high confidence*) {7.2, 7.4}.

Regional heat extremes are intensifying faster than mean warming in several mid-latitude regions, driven partly by circulation changes imperfectly captured by coarse-resolution models, and dynamical downscaling is therefore assessed as necessary but not sufficient — it adds fine-scale value only conditional on a credible large-scale driver (*high confidence*) {7.2}. Storylines and physically self-consistent narrative approaches have become a standard complement to probabilistic projection for communicating deeply uncertain, high-impact regional outcomes (*high confidence*) {7.6}.

## D. Abrupt Change, Tipping Points, and Irreversibility

### D.1 The climate system contains large-scale components that can undergo self-sustaining and in some cases effectively irreversible transitions once forcing crosses a critical threshold, and several show observational signals consistent with declining stability (*high confidence*).

The defining features of a tipping element are threshold behaviour, the dominance of self-amplifying internal feedbacks once the threshold is crossed, and hysteresis — the property that reversing the forcing does not straightforwardly reverse the transition (*high confidence*) {8.1}. This behaviour violates the tacit assumption of proportionality that underlies much of impact assessment: in the neighbourhood of a threshold, a small additional forcing can produce a disproportionate and possibly unrecoverable change, which is why abrupt and irreversible change is assessed as a distinct category of climate risk rather than folded into the assessment of gradual change {8.1}. Individual tipping elements are embedded within an interconnected biosphere whose overall resilience is itself being eroded under multiple simultaneous pressures, which makes threshold behaviour in any one component more likely, all else equal (*high confidence*) {8.1}.

### D.2 Warm-water coral reefs are the tipping element most imminently at risk, while the Amazon forest shows a pronounced loss of resilience since the early 2000s (*high confidence* for reefs; *medium confidence* for the Amazon).

Warm-water coral reef systems are assessed to be the most imminently threatened tipping element, with the biological impacts of marine heatwaves already driving mass mortality at present levels of warming (*high confidence*) {8.2}. The Amazon forest shows declining recovery rates from perturbation across a rising fraction of the biome, and a combination of warming, drought, deforestation, and fire could drive large areas past a threshold into a degraded, lower-biomass state (*medium confidence*) {8.2}. The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation is assessed to possess threshold behaviour arising from a positive salinity-advection feedback; there is *high confidence* that it will weaken over the coming century and *low confidence* in the level of forcing that would trigger an abrupt collapse, for an overall assessment of *medium confidence* {8.2}.

### D.3 Permafrost constitutes a large and vulnerable carbon reservoir whose thaw produces a positive feedback that is expected to be gradual and pervasive rather than a single global-scale abrupt transition, though abrupt thaw can dominate locally (*high confidence*).

The magnitude of the permafrost carbon feedback remains a significant source of uncertainty in the assessment of committed warming, and permafrost carbon release could continue to add carbon dioxide and methane to the atmosphere after anthropogenic emissions cease (*high confidence*) {8.2, 9.3}. Terrestrial ecosystems more broadly show emerging, spatially coherent signals of declining resilience, with hotter droughts producing tree mortality across a wide range of forest biomes, with implications for the trajectory of atmospheric carbon dioxide and for the stability of forest-related tipping elements (*high confidence*) {8.2, 4.3}.

### D.4 Tipping elements do not operate in isolation: interactions among them, and between climatic and non-climatic stresses, can produce cascades and systemic risk, and low-likelihood high-impact outcomes must be weighted despite their deep uncertainty (*medium confidence* on cascades; *high confidence* on the value of assessing tail risk).

Interacting tipping elements, and the interaction of climatic hazards with pollution, land-use change, biodiversity loss, and socio-economic fragility, can lower the threshold at which a component tips and raise the severity of the resulting impact, coupling the climate crisis to biodiversity loss and to broader societal shocks (*medium confidence*) {8.1, 8.2}. Early-warning indicators grounded in the theory of critical slowing down provide a physically motivated but not infallible basis for detecting approach to a threshold, and their interpretation is complicated by observational limits, confounding trends, and the possibility of tipping without prior warning (*medium confidence*) {8.2}. Because the consequences of crossing certain thresholds would be severe and in some cases effectively permanent on human timescales, a finding that a catastrophic outcome cannot be assigned a precise probability is emphatically not a finding that its probability is negligible; a risk assessment that omits such outcomes because they are hard to quantify systematically understates the total risk (*high confidence* in the value of assessing such outcomes) {8.1}.

## E. Limiting and Stabilizing Climate Change

### E.1 Stabilizing global surface temperature at any level requires global net anthropogenic carbon dioxide emissions to reach and be sustained at zero, and every additional increment of cumulative emissions moves the achievable stabilization level upward in near-linear proportion (*virtually certain*).

The near-linear relationship between cumulative carbon dioxide emissions and peak warming remains the central organizing principle of stabilization science, and it is on this relationship that the concept of a remaining carbon budget for a given temperature limit rests (*virtually certain*) {9.1, 9.3}. Stabilizing temperature is equivalent to bringing net carbon dioxide emissions to zero, and the level at which temperature stabilizes is set by the cumulative total emitted up to that point {9.1}. The remaining carbon budget consistent with limiting warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius has continued to contract as emissions have persisted near record levels, and on present trajectories the budget for a better-than-even chance of holding warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius will be exhausted within the current decade (*high confidence*) {9.2}.

### E.2 When global net carbon dioxide emissions reach zero, the multi-decadal warming trend is expected to level off within about a decade rather than continue rising, because declining atmospheric carbon dioxide approximately offsets continued ocean heat uptake (*medium confidence*).

This near-zero zero-emissions commitment for carbon dioxide means that halting warming is achievable within a human generation, but that already-emitted carbon dioxide commits the Earth system to sustained elevated temperatures for centuries (*medium confidence*) {9.3}. The commitment is assessed with *medium* rather than *high confidence*, and is regarded as more likely to be revised upward than downward, because Earth-system feedbacks not fully represented in the models used to derive it — permafrost carbon release and committed changes in the biosphere among them — could slow the post-emissions decline in atmospheric carbon dioxide (*medium confidence*) {9.3}. Reaching net zero halts warming but does not by itself cool the planet; reducing global temperature below its peak requires sustained net-negative emissions, a physically distinct undertaking whose temperature decline is not simply the mirror image of the rise because the slow components of the system respond with hysteresis {9.3, 9.4}.

### E.3 Overshoot pathways can stabilize temperature but carry physical risks that are not symmetric on the way up and the way down, and several Earth-system responses are only partially reversible on policy-relevant timescales (*high confidence*).

Committed changes in the deep ocean, ice sheets, permafrost, and biosphere carbon stores mean that returning global mean temperature to a target level does not return the full Earth system to its prior state (*high confidence*) {9.4}. Earth-system responses to stabilization are heterogeneous in time: global mean surface temperature responds within decades, but sea level, ice sheets, deep-ocean heat content, and some ecosystem carbon stores continue to evolve for centuries to millennia after temperatures stabilize, so assessing stabilization requires distinguishing the fast-responding components from the slow-responding components whose trajectories are effectively locked in over human planning horizons (*high confidence*) {9.1, 9.4}.

### E.4 Carbon dioxide removal is a necessary element of pathways that reach net-zero and net-negative emissions, and solar radiation modification could rapidly lower temperature but does not address the underlying drivers and introduces distinct risks (*high confidence*).

Carbon dioxide removal approaches — nature-based and engineered, including direct air capture and geologic storage — differ sharply in monitorability, permanence, and reversibility, and these physical characteristics govern their net climatic effect (*high confidence*) {9.5}. Solar radiation modification could in principle rapidly reduce global mean surface temperature by increasing planetary albedo, but it does not address ocean acidification, would impose distinct regional climate and hydrological responses, and would create a termination-related risk of rapid warming if sustained deployment ceased; it is not a substitute for emissions reductions or carbon dioxide removal, because it acts on a different lever of the energy balance than the atmospheric carbon dioxide burden that drives long-term change (*high confidence*) {9.6}. This volume assesses only the physical-science characteristics of these interventions; their economic feasibility, governance, and social dimensions fall to other working groups {9.1}.

## F. Climate Information, Uncertainty, and Decisions

### F.1 Climate information has become a distinct, decision-facing infrastructure, and the binding constraint on its value is no longer the availability of data but the fidelity of the processes that translate physical-science knowledge into salient, credible, and legitimate forms (*high confidence*).

The value of climate information is a joint property of the information and the decision context and cannot be evaluated by reference to scientific quality alone; a physically sound projection can be unusable if delivered at the wrong scale, against the wrong baseline, or with uncertainty language the user cannot interpret (*high confidence*) {10.1}. The co-production of climate information — sustained, iterative collaboration between producers and users — measurably improves uptake relative to one-directional "loading dock" delivery, but is resource-intensive, unevenly distributed, and difficult to sustain (*medium confidence*) {10.2}. A decision-first framing privileges information about thresholds, tails, and rates of change over central tendencies, and makes the communication of uncertainty a first-order design problem rather than a caveat (*high confidence*) {10.1}.

### F.2 The observational and reanalysis foundation has strengthened substantially, and machine-learning methods have become established components of the climate-information value chain, delivering real gains alongside well-characterized risks (*high confidence*).

Global reanalyses and satellite-derived land-surface products now provide internally consistent, decision-usable representations of the recent climate at fine spatial resolution, though systematic gaps remain over parts of the Global South and for many essential variables (*high confidence*) {10.3}. Artificial intelligence and machine-learning methods deliver skilful short-range prediction, computationally efficient emulation, pattern extraction from large Earth-observation archives, and new modes of downscaling and bias correction; data-driven emulators now match or exceed operational physics-based systems for medium-range global weather forecasting at a fraction of the cost (*high confidence*) {10.4, 6.1}. These gains are accompanied by brittle out-of-distribution behaviour, opaque uncertainty, and non-trivial environmental and equity costs, and for projection of novel forcing regimes the emulators complement rather than replace process-based general circulation models, which remain the authority on the response to forcing outside the training distribution (*high confidence*) {10.4, 6.1}.

### F.3 Robust, well-calibrated communication of uncertainty is a prerequisite for climate information to support good decisions, and the physical-science findings enter impact and adaptation assessment through a regional-risk interface whose quality depends on shared definitions and traceable uncertainty (*high confidence*).

Where uncertainty is suppressed, misrepresented, or presented without decision-relevant framing, climate information can degrade rather than improve decisions (*medium confidence*) {10.5}. The calibrated-language framework used throughout this volume — separating quantified likelihood from assessed confidence — is one instrument for conveying the state of knowledge honestly to non-specialist users, and it enforces the discipline, established in the assessment's framing, of never reporting a quantity without its baseline, its support, and its uncertainty representation (*high confidence*) {1.4, 10.5}. Regional climate risk assessment is the principal interface through which this Working Group's findings enter the impact, adaptation, and vulnerability assessments of Working Group II, and the quality of that interface depends on shared definitions of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability and on the traceable propagation of uncertainty across the boundary (*high confidence*) {10.6}.

### F.4 Equity, capacity, and the inclusion of Indigenous knowledge and local communities are central determinants of whether climate information is trusted and used, and of whether its benefits are distributed fairly (*medium confidence*).

Outcomes in conservation and land governance are more effective and more equitable where Indigenous peoples and local communities are recognized as knowledge-holders and decision-partners rather than as passive recipients (*medium confidence*) {10.7}. This equity dimension is not peripheral to the physical-science enterprise: because the same degree of physical warming produces very different human outcomes depending on exposure and vulnerability {3.1, 3.4}, and because the burden of unabated change and the reach of decision-relevant information both fall unevenly, the assessment treats the capacity to produce, access, and act on climate information as a determinant of realized risk rather than as a matter external to it (*medium confidence*) {10.7}.
